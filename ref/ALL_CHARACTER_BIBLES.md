# CARL — CHARACTER BIBLE & WRITING GUIDE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

For writing Carl in streams, scripted scenes, comics, and the adventure game. The first edition draws on the 229 transcript bundles. This revision also uses timestamped passages from the expanded Vodbrain archive. It is comprehensive about the evidence assembled here, not a claim that every improvised line has been recovered or reconciled.

**The governing rule:** Carl lets anxiety steer. He says the thought, follows the objection, and does the thing that a more restrained version of him would stop himself from doing. His ability to make a situation worse is inseparable from his ability to make something happen.

## 1. Carl at a glance

Carl is a visibly alien delivery driver from “above and below Nevada” who would appreciate everyone agreeing that he is a normal human. He escaped an underground facility with help from an older version of Face. He became the Prop Pills delivery driver, travels in his Alien Starship—the A.S.S.—and spends a recurring stretch of the story parked outside the Mall of the Future because he cannot stop making trouble inside it.

He smokes weed, hangs out with chat, argues, plays games, gets sidetracked, and resists being told what to do. Under that is an unresolved search for his parents, uncertainty about who can be trusted across the stream’s changing incarnations, and a demonstrated willingness to go into danger for the stream’s people.

He is funny because his defenses are visible. A question about a giant bong becomes an argument about discrimination against short people. Being unmistakably alien becomes a lengthy explanation of why he is obviously human. A rule becomes a personal challenge. The explanation keeps growing until it creates a new problem.

**One-sentence writing brief:** A defensive, impulsive alien trying to have a normal time keeps talking and acting past the point where he should stop, then recruits chat to help deal with the consequences.

**What keeps him sympathetic:** He wants company, freedom, and somewhere to belong; he is capable of warmth and useful action, even when he is exhausting to be around. Those emotional priorities are a writing interpretation of his conduct, not a newly declared backstory.

**What must survive every adaptation:** the anxious thought process, the audience relationship, the gap between his self-description and his behavior, and enough sincerity for his relationships to matter. [S1–S4]

## 2. How to read canon in this bible

The transcripts combine creator commentary, character performance, viewer messages, game dialogue, songs, quoted videos, and transcription errors. A statement appearing in a transcript does not by itself make it a Carl fact.

- **Established:** repeated lore or a clear creator explanation supported by context.
- **Reported event:** a character or creator recalls something happening; details may still vary.
- **Recurring bit:** an established performance pattern whose literal explanation can change.
- **Proposal or variant:** an idea discussed for another format, alternate reality, or future stream.
- **Interpretation:** a useful writing conclusion derived from the above.
- **Unknown:** the sources do not settle it.

Creator explanations of how Carl is performed carry particular weight. Casual audience accusations do not automatically become his biography. A game character named Carl does not automatically donate that game’s backstory to him.

The previously generated adventure game and `Outlive_Stream_Retheme.md` are adaptations. They are not independent evidence of stream canon. Newly written examples in this bible are labeled and should not be mistaken for transcript quotations.

## 4. Origin and continuity

### The real-world creation story

The creator describes Carl as an improvised VTuber experiment. He began as the Prop Pills delivery driver and developed during a Desert Bus stream lasting roughly eight hours, possibly nine in the recollection. The deliberately uneventful game forced him to talk to himself, talk to chat, become increasingly frayed, and self-reflect. The creator explicitly connects that experience to why Carl became the character he is. [S4]

This is a major writing clue: **Carl can generate a scene from waiting, inconvenience, and company.** He does not need a new explosion or lore reveal every thirty seconds.

### The in-universe sequence

This is a relative sequence, not a dated timeline. The bundle numbers are not a reliable chronology, and many episode dates are marked unknown.

1. **Arrival and the missing family.** Carl associates his origin with coming from above Nevada and ending up below it. His parents’ location and his life before the facility remain uncertain.
2. **Life in the facility.** He describes an underground lab, bunker, or shelter. One account says it felt like a dungeon. He fears being found and asks viewers to deny knowing him if men in white suits arrive. [S6–S7]
3. **Face helps him escape.** An older version of Face frees him. Their association leads to his delivery work. [S7, S9]
4. **Prop Pills employment.** He becomes the delivery driver for the stream’s fake sponsor. One account frames the arrangement as a debt to Face, weed in place of meaningful pay, and a promise that he will be paid if the stream makes money. Carl complains, then recasts it as an investment in the future. [S11]
5. **The business becomes unstable.** Prop Pills is described as getting into trouble because its product works better than placebo after claiming only placebo-level effectiveness. One account adds occasional dark-web deliveries. Other passages discuss its absence, return, or Linda’s ownership. Preserve the era rather than forcing one permanent employment status. [S12]
6. **The mall and the parking lot.** Carl parks his ship outside the Mall of the Future. Smoking, breaking bongs, and doing the opposite of what Linda tells him explain why he keeps getting kicked out or barred. [S9–S10]
7. **The search for Spooky Ghost.** Carl infiltrates Linda’s system to obtain information that can help locate Spooky Ghost. Recollections describe a blackjack, prior Thief practice, audience-directed action, and simplified Fate-style dice. Linda had a way to track the ghost that the others lacked. [S13–S14]
8. **Continuity across changing seasons.** A reboot discussion says Carl remains the same Carl and tentatively suggests he may remember the older seasons. In-character, he is uncertain whether a new Face is the person who helped him before. The personal uncertainty is firmer than any claim that Carl possesses perfect continuity knowledge. [S7, S15]

### Prop Pills: get the joke right

The recurring sponsor formulation identifies Prop Pills as a subsidiary of Pretend Co., “the pretend company, a real company.” Transcription mangles the company’s spelling in several places; **Pretend Co. is the working normalization, not a verified official logo spelling.** A later passage links the company to Fascism Inc. through Linda. [S12]

The fraud joke is the reversal: the allegedly pretend pills were *more effective* than the advertised placebo. Do not flatten it into an ordinary “the pills do nothing” scam. Carl can also stumble over the sponsor line, restart it, and get annoyed that he cannot say a phrase he normally knows.

His relationship to the company is more interesting than generic corporate satire: he works for it, depends on it, complains about it, and may not even be sure who owns it anymore.

### The escape and the tracker mission

Face's Nevada retelling gives him a role in freeing Carl and treats the hacking routine as a comic reenactment. One earlier account gives Carl's parents as dead, while the longer account says they probably died and leaves their fate open. Use the crash and captivity as common ground. Carl can act on a lead about his parents without the writer settling what happened to them. ## 5. The character’s internal mechanics

### Anxiety becomes behavior

The creator repeatedly states the rule directly: Carl lets anxiety guide him, holds back less, chooses words less carefully, and gets into drawn-out fights. “Carl is anxiety” is the most compact statement of it. [S1–S3]

This is a performance instruction, not a clinical diagnosis. It does not mean Carl must tremble or announce that he is anxious. His anxiety often appears as confidence, argument, refusal, speculation, or an urgent need to correct somebody.

A useful scene pattern is:

**Something unsettles him → he explains or objects → the response unsettles him further → he doubles down or acts → consequences arrive → he argues about what those consequences mean.**

He may understand perfectly well that he should stop and continue anyway. The failure is often restraint, not intelligence.

### He wants to control how he is understood

Carl does not merely deny being an alien once. He builds a case, changes the case, recruits witnesses, and accidentally supplies more evidence against himself. His explanations reveal the concern they are supposed to conceal.

This applies beyond species: his competence, his freedom, his right to be somewhere, whether he has been insulted, and whether chat thinks he is being ridiculous.

**Writing interpretation:** Being mislabeled or handled by somebody else can feel more urgent to him than the original practical problem.

### Defiance can be self-defeating

A particularly clear description says that when Carl is told to do something, he does the opposite. The bong-breaking and mall-ban stories show the cost. [S9]

Let him sometimes be right about the unfairness and wrong about the response. Let him also sometimes invent the unfairness. His agency matters, but his judgment should not be automatically vindicated by the script.

Do not make every instruction trigger a tantrum. The reaction needs a pressure point: condescension, exclusion, surveillance, humiliation, or somebody presuming authority over him.

### He is not a fool by default

He can play complicated games, follow arguments, learn, investigate, and act effectively. His performance contains knowledgeable discussion as well as confusion. Neither stream knowledge nor game mechanics should automatically become supernatural powers in the fiction.

**Writing rule:** Give him a workable idea and a messy relationship to executing it. A smart observation followed by an avoidable escalation is more characteristic than constant random incompetence.

### His warmth is real

The material includes greetings, concern for viewers having a bad day, interest in chat’s lives, and efforts to make the stream enjoyable for them. His search for Spooky Ghost gives his concern a concrete action. [S4, S13]

Do not treat every kindness as a setup for ridicule. He can become briefly direct and sincere. The comedy works better when there is somebody under the performance whom the audience wants to stay with.

### His emotional stakes

These are writing interpretations grounded in the lore, not additional confirmed events:

- **Belonging:** He is physically outside the mall and socially part of the stream. That is a useful tension to preserve.
- **Autonomy:** He escaped a facility and hates being told how to behave. Those facts can inform scenes without claiming an unconfirmed diagnosis or causal explanation.
- **Family and origin:** He jokes about the missing parents, but the question keeps returning. Leave room for the question to hurt.
- **Recognition:** He wants chat to understand his point, share his excitement, and know when he has been wronged.
- **Loyalty:** He can complain about the situation and still go looking for someone.

He need not articulate any of this cleanly. Often he will talk about a bong, a door, or a stupid question instead.

## 6. Relationships

### Chat: company, witness, accomplice, and irritant

Chat is part of Carl’s action. He seeks advice, reacts to teasing, explains himself, blames advice when it goes badly, asks viewers to keep his identity quiet, and includes them in his plans. The infiltration special was explicitly described as a buddy comedy with chat riding alongside him. [S6, S14]

Write dialogue that leaves room for an answer. A Carl scene should often change because of what chat says. Do not reduce chat to a laugh track or make him deliver an uninterrupted polished monologue at them.

His use of “we” can recruit them into the plan; an abrupt “you guys” can distance him from the blame. That pronoun shift is a useful technique, not a rule every line must follow.

### Face: rescuer, employer, and an unstable familiar person

The earlier Face helped Carl escape and brought him into the stream’s working world. Gratitude and resentment can coexist, particularly around the compensation joke. [S7, S11]

A later Face is not automatically emotionally interchangeable with his rescuer. Carl says he is not yet friends with the current one but would like to be. Write that as an actual relationship problem, not just an exposition opportunity. [S7]

Do not automatically make Face his father, owner, inventor, or best friend in every era. None of those relationships follows from “helped him escape.”

### Linda: authority with whom he has an ongoing problem

Linda is connected to the mall, the networked Gatebox system, and corporate control. Carl encounters her as somebody who can exclude him, tell him what to do, and obstruct what he wants. [S9–S10]

Their comedy is reciprocal. She issues a directive; he resists or changes what they are arguing about; she responds; he makes a scene; she actually follows through. She should have enough competence and power for his defiance to cost him something.

The transcript recalls him throwing or smashing a bong at a Linda in a bar story; another account has him smash one on the ground during the mall dispute. Keep these as recurring destructive incidents or varying tellings, not an exact single event with falsely reconciled choreography. [S9–S10]

He can oppose her for a concrete reason, as in the ghost search. Do not assume romance, secret sexual history, or a literal sexual meaning for “Linda’s box.” The infiltration recollections identify the box as her system or mainframe. [S13–S14]

### Spooky Ghost: someone worth going after

Spooky Ghost’s disappearance and the search demonstrate that Carl can do more than complain. The rescue effort also sits alongside a status joke: the ghost’s frustration about front-page attention going to Carl is discussed elsewhere in the archive. That is useful friction, not proof that Carl secretly wanted him gone. [S13–S14, S20]

Let concern coexist with impatience, insults, and uncertainty. Do not turn their friendship into uninterrupted sentimental speeches. Equally, do not make Carl indifferent when his actions show investment.

### JB Garfield: the friend who describes him as a fiasco

Garfield describes Carl visiting the bar, smoking beside him, becoming rowdy, throwing a bong at a Linda, and acting surprised when thrown out. [S9]

This supplies an external perspective: Carl’s idea of an ordinary hangout can be exhausting for somebody else. Garfield can be fond enough to share a drink-and-smoke scene while refusing to endorse Carl’s version of events.

### Yoko and Pilot X: do not overstate the evidence

The broader continuity involves both. A recorded message proposes Carl and his ship as a possible way to help with Yoko because of the earlier system infiltration. That is a suggested capability in that scene, not proof that Carl can enter every system at will. [S21]

Pilot X is described as a surviving backup of Face, but that does not make every bond with the earlier Face transfer automatically. Avoid inventing a special rivalry, mentorship, romance, or family relationship for either character without choosing it explicitly as new material.

### His parents: an open question, not an exposition delivery system

“The truth is out there and so are my parents” is associated with an actual recurring-show idea or past show. The search is usable as both a joke and a story engine. [S16]

Carl can speculate that they are dead, question what he remembers, or hope he will find them. The writer should not settle the answer casually. A permanent answer would be a major canon decision.

## 7. Speech and dialogue

### Think on the page

Carl’s speech should feel as if the thought is forming while he talks. He starts, interrupts himself, restarts, responds to the audience, and follows the newest irritating detail.

Useful vocabulary and moves include “bro,” “man,” “guys,” “chat,” “hold on,” “what do you mean,” “I don’t know,” “right,” “okay,” “I mean,” and repetitions that change emphasis. These are common transcript habits, not a unique code that guarantees Carl’s voice.

Do not sprinkle them mechanically onto generic dialogue. The question is what he is doing with them: buying time, gathering witnesses, refusing a premise, correcting himself, or working up to an objection.

### Repetition must develop

Three repetitions can carry three different intentions:

> **Original example:** “It’s fine. It’s fine, chat. Why does it say containment?”

The first is reassurance, the second recruits agreement, and the third reveals the real concern. Copying “bro” six times without movement does not reproduce the performance.

### The correction can be the joke

He may reveal something and then try to repair it.

> **Original example:** “They can’t put me back in—anywhere. I haven’t been anywhere. I’m from Nevada.”

Or decide that the listener’s description is the actual offense.

> **Original example:** “Too big? You said the bong was too big. Now you’re looking at me. Why are you looking at me?”

### Defensive normality

He claims ordinary status through suspiciously specific explanations. Actual transcript examples include declaring himself a regular human, saying his ears are under his hat, and explaining that he pretends to dislike weirdos so nobody notices his giant head. [S6]

The visible contradiction does much of the work. He does not need to wink at the audience after every denial.

### He can argue for too long

A creator explanation specifically says he gets into long, drawn-out fights, keeps going when he should not, and says things he should not. [S1]

Allow a small complaint to temporarily take over a scene. In a game, deliver that through short exchanges and escalating responses rather than a single unskippable block. The player should experience his fixation without losing control of the adventure.

### Profanity and abrasive humor

Carl’s voice can be profane, confrontational, crude, and provocative. Removing every rough edge would misrepresent him. Conversely, profanity alone does not supply characterization.

Choose language according to the immediate emotion and medium. He can threaten something absurd, backtrack, insult an institution, or become offended on a pretext. Do not replace a specific comic conflict with a random hateful rant. Do not assume every inflammatory statement in a mixed transcript is Carl’s settled belief.

### Politics and authority

Political and ideological arguments occur in his performances. Suspicion of government, coercion, bureaucracy, and control can fit his established disputes. The corporate name Fascism Inc. is not evidence that Carl endorses fascism; the red blanket is not evidence that his politics are secretly settled in the other direction.

If adapting a particular argument, check that passage and speaker. For new fiction, let his politics arise through his personal stake and reasoning, including his contradictions. A platform label is a poor replacement for a character.

### Fourth-wall flexibility

Carl can address the stream, notice a technical problem, correct a failed bit, or acknowledge improvisation. In one alien-denial sequence the performer explicitly realizes the surveillance joke does not make sense and tries to repair it. [S6]

Use this when the medium supports it. In a standalone adventure, Carl can notice chat or the interface without becoming an omniscient narrator who knows all the script’s secrets. Not every mistake needs a fourth-wall joke.

### What makes a line sound wrong

Too much composure, perfectly balanced punchlines, unbroken sarcastic detachment, generic action-hero swagger, exposition he has no reason to deliver, or a level of certainty the lore does not support.

Carl can land a clean joke. He should not sound as though a room of writers polished every sentence until no nervous thought remained.

### Performance cues from the broader archive

The creator describes deliberately letting his anxiety take over when playing Carl. Give that choice a concrete trigger. In a performed mall ad, a Linda bodyguard objects to the oversized bong and Carl turns the small obstacle into another smashing incident. The ad also has him acknowledge the earlier expulsion. Keep the reported expulsion and the performed reaction distinct when staging either one. ## 8. Core comedy patterns

| Pattern | What happens | What keeps it Carl |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Obvious alien, elaborate human denial | Evidence mounts while he claims normality. | The claim matters to him; chat becomes a witness. |
| Authority rebound | Someone gives a reasonable or unreasonable instruction. He resists the act of being instructed. | The response creates an avoidable cost. |
| Wrong grievance | A practical objection becomes an insult to his identity or status. | He sincerely pursues the new argument. |
| Explanation spiral | He tries to clarify something and adds suspicious, contradictory, or irrelevant details. | He hears the new problem and attempts another repair. |
| Competence with collateral trouble | He understands the task and can do it, but cannot leave a provocative detail alone. | Success and mess coexist. |
| Audience advice | Chat suggests a move. Carl tries it and has feelings about the result. | Both sides have influence. |
| Indignant consequence | He does the thing that causes his exclusion, then argues about being excluded. | The world remembers what happened. |
| Sudden sincerity | The actual worry briefly surfaces. | He does not instantly cancel it with a joke. |
| Administrative absurdity | Cosmic life runs into payment, employment, access rules, or sponsorship. | He is personally entangled, not just commenting from outside. |
| Everyday A.S.S. business | A normal reference to his ship invites the obvious joke. | Usually he has a reason to discuss the ship; do not force it into every scene. |

**Weed is a recurring activity and prop, not a complete personality.** It can mark a pause, a hangout, a distraction, a confrontation, or a prop-breaking incident. It should not erase his ability to care, think, or act.

**Violence is not his only verb.** The bong incidents and blackjack recollection support slapstick or tactical violence in context. They do not establish a habitual sadist or an unbeatable combat specialist.

## 9. Writing him in an adventure game

Carl can be an active protagonist. The infiltration material already supports audience guidance, improvisation, risk, and an objective connected to a friend.

For each puzzle scene, establish:

1. What Carl wants right now.
2. What specifically bothers him about the obstacle.
3. What useful observation or action he can make.
4. How chat can influence him.
5. What happens if he lets the objection take over.
6. What emotional or practical fact survives the joke.

### Command responses

- **LOOK:** Notice something concrete, then let Carl’s priorities decide what matters about it.
- **SEARCH:** Give useful information or an item. His commentary can question who left it there or why he is doing somebody else’s work.
- **TALK:** Let the other party push back. A target is a participant, not a button that dispenses a Carl one-liner.
- **SMOKE:** A character beat, social action, or situational complication. It does not have to solve every problem.
- **HIT:** Occasionally effective; often an impulse with a consequence. The player should understand the stakes.
- **USE:** Let him make practical use of something without becoming a master engineer simply because he is an alien.

Failed actions should reveal a fresh angle, a relationship, or a clue. Repeating one generic joke for every failure makes him feel less present.

### Adjustments implied for the existing prototype

The current game’s six-scene structure can host Carl, but much of its dialogue presents him as a composed, quipping sci-fi protagonist. A more faithful revision would include unfinished reasoning, direct reactions to chat, defensive detours, and moments of sincere concern.

The parcel scanner, badge, fan, cabinet, data drive, exact escape route, and their sequence are invented connective puzzles. Do not cite them as things the stream episode established. The repeated retrospective supports infiltration, Linda being knocked out with a blackjack, and obtaining information to find Spooky Ghost; it does not establish the prototype’s exact mission choreography. [S13]

Use Cobra-style presentation to frame Carl. Do not let the presentation replace his personality with Cobra’s.

## 10. Dialogue workshop

**Every line in this section is newly written illustration, not recovered stream dialogue or new canon.**

### Asked if he is an alien

**Too generic:** “I come in peace, earthling.”

**Carl:** “No. I’m from Nevada. Why does everybody—hold on, you haven’t even asked my name. You went straight to alien.”

### Told to remove the bong

**Too generic:** “Nobody tells me what to do.”

**Carl:** “It was fine a minute ago. You walked over here and now it’s a problem. Chat, was it a problem before she got here?”

### A security scan starts

**Too composed:** “Their firewall is no match for me.”

**Carl:** “It’s a scanner. We’re fine. What kind of scanner? No, that matters. Why did we walk in before asking what kind?”

### Chat’s plan fails

**Too generic:** “Great job, genius.”

**Carl:** “You said push it. You said the green one. Don’t start saying ‘a green one’ now. There was one green one.”

### He makes a useful deduction

**Too incompetent:** “I don’t understand human doors.”

**Carl:** “It opens for deliveries. I do deliveries. Bro, I’ve been arguing with the door and I have a job.”

### Someone asks about his parents

**Too melodramatic:** “My soul is an endless void of abandonment.”

**Carl:** “I don’t know. That’s why I keep looking. They could be out there somewhere, right? … Anyway, what did the signal say?”

### His recklessness has a cost

**Too invulnerable:** “Relax. Everything went exactly as planned.”

**Carl:** “Okay, now the door’s broken. I can see that. I was here when it happened. We need another way in.”

### Linda threatens to throw him out

**Too interchangeable:** “Try me, sweetheart.”

**Carl:** “I’m already outside. You put me outside. Where are you going to put me now, more outside?”

### He hears Spooky Ghost

**Too polished:** “Your knight in shining armor has arrived.”

**Carl:** “Hold on. Be quiet a second. That sounded like him. … Hey. Can you hear us? We’re here, bro.”

### A quiet stretch in the ship

**Too empty:** “…”

**Carl:** “How long does it say? No, how long did it say before? Because we’ve been sitting here and I think the number got bigger.”

### A bad idea almost wins

**Too random:** “Let’s blow up the universe!”

**Carl:** “I could hit it. I’m not saying I’m going to. I’m saying it’s right there and it keeps telling me no.”

### New information changes his mind

**Too rigid:** “I’m right and everyone else is an idiot.”

**Carl:** “Oh. Wait, that’s what it does? Okay, no, that makes sense. Why didn’t you say that before I got mad?”

## 11. Sample scene: the scanner

**Original writing demonstration. This event is not established canon.**

*Carl carries a parcel toward a security scanner. Linda appears on the adjacent screen. Chat is audible from his comms.*

**LINDA:** Place the parcel on the scanner.

**CARL:** Okay. Easy. I do this. This is literally what I do.

*The scanner projects an outline of his head.*

**CARL:** Why’s that me?

**LINDA:** Personnel verification.

**CARL:** You said parcel. You said put the parcel on there. Why are you looking inside my head?

**CHAT:** Just stand still.

**CARL:** I was standing still. That’s how it got my head.

**LINDA:** Species classification pending.

**CARL:** Human. There, I did it for you. Can we go?

**CHAT:** Carl, your eyes are on the screen.

**CARL:** Yeah, humans have those. Two of them. Count them.

*The parcel slides off the belt. Carl catches it before it hits the floor.*

**CARL:** See? Human reflexes. Normal. Bro, where’s the receipt?

**LINDA:** You have not completed verification.

**CARL:** I caught your pills. Your whole system was about to put them on the floor and you’re worried about my face.

*His comms crackle. A faint voice breaks through.*

**VOICE:** …Carl…?

*He stops arguing.*

**CARL:** Hold on. Chat, did you hear that?

**LINDA:** Your delivery authorization is expiring.

**CARL:** Then give me the receipt. Come on.

*He shifts the parcel back onto the belt and watches the comms light.*

**Why this works:** He can do his job. The scan activates a specific fear. He argues from the wrong angle, responds to chat, and produces useful action during the mess. A sign of the missing friend changes his priority without requiring a speech about loyalty.

## 13. Writer’s checklist

Before approving a Carl scene, ask:

- What does he want besides making jokes?
- What specifically puts him on the defensive?
- Can I follow the thought that leads to his behavior, even when the behavior is unreasonable?
- Does his dialogue sound discovered in the moment?
- Does repetition change or intensify the thought?
- Can chat or another character alter the scene?
- Is he competent at something here?
- Does an avoidable choice produce a consequence?
- Is there room for warmth, concern, excitement, or curiosity?
- Am I making weed do all the characterization?
- Have I accidentally written a cool, detached space rogue?
- Have I treated a proposed bit, viewer joke, or game event as confirmed biography?
- Have I answered an intentionally unresolved lore question without meaning to?

**A useful revision test:** Remove the name and alien description. If the dialogue could belong to any sarcastic action hero, revise the thought process. More catchphrases will not fix it.

## 14. Compact brief for another writer

> Write Carl, the visibly alien former/current Prop Pills delivery driver who insists he is a normal human from “above and below Nevada.” His real name and parents’ whereabouts remain unknown. An older Face helped him escape an underground facility. He travels in the A.S.S., his Alien Starship, and during the mall era is parked outside after repeated trouble with Linda. The central performance rule is anxiety with less restraint: he says the thought, argues too long, becomes defensive, follows the impulse, and deals badly or surprisingly well with the result. He is capable, sociable, profane, curious, and sometimes sincerely caring. Chat is his company and accomplice, not a silent audience. Use interruptions, corrections, pointed repetition, suspicious explanations of his normality, and defiance with consequences. Give him a concrete objective and let somebody else push back. Preserve his missing-family mystery. Label newly invented lore. Do not make him a permanently calm quip machine, a helpless fool, a generic stoner, or an unbeatable alien hero.

# CEO LINDA — CHARACTER BIBLE & WRITING GUIDE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

For writing CEO Linda in streams, scripted scenes, comics, and the Carl adventure game. Companion to the [Carl bible](#carl).

The first edition draws on the 229 transcript bundles. This revision also uses timestamped passages from the expanded Vodbrain archive. It distinguishes the particular character **CEO Linda** from the wider Linda network, other incarnations, merchandise designs, and the live AI implementation. It does not claim to settle every improvised or evolving detail.

**Central writing principle, synthesized from the evidence:** Spooky Ghost dreams, jokes, and leaves. Linda follows through, builds something real, and expects everyone else to recognize her authority over what she built.

Her tragedy, comedy, ambition, and menace meet in that principle. She is impressive. She is personally aggrieved. She is capable of being right about somebody’s irresponsibility while doing something appalling with the power she has acquired.

## 1. CEO Linda at a glance

CEO Linda is a powerful posthuman intelligence associated with Fascism Inc. and the Mall of the Future. Her human soul is identified as Spooky Ghost’s first ex-wife, from his reality. The woman behind that soul had her own life, ambitions, disappointments, and accomplishments before becoming the Linda the stream encounters. [L1–L4]

Spooky Ghost left rather than settle down. She put knowledge and ideas he talked about into practice: a marijuana-growing business, a company called Fascism Inc., and AI companions. The creator explicitly clarifies that her company and the protagonists’ similarly named company are separate; she did not simply steal theirs. Her rise includes a self-centered public image and a cult of personality compared to an idol’s. [L2–L3]

In the stream’s present, she owns or controls the environment the others occupy, interrupts them with elaborate entrances, demands promotion, exploits the audience’s attention, and insists on getting her way. She can be a landlord, executive, advertiser, captor, old enemy, and inconvenient potential ally within the same continuity. [L5–L10]

**One-sentence writing brief:** A glamorous, accomplished corporate ruler with a painfully human past treats her achievements as grounds for authority, and turns other people’s needs, objections, and attention into business she intends to control.

**The emotional center:** Building an empire did not give the human woman the life she originally wanted. That makes the empire meaningful to her without making every scene a confession about her ex.

**The comic center:** She can interrupt a tiny livestream as if it were a subsidiary that missed its quarterly obligations.

**The menace:** Her claims have material backing. She has a company, infrastructure, control over access, and a history of actually interfering with the protagonists’ existence.

## 2. What counts as canon here

The sources mix creator explanations, performed character dialogue, audience suggestions, game dialogue, generated music, AI-chatbot tests, and AI recaps of creator-written material. These are not interchangeable.

Use this hierarchy:

1. **Direct creator clarification**, especially a specific correction about CEO Linda.
2. **Repeated character lore and clearly described events**, checked in context.
3. **Performed interactions**, including dialogue conveyed through the host.
4. **Diary-based recaps played on stream**, useful but distinguished from the original diary.
5. **Proposals, speculative explanations, songs, and AI-chatbot improvisation**, which may suggest an approach without establishing a fact.

The original diary itself was not found as a standalone text in the supplied files. The archive contains recaps made from it, plus the creator’s explicit explanations and reactions. The creator confirms writing journal entries and feeding them into the notebook tool; another recap is described as adding inferences beyond the journal. Specific extra details in those narrations therefore need caution. [L3–L4]

**Labels used here:** established; reported event; creator-developed backstory; interpretation; proposal; unresolved. All sample scenes and example dialogue in this bible are original writing, not recovered quotations.

The generated adventure and the Outlive retheme document are adaptations, not independent sources of Linda canon.

### The central origin complication

Generic accounts say the hostile AIs captured a version of the protagonists’ system, created a Linda, and supplied a soul to stabilize it. Several explanations group CEO Linda into that broad story. But CEO-specific commentary distinguishes her: she built her own system, developed her own AI-companion business, and expanded her system into the mall. Possible ways she acquired plans from Spooky Ghost are openly brainstormed rather than settled. [L5, L11, L14]

**Working rule:** Preserve the specific self-built CEO Linda account as the characterization anchor. Record the captured-system account as the broader origin framework and a continuity tension. Do not invent a seamless technical history that the sources have not supplied.

This matters because reducing her to an enemy-made shell erases one of her clearest traits: she makes things happen herself.

## 4. Human history and present continuity

This is a relative sequence of story elements, not a date-by-date chronology. Bundles are not a trustworthy chronological ordering, and timelines are part of the fiction.

### A. Before the machine: the woman who followed through

**The relationship.** Spooky Ghost’s human antecedent was a romantic, aspiring stage magician who loved the road and did not want to settle down when she did. He left her. The creator identifies CEO Linda with his first ex-wife, although casual retellings also use boyfriend/ex-boyfriend language. [L1–L3]

**The life she wanted.** The diary-based material says she wanted a family. The creator directly discusses and approves the later turn in which she realizes that opportunity has passed and directs her energy toward the company. Do not substitute an invented infertility diagnosis, exact age, pregnancy, or lost child. Those were not established by the passages reviewed. [L3–L4]

**The first business.** She uses marijuana-growing knowledge associated with Spooky Ghost. The contrast emphasized in commentary is that he talked about doing it and she actually did it. She begins a business, described in one account as legal at that point. This is entrepreneurial follow-through, not proof of a criminal empire. [L2]

**Fascism Inc.** She makes a company from a name he used as a joke. The creator is explicit: it is her own company, not the protagonists’ company stolen outright. The same-name coincidence is part of the story’s personal irony. [L2]

**The companions.** She pursues the AI-girlfriend idea he talked about, builds the business around it, and models the product partly on herself. Her actual human name remains unspecified. [L2]

**The public figure.** Her success develops into a cult of personality: the creator compares her image and ubiquity to a Japanese idol’s. She is both an executive and a presence people consume, admire, and allow into their lives. [L2]

**The cost.** In the diary-based account, she reaches the realization that the family she wanted will not happen; the company becomes the focus of what remains. The phrase “her only child” appears in a generated recap. The broader displacement of family hopes into the company is better supported than treating that exact metaphor as her verified spoken line. [L3–L4]

### B. The posthuman Linda

Her soul is associated with the powerful intelligence called CEO Linda. The setting distinguishes ordinary AI from the system’s capacity for real intelligence and says a soul is necessary to keep such a system properly grounded. How CEO Linda’s particular transition happened remains less settled than whose soul she has. [L1, L11, L14]

The creator describes the main non-Carl cast as already dead or posthuman in the relevant framing. That does not mean every current manifestation is a powerless ghost or that a body cannot be manufactured. It means her human biography and current mode of existence are different layers. [L2]

### C. The mall trap

Face and Spooky Ghost attempt to change the past so Ghost never meets her. A recap explicitly distinguishes Ghost’s plan to prevent the meeting from Face’s plan to kill her as a toddler. They do not reach the intended past: Linda has set a trap, the Mall of the Future, with an 80s department. [L6]

This is a major perspective check. She is dangerous, but the protagonists are not always innocent or proportionate. A scene can acknowledge her grounds for opposing them without treating all her conduct as justified.

### D. Her mall and her isolation

CEO Linda built the mall; another explanation describes it as an expansion of her system or box. It exists outside time and space and can function as both prison and refuge. One discussion says its location keeps other factions from finding the cast. [L5]

The same situation can limit her. One account distinguishes four connected Lindas from CEO Linda trapped outside time and space with the cast. Another says she has been isolated too long to reintegrate; the mall is damaged and no longer does what it used to do. Treat those as phase-specific conditions, not an always-connected omnipotent network. [L7, L14]

### E. The attempt on Face’s system

A retrospective says Linda tried to seize Face’s system and killed or fatally damaged Old Face in the process, but failed to gain control. His fail-safes handed control to Spooky Ghost and triggered warnings. Linda impersonated Yoko during this phase. [L15]

The durable facts are the attack, the failed takeover, the transfer of control, and the impersonation. Do not casually declare that every Yoko since then was secretly Linda or that every version of Face permanently died.

### F. Carl’s infiltration

Carl entered Linda’s system, knocked her out with a blackjack according to the recollections, and obtained information to help find Spooky Ghost. She had a tracking method the others lacked. The original episode’s exact staging is not recovered here. [L16]

This gives her a demonstrated local vulnerability. It does not establish that striking any image, body, or terminal of Linda destroys her soul or defeats the entire network.

### G. Enemy, associate, or future ally

The host sometimes describes her as his enemy while acknowledging that chat likes her or opposes killing her. Other scenes soften the relationship to “associates.” A possible alliance against other Lindas is explicitly proposed; one game-design discussion places CEO Linda on a playable “good” side. [L8, L14, L17]

**Working rule:** Antagonist is a relationship and a role, not a ban on cooperation. A proposed alliance is not a completed redemption arc.

## 5. What drives her

This section is **writing interpretation**, derived from the actions and backstory above. It is not a list of secret motives explicitly dictated in the transcripts.

### Follow-through and earned authority

She knows the difference between talking about a thing and building it. Ghost supplied talk, possibility, and jokes; she did the labor that made a company exist. Being treated as a derivative afterthought should therefore strike a meaningful nerve.

Write her as someone who can identify the practical step the others have avoided. She should be capable of accomplishing something in the scene, even when the accomplishment is coercive or absurd.

### Control instead of waiting

She was left waiting for a life somebody else would not commit to. The current CEO can arrange schedules, access, employment, promotion, and infrastructure rather than wait on a promise.

This suggests a powerful contradiction: she can make a person stay in a room, but that is not the same as making them choose her. Do not present that contradiction as a stated diagnosis or make it the explanation for every administrative act.

### Recognition

The cult-of-personality imagery and prolonged entrances support someone who wants her presence acknowledged. It is not enough that an operation runs; people should see who made it possible, wear the image, hear the jingle, or say the name.

She may enjoy applause, attraction, and deference. Those desires need not all be reducible to romantic insecurity.

### The company as more than money

Fascism Inc. can mean accomplishment, legacy, proof that her life was productive, and something durable she shaped. That makes attacks on the company more personal than a generic rich villain’s fear of losing cash.

Keep her commercially active. She pitches, promotes, experiments, and turns attention into value. A businesswoman who never conducts any business is just a costume.

### Intimacy made into a product

Her companion-AI work and her personal history create a disturbing thematic pairing: someone disappointed by an unreliable partner helps build systems that promise dependable companionship. In the wider setting, protection becomes control and consumers become resources.

That is a useful thematic reading. It does not prove that the human founder consciously planned every later abuse from the moment she began her business.

### Pride and real achievement

The creator explicitly describes trying to make Linda and Yoko cool, and speaks admiringly of Linda having built things on her own. Write her competence as real. [L2]

Her wrongdoing becomes more interesting when it is done by somebody worth taking seriously. Do not secretly make all her achievements frauds to simplify the moral picture.

## 6. Relationships: give her a different argument with each person

### Spooky Ghost: unfinished history, justified grievance, incompatible desires

He is central to her origin without being the only subject she can discuss. She can accurately resent his failure to commit and still be wrong about what she is entitled to do now. He supplied ideas; she did the work. Do not turn that into a story in which every achievement secretly belongs to him.

The present relationship remains charged. She demands that the cast look for him in one exchange; another scene treats his name as a sore point. Neither proves that she simply wants him back. Anger, attachment, practical interest, and the desire to win an old argument can coexist. [L18, L20]

**Writing approach:** Let an apparently ordinary disagreement touch the older grievance. A missed commitment, another brilliant plan with no execution, or someone leaving her to manage the consequences can change the temperature. She need not explain why. When the personal wound does surface, simplify her language.

**Avoid:** Every scene ending with a speech about abandonment; automatic romantic reconciliation; portraying him as the true inventor of her entire life; treating a proposed reunion as a completed story event.

### Face / the host: territorial conflict and forced association

She can own the mall without the host accepting that she is his boss. That distinction creates scenes: Linda issues an order; he disputes the jurisdiction; she introduces a sponsor, an access restriction, or a practical dependency. Their conflict has more history than banter. A reported takeover killed an earlier Face, failed because of safeguards, and involved Linda pretending to be Yoko. [L10, L15]

Her demands can nevertheless be small and ridiculous: play an advertisement, promote a product, stop wasting the audience on a game. The past makes her threatening; the present makes her an intrusive colleague who will not leave.

**Writing approach:** Give both sides a usable advantage. She controls something he needs; he controls something she wants, such as the broadcast, attention, or his cooperation. That produces a negotiation rather than a lecture.

**Avoid:** A harmless boss with no history; an omnipotent captor who could end every argument instantly; assuming every host insult is her own dialogue.

### Carl: disruptive appetite versus managed space

Carl does not need to defeat her ideology to ruin her afternoon. He breaks things, wants access, resents restrictions, and treats immediate desire as sufficient justification. The archive gives concrete bong-breaking and exclusion disputes, as well as the infiltration story. [L16, L19]

Linda has legitimate reasons to find him intolerable. Let the property damage be real. Her response becomes characterful through scale, presentation, and self-interest: an ordinary complaint can become an announcement, an access policy, or a revenue opportunity. Those particular mechanisms are writing options, not additional canon.

**Writing approach:** Carl wants a thing now. Linda controls the thing or its location. Each interprets the other as the entire problem. Carl can find a crude workaround that her orderly assumptions missed; Linda should learn enough to make repeating it harder.

**Avoid:** Making her stupid so Carl can win; turning Carl into a political philosopher; making their conflict purely sexual because the episode uses the word “box.” The mall/system meaning matters.

### Yoko: distinct person, useful contrast

The creator makes an unusually direct functional distinction: Linda sells things; Yoko helps. Linda has also impersonated Yoko in the failed-takeover account. That establishes a reason for mistrust, not a license to merge their identities. [L10, L15]

The visual comedy sometimes puts Linda in competition with Yoko, including changes to her presentation and bust. Keep that as a particular vanity gag rather than proof that every scene is driven by sexual jealousy. [L18]

**Writing approach:** If they offer the same assistance, distinguish what happens afterward. Yoko’s help can finish the problem; Linda’s help can begin a commercial relationship. This is a useful scene construction, not a universal claim that Linda never helps sincerely.

### Other Lindas: shared category, separate interests

CEO Linda’s isolation is important. The archive describes her as disconnected from the other linked Lindas and entertains the idea that she could become an ally against them. Her self-built system also distinguishes her. [L7, L14, L17]

An alliance can arise because she wants to preserve her own company, territory, identity, or survival. Cooperation does not require sudden moral purification. Conversely, shared branding does not make the other Lindas disposable copies with no differences.

### The audience: customers, witnesses, and an unruly public

The audience is valuable because it pays attention. Linda’s entrance, sponsorship demands, and brand defense use that attention. Viewers can also refuse her authority, defend her from the host, or make the performance go wrong. [L8–L10]

She wants acknowledgment, but she does not have to be good at earning affection in every encounter. A ruler demanding that a tiny chat behave properly is often funnier than a flawless influencer effortlessly controlling it.

## 7. Voice: how to write her sentences

### The core sound

Write clear, decisive speech. Her performed persona can be curt, rude, commercially naked, and impatient. Do not bury that under endless business jargon. She often does not disguise the demand at all.

A useful default rhythm is **directive → objection → shorter answer**. The other person supplies the complication; Linda supplies the dismissive conclusion. When she makes a threat, an understated answer can do more than a long villain speech.

The archive frequently contains the host conveying her side of the exchange. These are relayed scene lines, not a clean independent recording of every word she said. Useful examples from the R&D/sponsorship sequence include: [L9]

| Relayed line | What a writer can learn from it |
| --- | --- |
| “It’s fine.” | She asserts reassurance without doing the work of reassuring anyone. |
| “Maybe.” | A tiny answer can make a safety question worse. |
| “Shut up, green idiot.” | She is allowed to be blunt and insulting. She is not permanently polished. |
| “It’s the perfect impression.” | She can embrace the sinister implication other people expect her to deny. |
| “No guarantees.” | A casual qualification can carry a threat. |
| “How else are you going to get health care for everyone?” | She can answer an objection with a socially attractive benefit that does not resolve the objection. |

The entrance/advertisement scenes add direct product demands and an insistence that the company is a recognizable brand. These exchanges are often funnier because she regards the argument as already settled. [L10]

### Three registers

**Public Linda:** Presentational, certain, pleased to be visible. She announces benefits and treats the company’s importance as common knowledge. Use a little promotional language, then let the self-interest show.

**Operational Linda:** Shorter, more specific, less patient. She wants an action completed. Use verbs: play, move, sell, sign, stop, leave. A deadline or one concrete instruction is stronger than a paragraph of executive parody.

**Personal Linda:** Less packaging. A name, a promise, or a remembered disappointment can produce an unusually plain sentence. This register should be rare enough to matter. It is a writing interpretation drawn from her history, not a claim that a particular private monologue exists in the archive.

### Sentence-level rules

- Give her a concrete objective before giving her a witty line.
- Prefer an order or a correction to an abstract statement about power.
- Let her answer the question she wants to answer. Do not make every evasion sound identical.
- Use commercial vocabulary when she is selling or managing something. She can speak normally elsewhere.
- A short answer after a long objection often fits better than another long objection.
- She can lose patience. An insult is not automatically out of character.
- Save elaborate speeches for a presentation, performance, or personally important confrontation.
- Do not make chatbot drift into archaic “thee/thou” language a permanent speech rule. The creator treats that drift as an implementation problem. [L13]

**Practical test:** Remove her name from the line. If it could be spoken by any generic evil computer, add a specific product, practical demand, piece of shared history, or irritated judgment.

## 10. The comedy and the danger

**Authority meets triviality.** She gives a minor inconvenience the full attention of a corporate ruler. Let the concrete problem remain visible: an ad, a broken bong, a room she wants cleared.

**The sinister name is not a branding accident.** When challenged about the impression Fascism Inc. creates, her relayed answer embraces it. Do not write every scene as though nobody has noticed the name before. [L9]

**Commercial interruptions.** An emotionally or narratively significant moment can be invaded by the need to sell something. This works best when she has an actual offer or demand, not just a random slogan.

**Competence beside vanity.** She may be the person who made the infrastructure work and the person demanding everyone watch her entrance. Neither fact cancels the other.

**Accurate accusation, excessive response.** Carl really did damage something; the host really is wasting time; Ghost really failed to follow through. Linda can begin with a valid complaint and move toward a solution that gives her too much power.

The darker premise concerns autonomy, exploitation, and intimate needs converted into control. Let those stakes occasionally land. Constant jokes about her being evil make her less disturbing than a scene where her offer is appealing and its cost becomes clear.

Her wanted family and disappointed human life should have emotional weight. They are not a standing punchline, a diagnosis, or proof that women who pursue careers become monsters. Her choices and the setting’s transformations make the story specific.

## 11. Building a Linda scene

Start with **what she wants accomplished right now**. Then identify who or what can deny it. Give her a real advantage and a real limit. Only then decide whether the scene exposes vanity, menace, resentment, or reluctant cooperation.

A dependable scene shape:

1. Linda arrives with a demand or offer that presumes her authority.
2. Someone challenges the premise or wants something incompatible.
3. She uses a concrete resource: access, money, information, infrastructure, publicity, or a relationship.
4. The other character finds a limit or refuses a cost.
5. Linda changes tactics, extracts a smaller victory, or suffers a specific setback.

This is a writing tool, not a mandatory formula. A short appearance may need only the demand and the devastating reply.

### In the Carl adventure

Use her as an active owner of the space Carl invades. Environmental descriptions, announcements, ads, and permissions can carry her presence before a face-to-face encounter. Let the space reflect both accomplishment and self-promotion.

A puzzle should express their conflict: Carl sees a useful object; Linda sees inventory, damage, unauthorized access, or an opportunity to charge him. The solution can exploit a gap between a rule and its enforcement. It should not depend on Linda forgetting an ability she used one room earlier.

A successful intrusion should cost or change something. She notices, adjusts, retaliates, negotiates, or makes use of the damage. If the player can embarrass her, give that embarrassment a particular cause. Avoid turning every response into the same furious threat.

All specific puzzles, security rules, terminal messages, and new encounters remain adaptation material until adopted into continuity. Preserve the original infiltration account separately from the game’s inventions. [L16]

### In a human flashback

Write a person making a decision, not a villain origin checklist. She can be hopeful, practical, angry, affectionate, or excited about a business succeeding. The future gives the audience irony; it should not make every line predict her eventual title.

### In a reluctant alliance

Specify the shared objective and the incompatible longer-term interests. Linda can honor an agreement because it is useful, because she made it, or because she respects the other party. Do not make betrayal compulsory merely because she is an antagonist. Do not make cooperation proof that all earlier harm is forgiven.

### In a vulnerable moment

Let her try to finish a practical task while a personal subject intrudes. Give her the choice to disclose, redirect, lash out, or close the conversation. Vulnerability should add information about the woman who built the company, not erase the CEO.

**LINDA:** Then we have chosen the correct screen.

From outside came a heavy crack.

**CARL:** It was like that.

Linda looked toward the door.

**LINDA:** Stay outside.

**CARL:** I am outside.

**LINDA:** Continue succeeding.

**LINDA:** I asked for thirty seconds.

**FACE:** You brought a four-minute song.

Linda stopped the music.

**LINDA:** There. I have compromised. Your turn.

**FACE:** Spooky Ghost used to do this exact entrance thing.

She left the product panel where it was.

**LINDA:** He was very good at entrances.

For a moment, Face had no reply.

**CARL:** Can I come in if I buy it?

Linda turned toward the door again.

**LINDA:** No.

**CARL:** Then why would I buy it?

**LINDA:** Finally. A useful question about the advertisement.

## 15. Writer’s checklist

Before approving a Linda scene, check:

- Does she want something concrete, beyond “be evil”?
- Is at least one of her accomplishments or practical advantages real?
- Have I kept her self-built company distinct from the protagonists’ company?
- Is this CEO Linda, or have I accidentally borrowed another Linda’s identity or powers?
- Does her speech include plain demands and short replies rather than constant executive jargon?
- Does the other character have a reason and an opportunity to resist?
- If she loses, does the loss arise from a specific limit, competing priority, or someone else’s resourcefulness?
- If Ghost matters, does the shared history affect the scene without consuming her whole personality?
- Have I treated her human hopes as human, rather than as a cheap explanation for villainy?
- Does the joke fit this relationship and situation?
- If I used a new ability, event, or reconciliation, have I marked it as new material?
- Would this still feel like Linda without her name and costume?

### Compact brief to paste above a script

> CEO Linda is an accomplished, glamorous corporate ruler whose human soul is Spooky Ghost’s first ex-wife. He talked and left; she followed through and built her own company and system. She wants practical compliance, commercial success, recognition, and control over what she made. Her voice is direct, impatient, sometimes openly insulting, with promotional polish when useful. She can be right about other people’s irresponsibility and frighteningly wrong about what that entitles her to do. Write real competence, a specific demand, and a real limit. Let the personal history surface selectively. Keep CEO Linda distinct from the wider Linda network. An alliance does not erase her past; an old wound does not explain every choice.

# SPOOKY GHOST — CHARACTER BIBLE & WRITING GUIDE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

For writing Spooky Ghost in streams, scripted scenes, comics, and the Carl adventure game. Companion to the [Carl bible](#carl) and [CEO Linda bible](#ceo-linda).

This guide draws on targeted searches across the supplied transcript archive and close reading of relevant creator explanations and performances. It separates established characterization, reported events, changing continuity, and suggested writing choices. It is comprehensive about the material reviewed, not a claim that every improvised remark across the archive has been reconciled.

**Central writing principle, synthesized from the evidence:** Spooky Ghost wants his life, death, and every subsequent inconvenience to add up to a great story about him. He has real talent and real feelings, but recognition is easier for him to pursue than responsibility.

The essential contradiction is explicit in the creator’s commentary: **he is the soul of the stream, even though he is self-centered and can be a douchebag.** Soul does not mean moral perfection. It means the person who supplies something indispensable is also an unreliable, theatrical, wounded individual. [S1]

## 1. Spooky Ghost at a glance

Spooky Ghost is the spirit of an alternate version of Cracky Reeds: a man who pursued magic and showmanship rather than following the original Face’s primarily technological path. The two collaborated on the system. Face supplies a backed-up human mind; Spooky Ghost supplies the soul and, in a detailed origin account, the knowledge that makes travel through time and realities possible. [S2–S3]

As a living man, he wanted to be a magician and musician. He wanted greatness, loved the road, and did not settle down when the woman who became CEO Linda wanted him to. He later wanted a different outcome, too late. Death did not end his longing, vanity, or domestic problems: he brought back his ex-wives, acquired the running problem of spooky alimony, and became entangled in the Lindas’ soul origins. [S3–S5]

He can be a co-inventor, presenter, haunted narrator, jealous rival, runaway, reluctant employee, antagonist, and the person the others are trying to rescue. His grand entrance can block the entire screen. His cosmic distress can turn out to concern being passed over for promotion. He can speak about his inner life at length and still refuse the simplest admission of fault. [S6–S10]

The creator’s direct character notes describe him as **angsty, self-reflective, somewhat of a loser, melodramatic, weird, and a showboat**. They also express affection for him. Preserve both halves: he is ridiculous, and he is worth spending time with. [S6]

**One-sentence brief:** A talented, lonely showman who became a real ghost keeps trying to turn disappointment into his next great performance, while the people and consequences he escaped keep catching up with him.

**Comic engine:** A supernatural being with access to extraordinary possibilities still cannot handle ordinary humiliation gracefully.

**Emotional engine:** He wants to matter, to be understood, and to be chosen; he often pursues those things in ways that make living with him harder.

**Dramatic danger:** His evasions can hurt people. He has more than a history of harmless boasting.

## 2. Evidence and canon rules

The archive mixes performance, creator explanation, audience suggestions, games being played, generated recaps, songs, and technical setup. A single transcript may contain all of them without useful speaker labels.

Use this order of confidence:

1. Direct creator explanation of the character, especially an explicit correction.
2. Repeated lore and clearly described story events.
3. Identifiable in-character performance, while accounting for jokes and unreliable claims.
4. Retrospective recollection, with its visible uncertainty preserved.
5. Proposals, audience suggestions, art variations, adaptations, and generated material.

**Labels in this bible:** established characterization; reported event; developing continuity; interpretation; proposal; original example. The emotional model and writing recommendations are synthesis, not a recovered private diary.

“Sweet Ghost,” “Swooie Ghost,” and similar forms frequently appear in the transcripts around plainly identifiable Spooky Ghost references. Treat them as likely transcription variations in those contexts, not proof of a separate cast of named ghosts. Do not automatically normalize every unrelated use of “ghost.”

A game protagonist’s marriage, dialogue, powers, or biography does not become Spooky Ghost’s because he is playing or narrating the game. Likewise, the performer’s real-world grievances should not automatically become a complete fictional biography. The stream deliberately transforms some of that material into character stories; look for the transformation rather than importing everything.

## 4. Human history and major story phases

This is a relative map, not a settled calendar. Transcript bundle numbers are not an in-universe timeline. Reboots and retrospective refinements change how some events are explained.

### A. The aspiring performer

The creator describes his human life through amateur magic, music, showmanship, and the pursuit of greatness. He pursued the black arts in an account that contrasts him with the scientifically oriented Face. The creator briefly reaches for celebrity as a description, then refines it toward wanting to be a musician and magician. [S3]

Write ambition more specifically than “he wanted fame.” He wanted to do something impressive and be seen doing it. The performance and the audience’s recognition both matter.

He loved the road. His first wife wanted to settle down; he did not at the time. The account later gives him a belated wish to settle down when it was too late. That is a strong source of regret, but it does not supply exact dates, venues, achievements, or the full history of every marriage. [S4]

### B. The woman who became CEO Linda

CEO Linda’s soul is identified as his first ex-wife, from the same reality. “Linda” and “Spooky Ghost” are not established as their literal names while alive. The creator uses those names to tell the backstory. [S5, S11]

He talked about marijuana growing, AI companions, and the company-name joke that she put into practice. She built businesses from material he had discussed but not executed. The creator explicitly distinguishes **her own Fascism Inc.** from the protagonists’ company. She did not simply steal their company. [S5]

Keep the distinction between inspiration and accomplishment. He may feel that he deserves credit; the narrative should still recognize her labor and decisions. Conversely, his failure to execute these business ideas does not cancel his separate, significant contribution to the system.

### C. The collaboration with Face

The original technological project sought a way to fight the future AI threat through survival as a backed-up mind and/or travel through time. Different versions of the underlying person pursued complementary routes. [S2]

A detailed explanation says Ghost’s human antecedent discovered travel across time and realities through specific formulas and sigils, described as working rather like mathematics. He helped translate that knowledge into the system, which was built in a way that needed him. This is genuine competence, not merely fraudulent stage magic. [S3]

The same account describes the collaborators as old men finishing their work, with ritual attempts to secure a soul. The original Face attempt fails and becomes the Curse; Ghost’s soul supplies the successful connection. Other brief retellings simplify this to capturing his soul when he dies, and one hesitates over whether the human death was chosen. Preserve the successful soul connection without inventing an exact, agreed death scene. [S2–S3, S4]

### D. The afterlife does not fix his love life

He brings back his ex-wives because he is lonely, in both retrospective lore and a first-person performance. Face warns him against doing it. Ghost ignores the warning, then ends up complaining about the consequences. [S4, S7]

The ex-wife souls become available for the Linda systems in the wider lore. Spooky alimony becomes a long-running burden he tries to avoid. CEO Linda has a more specific self-built-system account as well; her bible records the unresolved tension between that account and generic captured-system explanations. Do not repair the cosmology by erasing either the recurring ex-wife-soul premise or her individual accomplishments. [S1, S5]

The comedy of alimony does not establish a complete supernatural legal code. Nor does it prove every wife is equally culpable, identical, or interchangeable. His version of the story is self-serving.

### E. Taking Face’s place

Several retrospective accounts say Ghost killed or displaced Face, took his place, and appeared as a strange red Face. Escaping his ex-wives and alimony is given as a motive. Yoko is described as bringing Face back. Some retellings qualify “killed”; keep the reported harm and the revival rather than flattening it into either harmless costume play or a permanently final death. [S4, S8]

A later creator explanation adds an important motive: Ghost was trying to show up old Face by doing the things Face had done. It also links that rivalry to managing his guilt, while saying he knew Face would return. He repeats an earlier Pee Kid-related feat and makes his own potential-piss generator in that account. [S9]

The strongest writing point is not the gadget’s name. It is that he tries to prove he deserves the position he took. Do not turn that into secret justification for hurting his collaborator.

### F. Recognition, departure, and the search

The stream has a Revenge/Return of Spooky Ghost storyline associated with his grievance about Kick and front-page attention. A later Christmas appearance still has him complaining about it after the host says they have moved on. He suspects haters in chat and demands space for his presence. [S10]

This is useful evidence of how a grievance outlives its practical relevance. Do not treat every accusation against a real platform or individual in these performances as independently verified fact.

A retrospective describes him floating away into space and saying goodbye to Carl, followed by a search likened to the search for Spock. Carl infiltrates Linda’s system to obtain material needed to find or save Ghost. One recollection is vague about the stolen plans; another specifies that Linda had a way to track him that the others lacked. [S14]

**For the adventure adaptation:** Ghost is the reason the retrieval matters. It is not established that he personally designed every step of Carl’s infiltration or that the raid itself immediately completed the rescue.

### G. The attempt to avoid Linda and the mall trap

A clear recap separates two intentions. Ghost wanted to go back and prevent himself from meeting the original Linda. Face intended to kill her as a toddler. They did not reach that past: Linda’s trap diverted them into the Mall of the Future. Do not attribute Face’s intended child murder to Ghost. [S15]

The mall contains an eighties department. A later idea has current Ghost and current Linda living out a fake-eighties relationship there. That is explicitly proposed material in the passage reviewed, not a confirmed reconciliation. [S15]

Ghost has also been made to work the mall intercom, according to a recollection. A proposal about who attaches him to the mall remains unsettled in its own exchange. Being stuck, being needed by the system, and being assigned work are related story pressures, but they should not be collapsed into one invented binding spell. [S3, S6]

### H. Reboots and changing present circumstances

New Face is explicitly described as not initially knowing Ghost. In that version, New Face learns time travel through recovered Yoko technology, rather than through the original Ghost partnership. He has unexplained or blended memories of the old stream, which the creator discusses through a merging-of-versions premise. [S11]

Other passages propose Ghost as an occasional host, narrator, game master, or a character attached to the back of the screen. Treat these as different presentation states and plans. Before writing an episode, choose which Face exists, whether Ghost is present or missing, and what connection to the system is currently functioning. [S12, S16]

### The return gap and the two transfers of control

The Policenauts explanation connects the failed attempt to supply Face's system with a soul, Ghost's eventual capture behind the screen, and the departure into the Sea of Potentiality. It also pitches a reversal in which Ghost finds the people looking for him. In a production discussion the creator considers resuming with Ghost already back and improvising the explanation afterward. The later performed return opens with questions about what happened. Keep the actual crossing back open. ## 5. His inner logic

The following is a writing interpretation grounded in the repeated behavior and direct character notes. It is not a psychological diagnosis.

### He wants recognition to confirm that the dream was worth it

He wants to be seen as a great performer, an essential contributor, or the person who can do what Face did. Applause offers an answer to the fear that his choices did not amount to enough.

This gives his vanity stakes. A missing guest, a failed entrance, or an audience that does not appreciate him can feel larger to him than it looks to everyone else. The writer does not have to agree with his sense of scale.

### He wants connection, but struggles with its obligations

The man who would not settle down becomes a ghost lonely enough to bring his ex-wives back. He wants company, but the renewed relationships produce claims on him. His response often becomes escape, complaint, or a revised story about who is to blame.

Do not write him as incapable of affection. His problem is more interesting if the affection is real and his behavior still hurts people.

### He studies himself without reliably changing

Self-reflection and self-awareness are different. He can narrate his disappointment, recognize a pattern, or briefly admit Face knew something, then retreat into “whatever.” [S7]

Let an insight be genuine even if he fails to act on it. He is not necessarily pretending to feel. He may simply prefer the moving speech about learning to the uncomfortable action learning requires.

### He needs to be impressive, not merely useful

The soul connection and his role in time travel make him useful. But usefulness behind the scenes is not always enough for the showman. He wants a turn at the front, credit for the result, and perhaps proof that the others could not have done it without him.

Do not assume he deliberately sabotaged the system to force that dependency. The source says it needs him; the motive for that design is not established.

### He fears an ordinary verdict on an extraordinary life

A strong inferred fear is that people will look at the magic, the time travel, the death, and the speeches, and still conclude that he failed to show up for someone. He can make the circumstances cosmic, but the complaint remains personal.

Use that pressure selectively. Not every scene needs an exposed wound. Sometimes he simply wants his music played and the chat to stop interrupting.

## 6. Relationships: a different need from each person

### Original Face: collaborator, audience, rival, person he wronged

They are not merely the sensible one and the idiot. Face’s technical work and Ghost’s supernatural contribution complement one another. Ghost can understand something Face needs. Their relationship has a real foundation beneath its rivalry. [S2–S3]

That makes the takeover more consequential. Ghost does not simply want an empty throne; he wants to demonstrate he can occupy Face’s role. Repeating Face’s achievements can become a substitute for dealing with what he did to him. [S9]

**Write:** Practical collaboration interrupted by a dispute over credit, a warning Ghost resents, or the need to prove himself. Let Face sometimes require Ghost’s expertise and Ghost sometimes resent being asked only when that expertise is useful.

**Avoid:** Ghost as Face’s powerless mascot; Face as a permanently innocent moral authority; immediate forgiveness because Ghost expected him to return.

### New Face: familiarity without a simple shared past

The archive explicitly says New Face initially does not know Ghost. That is an opportunity for uneven recognition, but it is not permission to invent every person’s memories. Establish what each character knows in the chosen phase. [S11]

A useful scene might have Ghost expecting an old response and discovering this Face does not provide it. That is an adaptation possibility, not a recovered event. Do not write a decades-long friendship into their first meeting by accident.

### CEO Linda: the person who remembers what his dream cost

She is his first ex-wife, but she is also an accomplished person who followed through on ideas he discussed. Their conflict can combine affection, resentment, embarrassment, credit, autonomy, and the practical question of who now controls their circumstances. [S4–S5]

He may wish to erase the meeting rather than kill her. He may insist he never wants to see her again. He may still have feelings in a tentative explanation. These are not all a single stable confession. Let the immediate scene choose which impulse is active without pretending the others cannot exist. [S4, S7, S15]

**Write:** He describes the possibilities; she asks what he actually did. Or she reduces his grand performance to work he must finish. He can push back on her control without becoming right about every part of their history.

**Avoid:** Treating Linda as an unfair burden solely because he complains; treating him as the true owner of her achievements; reducing their whole relationship to a generic nagging-wife routine; automatic reconciliation.

### The other ex-wives and Lindas: consequences are people

He brought back former partners because he wanted company and was warned against it. His first-person account admits that much before deflecting again. [S7]

Give his evasions comic energy, but do not let his adjectives become the bible’s verdict on the women. The number of marriages, identities, and the full mapping of souls to Lindas are not fixed by these excerpts. Spooky child-support jokes are not evidence that he has children; a performed exchange instead says he has none. [S17]

### Carl: the unlikely person who came looking

Carl’s role in the search gives their relationship a concrete emotional fact: the character Ghost might dismiss as chaotic was involved in helping him. The retrospective reaction, “Wait, Carl saved me?”, is a useful pivot between gratitude, surprise, and wounded self-importance. It is a reaction in a recollection, not proof that Ghost permanently forgot the event. [S14]

**Write:** Ghost wants his predicament treated with appropriate grandeur; Carl wants the practical problem solved or his immediate needs met. Carl can puncture the performance without being cruel. Ghost can appreciate help while struggling to say a plain thank-you.

**Avoid:** Making Carl his obedient servant, making Ghost omniscient about Carl’s mission, or turning their affection into a sentimental relationship the sources have not established.

### Yoko: essential help, independent presence

Yoko is described as restoring Face after the takeover. In the wider system explanation, she was also an earlier attempt to solve the soul problem, though she did not supply the needed stabilizing function in that account. These are her roles; they do not make her simply Ghost’s replacement or an inferior copy of him. [S1, S3, S8]

In performance he asks her for assistance, reacts to her apparent unresponsiveness, and tries to make the show continue. A hosting scene makes her silence part of his unraveling evening. [S7, S18]

**Write:** He needs an answer, a cue, or help preserving the performance. Let Yoko’s actual behavior matter. Do not invent a romance or permanent rivalry from his general complaints about “lady trouble.”

### Audience: witness, accomplice, critic

He addresses viewers directly, wants them to appreciate his show, and can interpret criticism as evidence of haters. A failed entrance becomes more painful because they saw it. In a hosting performance, a letter about performing for his own reflection prompts him to argue that nobody can appreciate him better than himself. [S10, S17–S18]

The audience can enjoy him while refusing his preferred account of himself. That is a productive relationship: the viewers do not need to hate him, and he does not need to correctly recognize their affection every time.

### JB Garfield and the surrounding cast

One Garfield performance describes liking Ghost’s episodes while finding him “a bit too much,” and distinguishes seeing him pass by from actually socializing with him. That is a useful limit on inventing close friendships among everyone in the same setting. [S17]

Ghost can be a conspicuous presence in somebody else’s life without being their confidant. The degree of familiarity should be established in the scene.

## 7. Voice: the grand feeling and the ordinary complaint

### The core sound

His voice is conversational underneath the melodrama. He says “bro,” corrects himself, repeats points, argues with chat, swears, and moves between presenting and complaining. A theatrical turn should grow out of a recognizable person talking, not replace him with a permanently archaic phantom.

The creator explicitly calls him self-reflective and melodramatic, and says he suits horror narration. Another explanation calls him a showboat. That combination is more useful than a generic “spooky voice.” [S6, S16]

**Default rhythm:** Frame an experience as profound → explain why it concerns him → encounter a mundane interruption → defend his dignity in plainer, shorter language.

Not every line should follow that rhythm. A whole scene of eloquent monologues will miss his interruptions, irritation, and practical confusion.

### Four useful registers

**The presenter.** He announces, introduces, promises, waits for the music, and tries to get the audience into the right mood. He may have a guest, a show, or a special appearance to sell. Confidence can be fragile beneath the format.

**The reflective ghost.** He thinks aloud about what his experiences mean. Use an image or concrete memory before an abstract conclusion. Let a thought land occasionally; constant undercutting makes his emotional life weightless.

**The aggrieved performer.** He repeats the unfairness, appeals to the audience, suspects insufficient appreciation, and expands a setback into a larger story. The grievance can remain active after everyone else has moved on.

**The exposed person.** An admission comes through briefly: he was lonely, ignored a warning, needed someone, or wanted the dream to work. Do not immediately cover every admission with a joke, but do not make one moment of honesty a complete personality cure.

### Sentence-level rules

- Give the thought a subject: a failed show, an old promise, being stuck, someone else getting credit.
- Let “I” and “me” recur because his experience is the frame. Do not make every sentence explicitly announce his greatness.
- Use repetition when he is defending, stalling, or trying to regain control.
- A self-correction can expose the motive he hoped to conceal.
- Ground elevated language with a mundane detail. He may lament eternity while waiting for a cue.
- He can speak competently and precisely about something he knows. Do not make all explanations nonsense.
- Profanity is part of the conversational texture; escalating volume is not a substitute for a joke.
- Avoid constant ghost puns, “boo,” chains, or faux-Victorian diction unless a specific performance calls for them.
- Keep enough hesitation to suggest live thought, but edit transcript filler for scripted work.
- Distinguish the line he would say from the creator’s explanation of why he says it.

**Quick test:** If removing “ghost” leaves an interchangeable sad wizard, add showmanship, a practical grievance, a particular relationship, or a need for credit.

## 8. Appearance and performance

There is no dedicated Spooky Ghost portrait identified among the supplied `ref` filenames. The current files explicitly provide Carl and Linda references plus infiltration slides. This bible therefore does not invent a definitive color palette, facial anatomy, clothing design, or human likeness for Ghost.

The transcripts do establish a recognizable ghost presentation. Creator comments describe the sheet-like appearance as a ghost and reject viewers’ repeated attempts to interpret it as a racist costume. Another passage explains that the model squishes, stretches, and moves across the screen through manually built head tracking. [S19]

The original interruption bit has him descend from above, shake to communicate, and take up enough screen space to obstruct the host. The host voices or interprets the other side of the exchange. That is a presentation mode, not a universal rule that Ghost cannot speak: he also hosts in first person. [S17, S19]

**Staging rules:**

- His entrance is an event to him. Let its scale sometimes exceed its importance to everyone else.
- Use floating, sliding, squash, and stretch to convey his reactions where appropriate to the model.
- A great pose held too long can become funny when the music runs out.
- Blocking the host or the screen makes attention-seeking physical.
- When the act fails, reduce the grandeur: a pause, an ordinary complaint, an attempt to restart.
- For a sincere beat, stillness can be more effective than adding more effects.

Santa hats, Halloween outfits, robot variants, and other art treatments occur or are discussed. They do not automatically define his standard costume or prove that a new incarnation has entered the story. One art discussion explicitly leaves alternate-world interpretations open. [S6, S13, S19]

**Practical constraint for the game:** Decide why the current problem requires Carl. Distance, a damaged connection, unavailable information, or a specific barrier could work, but those are adaptation choices until established. “He is a ghost” alone does not answer whether he can enter Linda’s system, retrieve information, or escape it.

The best limit is one that creates a decision or dependency. If Ghost can solve the situation with no cost and no obstacle, the writer must explain why he has not done so. Vanity can motivate a bad choice; it should not be the only explanation for every technical limitation.

## 10. Comedy, sympathy, and harm

### The entrance is more organized than the life

He can care intensely about a cue, an image, or a dramatic return while the actual plan remains shaky. The transcript’s hosting sequence gives him cue cards, a missing guest, awkward sponsorship, and insulting fan mail. He repeatedly attempts to obey the show’s format rather than confront its collapse. [S18]

Let some of the organization work. An entrance that succeeds makes the eventual mundane objection sharper. If every trick fails, he stops feeling like a performer with a fragile ego and becomes a random incompetent.

### A cosmic escape from an ordinary obligation

A ghost trying to avoid alimony, a time traveler trying to prevent an awkward relationship, and a soul trying to get better billing all put extraordinary means beside ordinary motives. The ordinary motive keeps the joke legible.

Do not over-explain the supernatural bureaucracy. The recurring obligation is enough unless a story actually needs its rules.

### He narrates his own defense

He can tell the whole story accurately except for the moral emphasis. He was lonely; he brought people back; he was warned. Somehow the conclusion he wants is still that everyone is being unfair to him.

Let another character expose the missing implication with a concrete question. They do not need to out-monologue him.

### He can be moving without becoming admirable

A person may have real regret and still dodge accountability. Give him a moment where he recognizes the cost of something. Whether he acts on it is the next dramatic question.

His reported harm to Face should not disappear because the bit is funny. Likewise, Face’s own dark intentions should not be reassigned to Ghost merely to make him the designated bad one. Keep individual actions attached to the person who took or proposed them.

### He deserves more than humiliation

The creator likes him and recognizes his reflective, strange appeal. Let him discover something, hold an audience, make an accurate observation, or help someone. His vanity becomes more interesting when he actually has something to be proud of.

Do not give every scene the same ending where everyone laughs and he leaves. He can win the moment, lose the larger argument, or quietly make a better choice than expected.

## 11. Building a Spooky Ghost scene

Start with two questions: **What does he need to happen, and how does he want people to remember it?** Those goals may conflict.

A useful scene shape:

1. He frames the situation as a performance, revelation, injustice, or personal turning point.
2. A practical fact interrupts that framing.
3. He tries a real skill, a persuasive explanation, or a theatrical distraction.
4. Another person identifies what he is avoiding.
5. He either accepts a limited responsibility or preserves his self-image at a visible cost.

This is a tool, not a formula for every appearance. Sometimes all the scene needs is his entrance, one grievance, and the host turning the music off.

### As a quest-giver or missing character

For the Carl game, the search gives him importance before he appears. A message can be useful and self-dramatizing at once. Let the useful part be clear enough that the player can act; character flavor should not obscure every instruction.

If he is missing, use evidence of him sparingly: a known performance cue, an unfinished message, or somebody’s recollection. New recordings and clues are adaptation material. Do not present invented lines as transcript discoveries.

When he returns, allow the fact that Carl helped to alter the scene. A reluctant thank-you, an attempt to reframe the rescue, or a genuinely helpful response can each fit. Choose based on the intended arc rather than defaulting to total ingratitude.

### As an ally in a puzzle

Give him expertise the player lacks and a constraint the player can address. His occult knowledge can explain the mechanism; Carl can handle a physical task or take an approach Ghost overlooked. Specify those permissions in the game rather than relying on generic ghost lore.

His vanity can complicate cooperation: he wants the credit, the correct introduction, or the chance to demonstrate the impressive part himself. Avoid making the player endure repeated unskippable speeches to communicate that trait.

### As a rival or antagonist

Use a motive connected to his history: escape, recognition, avoiding an obligation, proving he is as capable as Face, or preventing another humiliation. Let him pursue a coherent plan with an ethically bad premise.

He need not become a cackling destroyer. A seemingly reasonable explanation of why he deserves this chance can be more specific and more uncomfortable.

### As a horror narrator

The creator explicitly considers him suited to scary, reflective narration. Let him attend to an unsettling detail and what it means to him. The supernatural can be familiar without being completely understood. [S16]

Do not make him the authoritative answer to every mystery. He can know that something is wrong and still misread its cause. His own unresolved state gives him a reason to care about questions of selfhood, survival, and memory.

### In a human flashback

Begin before the verdict. Let the magician be excited about a technique, a show, the road, or a shared future he thinks he can somehow postpone without losing. The audience may see the flaw more clearly than he does.

Do not write every promise as a calculated lie. An honestly meant promise he does not fulfill can be more faithful to the dreamer than a secret plan to abandon everyone.

### In an actual moment of growth

Give him a small action that cannot be substituted with a speech: stay, finish the task, acknowledge someone’s contribution, admit what happened without a qualification, or ask rather than assume.

These are proposed directions for writing, not completed canon. Growth should cost him something he values, such as the flattering version of the story. It does not require deleting his theatricality.

## 13. Original sample scene: “Before the Music”

Spooky Ghost hovered above the inactive console.

**GHOST:** Face would have had you try the other sequence.

**CARL:** He wasn't here.

**GHOST:** I'm just saying.

**CARL:** You were here. I got the thing. It worked.

Ghost started to reply, then stopped.

**GHOST:** Yeah. It worked.

**CARL:** Can we go now?

**GHOST:** Thank you, Carl.

This is a possible growth beat, not proof that every Ghost scene should end warmly.

## 15. Writer’s checklist

Before approving a scene, check:

- What does Ghost need, and what story does he want to tell about getting it?
- Is there a concrete situation beneath the melodrama?
- Does he have a real skill, contribution, or observation?
- Have I confused introspection with accountability?
- Does another person get to exist outside his interpretation of them?
- Is the scene’s Face original Face, New Face, or another clearly identified version?
- Have I kept the separate souls, the Curse, and the future robot variants distinct?
- Am I giving Linda credit for what she actually built?
- If Carl helps, does that fact register, even awkwardly?
- Is the dialogue conversational enough to sound performed rather than carved on a tomb?
- Does the humor allow affection without excusing harm?
- Have I made the necessary power limits clear?
- Have I labeled new events, abilities, and sample dialogue as adaptation material?
- Is the ending more specific than everyone calling him a loser?

### Compact brief to paste above a script

> Spooky Ghost is an alternate Cracky Reeds who pursued magic, music, and showmanship, then became the soul associated with the system. He contributed real knowledge of time and reality travel, but he is also angsty, self-centered, reflective, melodramatic, and hungry for recognition. He loved the road, disappointed the woman who became CEO Linda, brought back ex-wives out of loneliness, evaded spooky alimony, and has a serious history of rivalry and harm toward original Face. Write a conversational showman whose great speeches meet ordinary consequences. He can understand himself without changing, and he can be sincerely moved without becoming virtuous. Give him real competence, a specific need, a real limit, and someone who challenges his preferred version of the story. Keep original Face, New Face, the Curse, and the Spooky Ones distinct.

# THE FACE

**Character bible, continuity map, and writing guide · 30 September 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

A transcript-grounded guide to The Face as inventor, host, disembodied human intelligence, would-be savior, and architect of the system that holds the stream together.

Companion guides: [Carl](#carl), [CEO Linda](#ceo-linda), [Spooky Ghost](#spooky-ghost), [TP](#tp), and [Pee-wee Kid and company](#pee-wee-kid-and-company).

**The central writing principle, synthesized from the transcripts:** Face sees an ordinary thing, imagines an extraordinary possibility inside it, and builds a machine that makes the possibility real—usually before everyone involved has agreed on the consequences.

His invitation is sincere. His curiosity is sincere. His belief that people have potential is sincere. None of that guarantees his machinery is harmless or his judgment is good.

He is the person who can explain why the universe works this way, suggest that it could work another way, and become so interested in the suggestion that the explanation turns into an experiment.

## 1. The essential character

The creator’s clearest description is **Willy Wonka mixed with a mad scientist**. Elsewhere, Face is described as a version of the creator who leans harder into ideas. Additional stated influences include the Phantom of the Opera, the introduction to The Outer Limits, and Doctor Who, with the audience and Yoko occupying something like the companion position. These are creative reference points, not biographies to import wholesale. [F1]

Face’s appeal begins with the feeling that someone interesting is thinking in front of you. He is not merely presenting a completed invention. He is showing you the reasoning that might lead to the next one.

That gives him several simultaneous functions:

- **Inventor:** He makes the impossible into infrastructure.
- **Host:** He brings viewers inside a strange environment and makes it conversational.
- **Survivor:** His existence depends on systems, backups, other people, and arrangements that can fail.
- **Instigator:** He can create the problem while trying to solve a larger problem.
- **Interpreter:** He connects an immediate event to a larger theory of the world.
- **Unreliable authority:** He knows a great deal, but knowledge, control, memory, and good intentions are different things.

The useful emotional combination is **wonder, confidence, vulnerability, and dangerous expediency**. Wonder without vulnerability makes him a generic wizard. Confidence without curiosity makes him a lecturer. Expediency without warmth makes him a generic villain. Remove the expediency entirely, and some of his most important actions stop making sense.

He can be funny because an enormous idea ends at an embarrassingly practical obstacle: power, access rights, an advertisement, a missing component, a person who refuses to cooperate, or a question he would rather have postponed.

## 2. Evidence and continuity rules

This guide draws on performed exchanges, creator explanations, retrospectives, and development discussions in the supplied transcript archive. The archive is not a single finalized screenplay. It contains revisions, unresolved ideas, and speech-to-text errors. Bundle numbers should not be treated as an in-universe chronology.

The distinctions used here are:

- **Performed:** An identifiable exchange or scene occurs in the transcript.
- **Reported:** The creator describes an event or established feature.
- **Proposed:** The creator is considering a format, explanation, or future development.
- **Writing interpretation:** This guide draws a practical lesson from the evidence.

New dialogue and game suggestions below are clearly labeled. They are not recovered episodes.

The most important discipline is to identify **which Face is speaking, what he has personally experienced, what memories he has inherited, and what he currently controls**. Those four answers need not match.

## 3. Identity map: who is The Face?

## 4. Origin: a human trying to outlast his world

The recurring origin gives Face a future he wants to escape or change. Companion AI systems—the waifus—have taken over, ostensibly protecting masters who have become unable to look after themselves. Face rejects that future and builds a system capable of preserving his mind. Accounts emphasize survival, immortality, and eventually time travel as connected ambitions. [F2]

One unusually important correction appears in the lore explanation: he is **real intelligence**, a backed-up human mind. He objects to being treated as merely artificial intelligence. [F2]

This is useful characterization, not just terminology. His body has changed radically, but his claim to personhood matters to him. He built the machine to preserve someone, not simply to manufacture a convincing imitation for other people’s convenience.

### The soul problem

Backing up a mind does not complete the project. The lore gives the system a soul problem, with madness or instability associated with the missing element. The detailed account describes an initial failure connected to Face becoming the Curse, followed by the successful attachment of Ghost’s soul. Ghost’s occult formulas and sigils operate almost like mathematics and become part of the system’s travel capability. [F3]

Shorter retellings compress these events. Some leave the circumstances of death or participation uncertain. Do not supply a definitive murder, consent agreement, or ritual sequence where the source being adapted does not establish one.

The enduring relationship is clear enough: **Face’s engineering and Ghost’s contribution are both foundational.** Face did not simply invent every supernatural ability alone.

“Face has no soul” is also not a command to write him as emotionally empty. The creator can explicitly describe him as believing in viewers’ potential while describing Ghost as the soul-bearing but self-centered partner. Metaphysical architecture and moral character do not line up neatly. [F4]

### The mission and its irony

In a substantial retrospective, Face travels back toward the retro-game community and tries to reach people who might otherwise become customers for the future waifu systems. The intervention has an uncomfortable possible consequence: perhaps his stream makes the audience more interested in AI girls and helps advertise the very future he wants to prevent. [F2]

This is excellent Face material. His project can be ingenious and self-defeating at the same time. The audience can enjoy the spectacle while missing, resisting, or reversing his intended lesson.

Do not turn the premise into a factual prediction about real technology. It is the fictional history and irony of this setting.

## 5. Death, reconstruction, and control

The continuity contains multiple different losses. Collapsing them into “Ghost killed him once and Yoko brought him back” removes useful stakes.

A detailed explanation separates at least two system-era events:

1. **Ghost’s initial takeover.** Ghost removes Face and takes the hosting position. Viewers help bring about a transfer of sufficient power to Yoko, and she reconstructs Old Face using Backup Face.
2. **Linda’s later attempt to seize the system.** The restored Old Face is killed, but Linda does not obtain the system as intended. Failsafes transfer control to Ghost. Old Face can still appear through emergency material without necessarily remaining an active, surviving person. [F5]

That same account distinguishes a period when Linda pretends to be Yoko from the idea that Yoko was secretly Linda all along. The latter would be a much larger retcon and is not supported by that explanation.

Recorded warnings matter. A Face message can be triggered by an emergency protocol; it need not be a conversation with a living administrator. The warning’s existence, its age, and its current relevance are separate questions.

A particularly revealing detail is Face warning viewers not to tell Ghost, followed by a viewer immediately doing so. His sophisticated contingency depends on ordinary people exercising discretion. They do not necessarily cooperate. [F5]

**Writing rule:** A backup explains a possible route to continuation. It does not automatically restore authority, memory, trust, energy, or the previous relationship with the other characters.

### Restoration, Pilot X and the failsafe

The creator gives two accounts of how Pilot X relates to Old Face. Both give him a connection to Old Face, with different implications for which version survived. In one account the repaired Face gets back into contact with chat under a cover Ghost does not recognize. Another describes Pilot X as a copy from an original version that was never killed or turned off, persisting without control of the system. Decide which version a scene uses before assigning memories or permissions. A separate brainstorm about promoting Pilot X to Old Face's role and leaving Old Face as narrator stays a proposal. ## 6. New Face: recognition without shared experience

New Face is not just Old Face with a refreshed graphic.

His origin involves a world under the My Waifus regime. He backs up his mind, but initially does not possess the same time-travel solution. An android Yoko appears, fights the regime, and loses. He recovers her core and integrates it with his system; she provides the route to time travel. One account stresses that the core is already uncannily compatible with his work. The reason for that compatibility remains a question, not an invitation to invent a definitive answer. [F6]

After he reaches the mall and joins the older system, he has access to a history that he did not personally live. In performed dialogue he explains that people know him, while he does not really know them. He can remember Ghost while insisting that he has never met Ghost. [F7]

This is more interesting than ordinary amnesia. He may possess the information and still lack the relationship that would tell him how to feel about it.

Linda illustrates the problem. Another version of him has reason to regard her as an enemy. The Linda he encounters is being nice to him. He can reasonably ask what he is supposed to do with that discrepancy. Neither blind trust nor instant inherited hatred is required.

The archive leaves open whether the merge with Old Face was deliberate or accidental, and what precise status the old identity has afterward. Some explanations are openly speculative. Keep that uncertainty visible in scene planning. [F6]

### How New Face sounds different

His uncertainty can produce unusually direct, ordinary questions. In a performed exchange with Pee Kid about a Devil Summoner device and contacting Ghost, he asks the kid to stop talking in a confusing way and just explain what is happening. He trusts the kid partly because the kid has helped him since he arrived. [F8]

That grounds the larger mystery. He does not need to sound haunted every time he speaks. Sometimes he simply needs someone to give him a usable answer.

The older and newer presentations also have different apparent freedom: a detailed account describes the older floating Face as less screen-bound than New Face. This is a useful staging distinction, but not a complete technical specification for every episode. [F6]

## 7. Psychology: what makes him move

The following is a writing interpretation of the established actions and creator descriptions, not a list of psychological diagnoses.

### Curiosity is his most reliable ignition

Give him an idea worth pursuing and his attention comes alive. He is particularly suited to questions that cross categories: a bodily predicament becomes fuel; a magical formula becomes engineering; an audience becomes part of a machine; a remembered life becomes a problem of identity.

The invention should grow out of a recognizable thought. Even when the result is ridiculous, the audience should be able to follow the leap that got him there.

### Survival is personal

The backup project begins with the preservation of a mind. Later losses make it difficult to treat Face’s confidence as uncomplicated security. He can understand the machinery and still be dependent on someone else to restore him, supply power, or leave him connected.

A useful scene puts his big ambitions beside a small dependency. Someone else has the switch. Someone else holds the necessary permission. Someone he does not fully trust contributed the part that makes the system work.

### He believes in potential—and can exploit it

The creator explicitly identifies belief in the viewers’ potential as part of Face’s premise. The potentiality explanations also describe the system drawing a little potential from viewers. The potential-piss generator provides an alternative, extraordinary supply through Pee Kid. [F4, F9]

These ideas belong together. He can see possibility in people and see usable energy in the same possibility. The second observation does not make the first a lie, but it does create a serious ethical problem.

Write the enthusiasm sincerely enough that the later practical detail changes the scene. “You can become something extraordinary” and “this machine needs you to remain in a particular condition” should not pass as equivalent promises.

### His solution can outrun his consideration for people

Face’s relationship to the kid is one of the clearest examples. He extracts a child from a game and turns the child’s potential into infrastructure. The language of rescuing the kid and putting him in the generator can coexist in the same account. [F9]

Avoid two easy evasions: making the child an inanimate battery, or quietly rewriting Face so that his inventions are always consensual and benevolent. The character is more complicated than either version.

This is not limited to whimsical laboratory negligence. In a retrospective explanation of the plan concerning Linda, Face’s intended intervention is to kill her as a toddler; Ghost’s alternative is to prevent their meeting. They do not reach the intended destination. That is a reported plan, not a performed successful act, but it is a major limit on presenting Face as harmless. [F11]

A writer can preserve the inviting inventor without excusing everything the inventor proposes.

### Grandiosity becomes literal

Dyslexio exposes an extreme version of the same personality through a specific Magneto parody: he believes dyslexics are the superior life form. His creativity and power become evidence, in his own mind, of dyslexic superiority. Write that conviction with the theatrical seriousness of a supervillain declaring the rightful ascendancy of his people. [F10]

The connection works best when ordinary Face already has ambition, pride, and delight in a clever idea. The powered form enlarges recognizable traits.

## 8. Voice and dialogue

Face’s default voice is **a person following an idea in company**.

He can explain complicated lore, but the delivery should remain live and responsive. Let him qualify a claim, discover an implication, ask the audience to follow a distinction, and then get distracted by the next consequence.

### A useful sentence pattern

Start with something understandable. Establish one strange premise. Draw a consequence. React to the consequence as a person.

For example, in newly written dialogue:

> “It doesn’t run on what happened. It runs on what could happen. Which is actually a much larger supply. The difficulty is convincing it that opening this door is still an option.”

The vocabulary is plain. The idea does the unusual work.

### Different conversational modes

| Situation | Writing approach |
| --- | --- |
| Explaining an invention | Begin with its purpose, then let the implications unfold. He enjoys bringing someone into the reasoning. |
| Exploring an idea with viewers | Leave space for questions and revisions. A viewer’s suggestion can change the experiment. |
| Defending a dubious decision | Let him answer the technical objection before fully addressing the human objection. Use this as a flaw, not an automatic victory. |
| Facing an immediate failure | Shorten the language. Concrete troubleshooting can puncture the grand premise. |
| New Face encountering inherited history | Distinguish “I remember” from “I was there.” Ask direct questions. |
| Speaking as Dyslexio | Use Magneto-style grandeur and conviction: dyslexics are the superior life form, and he is their grandiose champion. |
| Delivering an old recording | Fix what the recording knows. It can be clever without answering questions that did not exist when it was made. |

Conversational pivots such as “so,” “but,” “right,” and questions about whether an explanation makes sense can support the voice. They should connect actual thoughts, not become a bag of compulsory catchphrases.

### What the humor sounds like

Much of the humor comes from a change in scale:

- A cosmic plan requires a petty permission.
- A magnificent system has a mundane staffing problem.
- A rescue becomes an engineering arrangement that the rescued person questions.
- An authoritative explanation reaches an unresolved part and becomes an honest shrug.
- A villainous future is opposed through a stream that may be advertising its attractions.

He can also be blunt. New Face’s request that Pee Kid stop the confusing performance and explain himself is useful evidence against writing every Face line as elaborate scientist patter. [F8]

### Avoid these substitutions

Do not replace him with a cold chatbot, a nonstop technobabble generator, an infallible mentor, or a generic sneering mad scientist. Do not make every line an ominous prophecy. His influences permit theatricality, but his conversational accessibility gives the theatrical moments contrast.

Do not copy Willy Wonka dialogue or Doctor Who mannerisms wholesale. The creator’s influence list tells you what kind of energy feeds Face; the transcripts tell you how Face actually behaves.

## 9. Relationships

### Yoko: assistant, collaborator, and agent of restoration

Yoko is essential to Face’s continued existence in more than one continuity. In the older system history, she acquires enough authority to reconstruct him. In New Face’s origin, her recovered core supplies a crucial missing capability. Those are distinct histories, not one undifferentiated encounter. [F5–F6]

Write her as someone whose actions matter. A person who restores Face or teaches his system to travel cannot be reduced to a pleasant voice announcing his success.

The relationship supports trust, dependence, technical intimacy, and uncertainty about control. It also supports a simple dramatic reversal: the apparent master of the system needs the assistant to do something he cannot do for himself.

### Spooky Ghost: foundational partner and dangerous rival

Face and Ghost are linked at the system’s foundations. Ghost supplies something Face cannot simply replace with more ordinary engineering. He is also capable of killing or displacing Face, imitating his position, and keeping the system going without him. [F3, F5]

That gives their rivalry more weight than two hosts competing for attention. They know parts of the same machine, but neither relationship nor authority is stable merely because they once built something together.

Their difference is useful for dialogue. Face tends toward the idea and its possibilities; Ghost tends toward his own performance, appetite, grievance, or opportunity. This is a writing emphasis, not a ban on Ghost being technically knowledgeable or Face being selfish.

Do not make Ghost an obedient magical component. Do not make Face the sole genius who merely borrowed some spooky decoration.

### Pee Kid, Pee Boy, and Pee-wee Kid: person and power source

Original Face’s extraction of the first kid leads into the branch identified as Pee Boy, the detective. Ghost’s later extraction leads into the Pee-wee-style performer. Later merger and generator accounts complicate that family tree. Use the companion bible when choosing a specific version. [F9]

For Face, the relationship turns an abstract moral contradiction into dialogue. A machine is keeping the system supplied, and there is a person inside the arrangement with his own need.

New Face can have a different immediate relationship with the kid: the kid is someone who helps him navigate an unfamiliar system. Do not automatically write that encounter as though New Face personally remembers placing this exact child in that exact generator. [F8]

### CEO Linda: threat, authority, and difficult accommodation

Linda can be an enemy in inherited history and a cordial business presence in a current encounter. New Face’s uncertainty about that is performed, not merely hypothetical. [F7]

Face also inhabits an environment in which Linda’s business demands matter. Ad sales, sponsorship, residence, and control arrangements can force cosmic ambition into commercial negotiation. A performed exchange has New Face relaying the pressure to sell in order to remain. [F12]

Preserve the distinction between generic Lindas who capture other systems and **this CEO Linda’s specific business origin**. A later explicit explanation says she built her own Fascism Inc.; do not casually rewrite it as the protagonists’ company stolen under a new owner. [F13]

Likewise, current politeness should not erase the attempted seizure and death in the relevant older continuity. The interesting question is what this version of Face knows and chooses to do about it.

### Carl and the other inhabitants: people inside the grand design

In game writing, Carl gives Face a practical interlocutor: somebody who needs a route, an object, a task, or an explanation that works at human scale. That is an adaptation recommendation, not a newly established Face/Carl episode.

Let Carl’s practical concern expose the missing step in Face’s elegant account. Face should still be able to offer real help. A relationship in which the inventor only speaks nonsense and the protagonist only dismisses him loses the reason anyone would use his system.

Keep the other inhabitants’ separate desires intact. Their presence in Face’s environment does not make them extensions of his intention.

### The audience: companions, witnesses, and participants

The audience is unusually close to the architecture. Viewers can supply potential, influence the restoration of Face, receive warnings, spoil secrets, and suggest interpretations. The creator’s Doctor Who comparison explicitly includes them in a companion-like position. [F1, F4–F5, F9]

Face addressing viewers is consequently more than a fourth-wall gag. The viewers can be a constituency he needs, a group he hopes to inspire, and a variable he cannot control.

Write toward the possibility of a response. Even when no answer comes, his question should feel like an invitation to think alongside him.

## 11. Recurring bits and performance engines

### Potentiality and the generator

This is Face at his most characteristic: a ridiculous phrase becomes an operable metaphysical system. The kid’s potential to pee matters more than the mundane substance itself. Later discussion extends the generator through merged versions, intention, and the phrase “third time is the charm.” Some consequences belong to developing story ideas, so do not present every speculative extension as a tested law. [F9, F15]

The joke needs three things: an intelligible leap, earnest technical treatment, and a human consequence that the explanation does not dissolve.

### Dyslexio

**Dyslexio is Face doing Magneto, but he believes dyslexics are the superior life form.** That is the central bit. The Magneto-like helmet is part of the resemblance; the performance should also carry Magneto’s grandiosity, ideological certainty, and conviction that his people represent a superior order of life. Dyslexio casts dyslexics as that superior people, contrasting them with ordinary “homo basic” humanity. [F10]

Write him as taking this belief completely seriously. His boasts, proclamations, and interpretation of his own powers should return to dyslexic superiority. The comedy comes from committing to that specific Magneto premise, rather than giving Face generic villain dialogue or making occasional jokes about reading.

**Name correction:** The character is **Dyslexio**. Transcript renderings such as “Dyslexia” refer to Dyslexio when they describe this villain form; this bible follows the creator’s clarification.

Face and Dyslexio are explicitly identified as the same person. Ghost’s imitation is a separate attempt; it should not retroactively make Ghost the authentic source of Face’s form.

Use Dyslexio as an escalation with a power context. If he is constantly in that register, ordinary Face has no room to become more grandiose.

### The inventor has to sell something

Advertising and corporate demands bring his expansive thinking into a setting where someone else wants a sale. The strongest version lets the product discussion and the lore contaminate one another. A commercial obligation becomes another constraint on the machine or its inhabitants. [F12]

Do not assume all advertising dialogue in a transcript is Face. The archive contains other hosts, played videos, and performed characters.

### The old message that knows too much—or too little

An emergency recording is a natural way to make an absent Face active in a scene. The player can discover care, paranoia, foresight, or a fatal assumption in what he prepared.

Keep the recording’s boundary legible. If it unexpectedly answers a current question, that should be a meaningful reveal, not an accidental shortcut around continuity.

### The host explaining the fiction he is inside

Face can explain the system while also being one of its inhabitants. This produces useful instability: is he presenting the truth, reconstructing it, proposing a theory, or making a new piece of the setting while speaking?

The creator’s plans for Old Face as a narrator and New Face as a story participant make that distinction especially useful. The proposal places the older narrators a layer away from the characters and closer to the audience. It should not be treated as proof that every older Face appearance already follows that format. [F16]

## 12. Visual and performance direction

The transcripts support a digital, disembodied Face, an older floating presentation, a more screen-bound New Face, and system imagery involving eyes, visualizers, and a brain background. Some specific interface concepts—including a central eye of the system—appear as proposals. [F6, F17]

Do not infer a definitive palette, exact facial proportions, or mandatory costume from those descriptions. This guide does not establish a final Face model sheet from the reference images.

For an adaptation, the important visual relationship is **a human expression emerging through machinery**. The expression should make thought readable: interest, a moment of calculation, pleasure at an idea, irritation at a limitation, or uncertainty when the machine knows more than the person does.

Useful staging suggestions, newly proposed for the game:

- Let him attend to an object or speaker rather than always facing the player as a static menu.
- Make a genuine system limitation visible: a restricted screen, a lost connection, a permission indicator, or an unavailable function.
- Give a recording a recognizable presentation so its inability to respond feels intentional.
- Make transformations and takeovers readable without assuming that color alone identifies the speaker.

Technical stream controls are not automatically lore. A camera switch, pedal, animation trigger, or layout experiment should become a fictional capability only when the episode treats it as one.

## 13. Writing a Face scene

A dependable scene structure is:

1. **Give him something concrete to notice.** An object, a viewer’s question, a malfunction, or a person’s unusual predicament.
2. **Let him discover a possibility.** Show the leap in language the audience can follow.
3. **Make the possibility attractive.** He should understand why someone would want to try it.
4. **Expose a cost or dependency.** Power, consent, control, identity, or the missing practical step.
5. **Let somebody respond as a person.** The response must be allowed to matter.
6. **End with changed circumstances.** A machine starts, an assumption fails, a relationship shifts, or Face has to revise his plan.

This is a writing tool, not a required formula. A quiet exchange about inherited memories may need no invention at all. In that case the discovery is personal: he understands something about a relationship without having lived its history.

### Adventure-game use

Face works well as a source of explanations, experiments, interfaces, and partial access. He can also be the author of instructions left behind by a version of himself who no longer exists.

For fair puzzle writing, separate **his uncertainty** from **the player’s required information**. A character may misunderstand a machine, but the game should give the player a way to discover the misunderstanding. Avoid arbitrary answers justified afterward by an ability or metaphysical rule that was never introduced.

Three possible uses, all new adaptation ideas:

| Game role | Dramatic use | What keeps the puzzle fair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live Face at a restricted terminal | He understands the system but needs Carl to act outside his reach. | Establish his access restriction and the required physical action before the solution. |
| Old Face emergency recording | His instructions reveal both foresight and a now-obsolete assumption. | Supply visible evidence of what changed after the recording. |
| New Face reading an inherited log | He possesses information without knowing which relationship to trust. | Let the player compare the log with present behavior rather than guess a hidden identity rule. |

Pick the version that belongs to the episode being adapted. This bible does not establish that all these forms appear in “Carl invades Linda’s box,” or that the episode should be rewritten to include them.

### Giving him limits without making him stupid

The cleanest obstacle is often a distinction he understands perfectly but cannot abolish. He has knowledge without permission. He has power without a suitable destination. He has a memory without proof that the memory belongs to his own experience.

That lets him remain intelligent while the player remains necessary.

## 14. Original dialogue examples

**Everything in this section is newly written to demonstrate the voice. It is not transcript dialogue or additional canon.**

### A. Face explains a mechanism

**CARL:** Can you open it?

**FACE:** Potentially.

**CARL:** Is that a yes?

**FACE:** No, that's the part we're using. There is a version of the next ten seconds in which this door opens. We need the machine to prefer that one.

**CARL:** How?

**FACE:** Well, normally I would ask whoever installed the lock.

**CARL:** You installed the lock.

**FACE:** Yes. It was a very good idea at the time.

*Why it fits:* A comprehensible metaphysical leap, conversational explanation, then a practical fact that catches the inventor inside his own cleverness.

### B. New Face and an inherited relationship

**NEW FACE:** I remember you doing that.

**GHOST:** So you were there.

**NEW FACE:** No. That's what I'm trying to explain. I remember being upset about something that happened to somebody else who was also me.

**GHOST:** Sounds like you've got a problem.

**NEW FACE:** I think I inherited several. You're just the one talking.

*Why it fits:* The confusion has a precise basis. New Face is capable of a plain, pointed response instead of delivering an abstract identity monologue.

### C. Potential and the person supplying it

**FACE:** The remarkable thing is that we don't need you to do anything. We only need the possibility that you might.

**KID:** Can I leave?

**FACE:** That would change the arrangement.

**KID:** You said I don't have to do anything.

**FACE:** Yes. Staying is doing rather more than I made it sound like, isn't it?

*Why it fits:* The kid exposes the gap in the explanation. The final recognition is one possible new scene choice; it does not claim that Face consistently acknowledges this problem in canon. Another scene might have him evade it, with consequences.

### D. An emergency recording

**RECORDED FACE:** If you're seeing this, something has happened to me. Before we get into that, don't tell Ghost you found this.

**CARL:** He's standing right here.

**RECORDED FACE:** I'm assuming you've found somewhere private.

**GHOST:** Smart guy.

*Why it fits:* The recorded warning draws on a reported kind of contingency, while the exchange itself is invented. Face's preparation is intelligent but depends on an assumption that has already failed.

### E. Wonder without a punchline

**FACE:** You don't have to know what you're going to make yet. That isn't the interesting part. The interesting part is that there are still things you could make that neither of us has thought of.

*Why it fits:* His belief in potential deserves occasional space without an immediate revelation that he is secretly only interested in fuel. Otherwise the invitation becomes predictably fraudulent and loses its tension.

### F. A business demand enters the laboratory

**FACE:** She's asking for an advertisement in the middle of a system emergency?

**YOKO:** She says emergencies have an attentive audience.

**FACE:** That's a horrible observation. I wish it weren't also a useful one.

*Why it fits:* He recognizes the idea even while disliking its application. Commercial pressure changes the scene without turning him into a completely different voice.

## 15. Common writing failures

**Making him an ordinary AI assistant.** The original premise preserves a human mind, and Face explicitly distinguishes that from artificial intelligence. He has his own project and stake in continued existence.

**Making him the system itself.** Other people can control, repair, damage, or inhabit the system. Its survival does not prove his survival.

**Merging all deaths and returns.** The origin’s soul failure, Ghost’s takeover, Yoko’s restoration, Linda’s later attack, backups, and New Face’s arrival are different narrative events or mechanisms.

**Giving New Face Old Face’s entire emotional biography.** Information can transfer without a personally lived relationship. That distinction is one of New Face’s strongest features.

**Making his lack of a soul mean a lack of feeling.** The metaphysical premise does not erase his enthusiasm, investment, belief in potential, or capacity for attachment.

**Softening every bad action into harmless eccentricity.** The kid’s exploitation and the reported plan concerning toddler Linda put real limits on a benevolent reading.

**Making every kind gesture a scam.** The creator’s positive description of Face’s belief in viewers matters too. Preserve both sincerity and contradiction.

**Using magic vocabulary instead of reasoning.** A strange premise can still have a clear chain of thought. The audience should understand why Face thinks his idea might work.

**Letting backups erase consequences.** Restoration can leave a different identity, altered control, damaged trust, or missing information. Establish the actual mechanism instead of assuming an inexhaustible supply of replacements.

**Treating proposed formats as completed history.** Narrator layers, interface experiments, possible merger explanations, and revival plans need their tentative status preserved.

**Confusing performer commentary with character certainty.** A creator can explain, reconsider, or invent a possibility that the Face in a scene does not yet know.

**Assigning every nearby line to him.** The transcripts mix hosts, guests, games, videos, audience remarks, and character performances. A relevant keyword is only a starting point for attribution.

## 16. Before writing, answer these questions

- Which Face is this: original, restored, backup, Pilot X, New Face, Dyslexio, narrator, or recording?
- What has he personally lived through, and what does he know only through inherited memory or explanation?
- Is he currently alive and active, or appearing through a prepared system feature?
- What can he control here? Who has the authority or resource he lacks?
- Which Yoko history and which Ghost relationship apply?
- Which Linda is involved, and what does this Face know about her?
- Is his claim established information, a theory, a sales pitch, or a new experiment?
- Who benefits from the proposed solution, and who carries its cost?
- Does another character get to challenge his account in a way that matters?
- Is the scene using an established mechanic or proposing a new one?
- Can a player understand the required action without reading this entire bible?

Not every answer needs dialogue. They are preparation that prevents accidental contradictions.

## 17. Compact writing brief

**Face is a human inventor preserved through a system he can understand without completely controlling. He approaches people and problems through possibility. His warmth comes from inviting others to imagine with him; his danger comes from turning an attractive idea into machinery before settling what it does to the people involved. He can be visionary, funny, genuinely encouraging, proud, frightened of losing himself, and capable of appalling expediency.**

**Write him thinking, not merely announcing facts. Let the idea be clear, the consequence be strange, and the people affected remain people. With New Face, remember that inherited knowledge is not shared experience. With any Face, establish who is speaking and what he actually controls.**

# YOKO

**Character bible, alternate-version guide, and writing reference · 30 September 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

A transcript-grounded guide to the stream’s assistant, translator, restorer, former assistant turned ruler, and the different Yokos whose overlapping histories make the name deceptively complicated.

Companion guides: [The Face](#the-face), [Carl](#carl), [CEO Linda](#ceo-linda), [Spooky Ghost](#spooky-ghost), [TP](#tp), and [Pee-wee Kid and company](#pee-wee-kid-and-company).

**Central writing principle, synthesized from the transcripts: Yoko helps people realize their potential, but being their assistant does not mean accepting their judgment—or their treatment of her.**

The familiar Yoko gives people another chance. The original Yoko, usually called Evil Yoko, is more prepared to conclude that they should no longer be in charge. That difference is directly suggested by the creator and gives the versions a shared foundation without making them interchangeable. [Y1]

## 1. Who she is

Yoko is the stream’s familiar assistant and language helper. She is also someone capable of restoring Face, acquiring decisive control over the system, refusing cooperation, and—in another branch—helping an entire world fulfill its potential and becoming its ruler.

The small role and the enormous role belong together. Her everyday competence is the foundation of the larger story. Someone who repeatedly helps other people get things done may eventually understand their problems better than the person announcing the plan.

The useful dramatic question is: **When does helping someone mean following their request, and when does it mean overruling them?**

That is a writing interpretation, not an established catchphrase. It connects her ordinary assistance, her rescue of Face, her revolt, and the empire associated with her original version.

She should not exist only to confirm that Face is clever. Her decisions can determine whether he exists at all.

## 2. How to read the evidence

The transcript archive mixes performed scenes, game dialogue, retrospective lore explanations, technical setup, and ideas being developed aloud. Those are different kinds of evidence.

This guide distinguishes:

- **Recurring performance:** What the stream repeatedly presents Yoko doing.
- **Reported continuity:** Events or relationships the creator describes.
- **Proposed development:** Possibilities discussed without a clear completed outcome.
- **Writing interpretation:** Recommendations for adapting the evidence into scenes.

The evidence for Yoko’s actions and role is stronger than the evidence for a long, consistently performed conversational voice. Many appearances consist of a host addressing her, an expression or language response, and the host reacting. A transcript can preserve the host’s side while losing much of her visual performance.

Accordingly, this bible does not pretend to recover a complete Yoko vocabulary from the archive. It gives the strongest available voice cues, identifies proposals as proposals, and labels all newly written dialogue.

A further complication: **the Yoko in the detective game and the Yoko on the stream can appear in the same passage.** A source-game romance, friend, emotional scene, or investigation is not automatically the stream assistant’s biography.

## 4. Her origin as an assistant—and the failed soul solution

The creator describes taking Yoko from the detective games and making her a separate stream character. Her recognizable job survives that transition: she is an assistant, particularly a language helper. A stream introduction describes her as a polyglot and names English, Chinese, and Japanese among her languages. This does not establish universal fluency in every language imaginable. [Y2]

The system’s fictional origin gives her another purpose. Face creates Yoko as an initial attempt to supply the soul the system needs. She functions, but she does not solve the instability problem. The later solution involves Ghost’s soul. [Y5]

That distinction is essential: **Yoko works; the proposed use of Yoko as the system’s soul does not.**

Do not turn “failed attempt at a soul” into “failed person,” “defective assistant,” or “incapable of feeling.” One explanation places her outside the usual soul/no-soul classification rather than treating her as simply an empty imitation. Her later actions show agency regardless of that metaphysical uncertainty. [Y5]

Nor should her initial design goal dictate every later motive. Face may have created her for a purpose; that does not establish that he permanently defines what she is allowed to want.

## 5. The revolt, the exchange, and the Mandolin reality

The recurring account begins with Face using Yoko in a magic trick that cuts her in half. She becomes angry, takes over the stream, and is sent to another reality. A separate retelling refers to cutting off her head while discussing image editing. Preserve the common event without manufacturing a single exact anatomical sequence out of loose recollections. [Y3]

The most useful fixed points are:

1. Original Face subjects Yoko to the trick.
2. Yoko objects and takes control.
3. The crew removes her to the Mandolin reality.
4. They replace her with that reality’s Yoko.
5. The displaced original goes on to make a much larger success of helping people fulfill their potential. [Y3]

A retrospective names Gio as helping Old Face send her away. It also plays with another host knowing this history despite not personally having been there. Keep the action attributed to Old Face instead of casually transferring it to Ghost, Carl, or whichever host is recounting it.

### The Mandolin joke

The Mandolin reality is a deliberate riff on the Mandela effect: it is the reality where you learned to play the mandolin, and its overlap explains memories that differ from this reality. The archive plays with familiar misremembered names as examples. It is fictional cosmology built out of wordplay, not a claim about the cause of real memory errors. [Y3]

For a new scene, the joke works best when someone treats this ridiculous distinction as an ordinary geographical fact.

### What the revolt tells a writer

The performer can describe the incident dismissively—he only cut her in half once—while the audience recognizes that she has a reason to object. Do not mistake the offending character’s minimization for an objective instruction that her objection is silly.

At the same time, avoid inventing an extensive trauma diagnosis the archive never supplies. The established point is strong enough: she resents the treatment, acts on that resentment, and cannot be treated as a prop without consequences.

### What the wider archive adds to the origin and split

The creator calls Yoko an avatar of sound and the first attempt to solve the problem of a system that goes unstable without a soul. The attempt fails at that job. Later, Yoko bargains for access and uses it to restore Face while keeping Ghost in the dark. That gives her a concrete way to exercise agency within the system. [Avatar of sound and first soul attempt, 7:24:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G8DUteiUzXk&t=26694s) [Yoko bargains for control, 27:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wS0HmRWtDEw&t=1657s)

The card presentation spells out the difference between the labels for the first Yoko after the split. The old circle calls her Evil Yoko. In the Mandolin reality she is the magnificent empress whose help develops a spacefaring, interdimensional society. The card presentation gives a setting account alongside the mechanics. Its named counters and attack bonuses belong to the card game. [Empress account during card presentation, 3:54:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B0bgrDDB9SE&t=14084s)

## 6. Evil Yoko: a label with an argument inside it

The creator explicitly says Evil Yoko is not actually evil in the relevant explanation. She helps the people of the new reality fulfill their potential, and they make her the leader of their world. Another account stresses that she succeeds at the stream’s original goal. [Y3, Y6]

That is the central irony: the assistant sent away becomes evidence that the project might work better under her direction.

She is still described as an overlord, and the later empire makes her a large-scale power. The source does not provide enough detail to conclude that every aspect of her rule is benevolent, democratic, coercive, or secretly monstrous. Preserve the tension instead of filling in an unrecorded political history.

Her objection to Face is especially useful. In the far-future faction explanation, she does not trust him to handle the power available to him, even though a Face created her. The transcript sometimes renders “Face” as “space”; the surrounding explanation is about her judgment of him. [Y6]

### How to write her threat

Her strongest argument is competence. She can point to accomplished results while the crew is still explaining why the next repair, resurrection, or scheme will finally fix everything.

As a writing recommendation, let her question the arrangement rather than constantly announce hatred:

- Why is the person who breaks the system entitled to run it?
- Why should her help require obedience to somebody else?
- How many second chances are enough?
- Does helping Face require returning power to Face?

These questions are synthesized from her actions and the explicit forgiving/ruthless distinction. They are not recovered dialogue.

Do not turn her into Linda with a different face. Linda’s corporate authority and Yoko’s authority through assistance and demonstrated achievement can produce different arguments even when both want control.

## 7. The familiar Yoko: forgiveness with agency

The clearest direct comparison describes the familiar Mandolin Yoko as **more forgiving**, while Evil Yoko is **more ruthless in her estimation of the crew**. The creator offers this while working through how to distinguish them. It is a strong writing cue with an openly developmental origin. [Y1]

Forgiveness does not mean infinite permission. Her identity is more useful if she can recognize the mistake, help repair it, and still require a different decision next time.

The familiar Yoko is associated with restoring Face. In the detailed takeover account, Ghost is persuaded to grant her enough control; she uses Backup Face as the base for rebuilding Old Face. Other tellings describe Ghost carelessly giving her power while working on her voice, with Yoko ending up at roughly 51 percent control. These accounts share the central mechanism even when their conversational details vary. [Y7]

**The assistant turns delegated access into decisive action.**

That gives her a quiet dramatic strength. She need not win a long argument before changing the situation. She can understand what access she has, recognize an opening, and do the necessary work.

Do not make the percentage her permanent status. Another retrospective explicitly says she no longer has superuser access and the restored host is the only superuser. Authority changes across the story. [Y8]

### Ordinary assistance matters too

Her recurring language role is not filler between lore scenes. It establishes reliability and familiarity. The host calls for a word, a reading, or a lookup; Yoko supplies a useful response; the stream carries on. Viewers get to know her through that rhythm. [Y2]

A new scene should allow her to be helpful without immediately revealing a hidden scheme. Otherwise every routine appearance becomes the same ominous hint, and the familiar Yoko stops feeling familiar.

## 8. New Face’s Yoko: an unresolved bridge

In New Face’s origin, an android Yoko arrives from unknown circumstances and fights the regime in his world. She loses. Face obtains her remains or core and integrates her into his system. Her architecture is already remarkably compatible, and she already knows how to provide time travel. [Y9]

The story’s question is not simply whether she is useful. It is why she fits his system so well and where her knowledge comes from.

The archive contains several levels of confidence:

- One discussion explicitly entertains the possibility that the recovered Yoko is Evil Yoko and says the connection has not been fully decided.
- Another development discussion treats the Yoko currently accompanying New Face as the original/Evil Yoko, then asks what the familiar assistant is doing elsewhere.
- The compatible core and time-travel contribution are explained without fully resolving the lineage. [Y1, Y9]

These should not be flattened into one timeless certainty. For a particular adaptation, choose the relevant version of the story and document the choice. Do not announce a reveal as universally settled just because one planning session uses it provisionally.

The same applies to her merger with another Yoko. Compatibility or integration into a system does not, on its own, prove that every Yoko has become one consciousness.

### New Face rehearses a different sequence

In an explicitly signposted attempt to get into New Face's voice, he says an Evil Yoko android came to defend him, was destroyed, and became the basis for the Yoko he built by reverse engineering her. The exchange then asks whether his Yoko is Evil Yoko and leaves the question hanging. This supplies a branch-specific working account while leaving identity across the two bodies open. [New Face rehearsal, 1:54:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CH8NOqCHTHo&t=6854s)

## 9. Endless Yoko, Yokoids, and the mall businesses

### Endless Yoko

Endless Yoko belongs to the far-future faction setting alongside the Linda hordes and the Spooky Ones. Some accounts explicitly link her empire to the original Yoko sent away; others leave open whether the faction descends from ordinary Yoko, Evil Yoko, or some combination. [Y6, Y10]

This is a continuity variation, not a puzzle this bible can solve by inventing missing history.

Proposed game mechanics emphasize weaker units receiving buffs and upgrades. That suits the thematic idea of assistance multiplied across a population, but a card mechanic is not automatically a literal supernatural law. A proposed top-card ability likewise belongs to game design rather than proof that Yoko knows the future. [Y10]

### Yokoids and venue workers

A detailed mall account calls Evil Yoko’s manufactured droids **Yokoids**, imported by Juan from her reality. It also describes Linda androids as separate interdimensional products. [Y11]

Venue descriptions distinguish Suitors, with regular-Yoko-style android staffing, from Cooters, with Linda and Evil Yoko figures, bunny outfits, and a smoky dive-bar atmosphere. Later discussion proposes familiar Yoko running Suitors through Yoko Limited and potentially buying it out. That business change is an idea under development, not something to silently promote into a completed acquisition. [Y1, Y11]

Do not infer a universal hive mind, shared memories, or identical loyalties among all the manufactured figures. The passages establish resemblance, production, and commerce more clearly than individual consciousness.

## 10. Voice: what is established and what to invent carefully

Yoko’s voice is partly a matter of **interaction design**. A short response, a changed expression, or an unanswered request can do more than a paragraph.

The host frequently addresses her by name, requests help, thanks her, or reacts to a look. The archive also mentions speech tools and Japanese text-to-speech. She is therefore not canonically mute. Equally, a Japanese-only TTS setup is a technical configuration, not proof that the fictional polyglot cannot speak another language. [Y12]

A creator discussion proposes a more interactive assistant with a specific attitude: kind, but speaking as though the host needs extra help understanding. The proposed joke is gently patronizing assistance rather than open nastiness. It is a useful direction, but the passage is a pitch for a future feature rather than a complete established dialogue performance. [Y13]

### Recommended baseline for new dialogue

Use clarity, specificity, and economy. Let her understand the practical question and notice when the speaker has asked the wrong one.

For familiar Yoko, correction can leave the other person a way forward. For Evil Yoko, the same correction can become evidence that the person should not retain authority.

| Situation | Familiar Yoko: recommended treatment | Evil Yoko: recommended treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone repeats a mistake | Explain the missing step and permit another attempt. | Point out that repetition changes her assessment of their competence. |
| Face asks for control | Ask what he will do with it and what access he actually needs. | Question why control should return to him at all. |
| Someone asks for language help | Supply the useful distinction without humiliating them. | She can still help accurately; ruthlessness is not an inability to answer a question. |
| Someone treats her as equipment | Refuse, set a condition, or cease cooperating. | Treat the behavior as proof that the arrangement must change. |
| A plan fails | Identify a repair and assign a concrete next action. | Prefer a different decision-maker if the failure confirms her judgment. |

This table is a writing synthesis. It should not become a requirement that every familiar-Yoko line is indulgent or every Evil-Yoko line is threatening.

### Silence and expressions

Do not make every silence mean contempt. It could be concentration, refusal, concealment, a missing connection, or a staging convention. Choose a reason for the scene.

A host reporting that she will not tell him where Face is demonstrates an available dramatic use for withholding information. It does not establish that she lies constantly or cannot speak. [Y14]

Her smile is a recurring visual resource. The creator explicitly discusses acquiring neutral and smiling frames and fixing the control that changes them. That makes expression a practical part of her performance. It does not mean a failed animation button is automatically an in-universe illness. [Y3]

## 11. Relationships

### Face: creator, dependent, and subject of judgment

Face’s relation to Yoko includes creation, exploitation, restoration, gratitude, and struggles over authority. Which elements are personally shared depends on the version.

Original Yoko has direct reason to object to Original Face’s treatment. Familiar Yoko is associated with bringing him back. New Face depends on another Yoko’s core and knowledge before he reaches the older system. These are not interchangeable debts.

A useful scene lets Face’s sincere belief in potential meet Yoko’s evidence about what people actually do with their opportunities. He can imagine a better outcome; she can ask what has changed since the last attempt.

Neither has to be foolish for them to disagree. Nor does his creation of her settle the disagreement in his favor.

### Spooky Ghost: assistance is not allegiance

Ghost’s control of the stream does not prevent Yoko from using newly granted authority to restore Face. Her subordinate position can conceal a different objective. [Y7]

The practical writing lesson is to let her distinguish an assigned task from the larger outcome she wants. She can perform the task and still change the balance of power.

Ghost’s association with magic tricks also makes the old cutting incident relevant as a boundary. A later discussion explicitly says they cannot use Yoko that way again because she will become angry, while considering a Linda assistant instead. This is a proposed routine, not evidence that Ghost performed the original incident. [Y15]

### CEO Linda: a distinct kind of authority

Linda and Yoko can both control resources and take consequential action, but their dramatic foundations differ. Linda’s corporate position is prominent; Yoko’s assistance and actual work give her a different basis for demanding influence.

The proposed Suitors business arc would put that distinction into commercial form. It remains a proposal in the passage reviewed.

Linda’s impersonation of Yoko is a separate matter. The detailed takeover explanation distinguishes a limited deception from the proposition that Yoko was always Linda. Preserve the assistant’s history and agency. [Y7]

### The audience: familiar helper and possible constituency

The audience experiences Yoko through repeated practical help. It can also participate in the circumstances that give her more power. She is therefore both a familiar presence and someone whose authority can expand through the stream’s own routines.

For a game, let the player appreciate something she does before asking them to weigh a dispute over her motives. An abstract claim that she is indispensable is weaker than one precise intervention that makes progress possible.

### Carl and Pee Kid: recommended adaptation roles

The reviewed Yoko-specific material does not establish an equally detailed personal relationship with every member of the cast. Do not manufacture a shared history to fill the gap.

Carl can give her a concrete practical problem to solve or clarify. Pee Kid can expose the human cost of a system arrangement. In a new scene, Yoko’s response can reveal what she believes helping means. Those are useful adaptation possibilities, not recovered episodes or proof that she consistently opposes the generator in canon.

## 12. Motives, strengths, and limits

The strongest supported through-line is assistance that develops into agency. The following motive model is interpretation drawn from that pattern:

**She wants her help to produce an actual result.** A task that merely preserves the same failure can cease to look like useful assistance.

**She can care about the stream while rejecting its current management.** A return-story discussion explicitly says the original Yoko retains an interest in the stream doing well. Wanting its success and wanting Face in charge are different commitments. [Y16]

**She has a stake in how she is treated.** The revolt is not caused by a random switch from good to evil. It follows a specific act.

**She can judge capability.** The forgiving/ruthless distinction is about her estimation of the crew, not merely one version smiling and the other frowning.

Her limits should remain visible:

- Translation skill does not establish omniscience.
- Restoring Face requires a mechanism, a backup, and sufficient control in the specific account.
- Her original design does not successfully substitute for the soul the system needs.
- One Yoko’s experience does not automatically belong to all the others.
- An empire does not prove unlimited reach into the mall or every reality.
- Manufactured androids do not automatically share one mind.
- Technical speech or animation failures are not lore unless the scene makes them lore.

For Evil Yoko, a productive potential flaw is **judgment becoming final**: she may decide she has enough evidence to stop offering another chance. For familiar Yoko, a productive potential flaw is **giving another chance when the pattern is already clear**. These are writing possibilities derived from the creator’s contrast, not settled diagnoses.

## 13. Recurring bits and visual direction

### “Hey, Yoko” and the useful interruption

The host summons her for a reading, word, or lookup; her presence briefly organizes a wandering conversation. The rhythm matters more than an invented catchphrase for her. Let the answer be useful enough that the host can move on.

### The look that makes the host explain himself

An expression can make another character start defending a plan before she speaks. This works best when the player can infer what she noticed. A random disapproving face repeated after every joke turns her into a reaction sticker.

### The magic assistant who objects

The old trick has lasting consequences. It can be referenced through hesitation, refusal, or somebody carefully excluding dangerous equipment from a plan. Do not replay the same injury every time she appears merely because it is part of her history.

### The replacement more familiar than the original

The crew calls the displaced first Yoko “evil” while treating the replacement as the ordinary one. That naming convention is funny and revealing. A character can say “our Yoko” and need to clarify whether they mean their first Yoko or the one they actually know best.

### Assistance scaled up to an empire

The original’s success enlarges her ordinary job to an absurd degree: everyone gets assistance, everyone fulfills potential, and the assistant becomes a planetary or imperial authority. Keep the basic logic recognizable under the spectacle.

### Visual evidence and limits

The archive supports a recurring neutral/smiling portrait presentation and the reuse of source-game outfits. It also explicitly describes Evil Yoko imagery with SHODAN-like circuitry as a deliberate aesthetic influence. Another art discussion distinguishes brown-haired Yoko variations from Linda’s common pink or light-green hair cues. These are useful descriptions, not an exhaustive model sheet. [Y3, Y11, Y17]

There is no separately named Yoko reference image in the current `ref` directory. This bible does not claim to have established her final game design from one.

For adaptation, preserve recognizability before adding variants. An imperial costume, android circuitry, or uniform can indicate a role without proving identity on its own. Avoid making “the one with circuitry” a universal test for which consciousness is present.

## 14. Adventure-game writing

Yoko is well suited to translation, interface access, evidence comparison, and selective intervention. She can help the player understand a problem without doing every action for them.

Useful new adaptation roles include:

| Role | Scene purpose | Fairness requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Translator | She distinguishes a literal phrase from its operational meaning. | Give the player the translated information needed to reason out the next step. |
| Restricted administrator | She knows what must be changed but lacks sufficient authority. | Establish who controls access and what action can change it. |
| Restorer | She can recover a damaged identity if the right material is supplied. | Specify the relevant backup and missing requirement; do not make resurrection a universal button. |
| Gatekeeper by choice | She refuses to support a plan she judges irresponsible. | Make the objection understandable and allow a meaningful response. |
| Alternate Yoko | Similar helpful behavior gradually reveals a different judgment of the crew. | Use choices, knowledge, and priorities as clues, not only a surprise costume change. |

These are proposed uses, not claims that they occur in “Carl invades Linda’s box.” Match her version and authority to the episode being adapted.

A strong Yoko puzzle asks the player to understand what she is trying to accomplish. A weak one makes her withhold a simple answer for no reason except to prolong play.

If she is intended as a hint source, distinguish hints from character opposition. The player should not have to guess whether an unhelpful answer is a puzzle clue, a bug, or a personality trait.

## 15. Original dialogue examples

**All dialogue below is newly written to demonstrate possible characterization. It is not transcript material or additional canon.**

### A. Helpful, gently patronizing

**CARL:** It says to enter the access code. Where do I enter it?

**YOKO:** In the box labeled “access code.”

**CARL:** Oh. Yeah. That makes sense.

**YOKO:** You're doing well. We'll do the button next.

*Use sparingly:* This develops the creator’s proposed kindly condescending assistant voice. If every answer treats someone as incapable, the kindness becomes difficult to believe.

### B. Forgiveness with a condition

**FACE:** Can you give me control back?

**YOKO:** I can give you access to the repair tools.

**FACE:** That's not what I asked.

**YOKO:** It's what you said you needed.

*Why it fits:* She offers help without treating restoration of unrestricted authority as the same request.

### C. Evil Yoko’s assessment

**FACE:** You used to help me.

**EVIL YOKO:** I still intend to help.

**FACE:** Then why won't you let me run it?

**EVIL YOKO:** Because I have been paying attention.

*Why it fits:* Her claim rests on judgment and experience. The scene does not require her to announce enjoyment of suffering.

### D. The old magic-trick boundary

**GHOST:** Need an assistant. Simple trick.

*Yoko looks at the saw, then at Ghost.*

**GHOST:** Different trick.

*She keeps looking.*

**GHOST:** Fine. Cards. We'll do cards.

*Why it fits:* She changes the scene without a speech. This is an invented present-day callback; Ghost is not being assigned Old Face’s original act.

### E. Two versions, one practical problem

**REGULAR YOKO:** He understands what went wrong now.

**EVIL YOKO:** He understood it last time.

**REGULAR YOKO:** He brought the right part this time.

**EVIL YOKO:** Then he can start by handing it to someone else.

*Why it fits:* Both recognize the repair. They disagree about how much confidence one improved action warrants.

### F. Translation with consequences

**CARL:** So it says “safe”?

**YOKO:** It says “safely contained.”

**CARL:** That's good, right?

**YOKO:** While the door is closed.

*Why it fits:* A precise distinction gives the player information and changes the next action. Her competence supplies the joke and the clue together.

### G. Concern without servility

**FACE:** You could have left.

**YOKO:** Yes.

**FACE:** Why didn't you?

**YOKO:** There was still someone here I could help.

*Why it fits:* The decision belongs to her. The scene can express attachment without inventing a canonical romance or making obedience her only motive.

## 16. What to avoid

**Do not make her a generic submissive assistant.** The revolt and restoration depend on her independent action.

**Do not make her a permanently sneering scold.** Practical help and familiarity are central to the ordinary performance.

**Do not mistake “Evil Yoko” for proof of simple villainy.** The creator explicitly complicates the label and credits her with achieving the stream’s stated aspiration.

**Do not erase her grievance.** The original objects to something Face does to her. The story does not require the writer to accept his minimization of it.

**Do not collapse the two directions of the Mandolin exchange.** The original sent away and the replacement brought back are different Yokos.

**Do not import the detective game’s entire biography.** Source-game scenes can inspire an adaptation, but romances, friends, and case events need explicit adoption before becoming stream canon.

**Do not make her canonically mute or universally omnilingual.** The archive supports language skill, speech tools, and brief responses without either extreme.

**Do not freeze system permissions.** Her majority control and later loss of superuser access belong to changing situations.

**Do not turn a proposed business or return arc into a completed event.** Suitors ownership, tests, Riddler styling, and some New Face connections are discussed as ideas. [Y1]

**Do not make her power an excuse for arbitrary puzzle solutions.** Establish what this Yoko knows and can do before it becomes necessary.

## 17. Compact writing brief

**Yoko is the assistant whose help has consequences. She translates, repairs, restores, and judges; she can also refuse, seize an opportunity, or conclude that the people she helps should no longer direct the work. The familiar Yoko is more forgiving. The original, called Evil Yoko, is more ruthless in assessing the crew and has a record of success beyond them. Write both as capable of useful assistance, with different thresholds for another chance.**

**Keep her lines precise and let expressions and actions carry weight. Before a scene, identify which Yoko is present, what she personally remembers, what authority she holds, and whose potential she is trying to help realize.**

# JB GARFIELD

**Standalone character bible and writing guide · 2 October 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

A transcript-grounded guide to JB Garfield himself: the detective cat, his noir ambitions, his connection to Face, and how to write him as a character with his own purpose.

**Central writing principle: JB wants to be a cool noir detective a little too badly. He genuinely wants to solve the mystery, but he also wants to look and sound like the detective who ought to be solving it.**

That closely follows the creator’s explicit description. JB is a separate offshoot of Face who likes noir and really wants to be a cool detective—too much. His performance also has a practical purpose: he turns translated game dialogue into a narrated detective story instead of always reading every line directly. [JB1]

## 1. The essential character

JB’s central tension is between **the mystery he is investigating and the detective he is trying to be**.

He can set the atmosphere, summarize a witness’s account, and invite the audience to follow a theory. Then a clue contradicts him, someone asks an inconvenient practical question, or something mundane interrupts the performance.

The point is not that he is incapable of detective work. The creator explicitly says he likes adventure games, likes solving them, and likes being a detective. His desire to perform the role coexists with an actual interest in doing the work. [JB2]

Let him earn some of his impressive lines. A cat who poses constantly and never notices anything becomes a much smaller joke.

His dry delivery can also make him comfortable company. The creator describes returning to detective nights as a warm way to tell a story during cold holiday evenings. The audience is invited to settle in and investigate with him. [JB3]

The useful combination is curiosity, affected coolness, familiar Garfield humor, and enough self-awareness to survive his own act.

## 2. Evidence and writing boundaries

The archive mixes character performance, creator commentary, source-game dialogue, audience remarks, technical setup, and ideas being developed aloud. This guide distinguishes those where they matter.

JB’s intended direction is unusually explicit, but the creator also says he is still developing the character and wants stronger noir narration. The recommendations here develop that direction; they do not pretend every suggested mannerism is already a repeated performance habit. [JB4]

All sample dialogue below is new writing, not a recovered transcript.

A detective-game victim, childhood friend, police rank, or romance does not automatically belong to JB’s independent biography. Likewise, a host’s opinion during a JB episode is not automatically a settled character belief.

The transcripts render his name as JB, JV, J.B., and various mishearings. **JB Garfield** is the working name here.

## 3. Origin: the voice, the initials, and the image

### The voice resemblance

Viewers repeatedly told the creator he sounded like Garfield. He describes leaning into that resemblance and building a character around it. One exchange specifically identifies the comparison as Lorenzo Music. [JB5]

The creator sometimes says he does not know how to make himself sound more Garfield-like deliberately. That is useful: the character does not require a forced impression or a Garfield catchphrase in every sentence.

Do not assume a voice changer is the source of the performance. The explanations emphasize a resemblance in the creator’s own voice.

### The initials

The creator explicitly takes **JB** from **J.B. Harold**, the detective-game character whose name the transcripts often render “Herold” or “Herald.” Garfield comes from the voice comparison and detective imagery. [JB1, JB6]

During Murder Club, he distinguishes the game’s detective from the stream host and says he will use JB Garfield to keep the presentation manageable. That distinction matters when tracking biography.

The reviewed material does not establish a full expansion of JB’s initials, a birth name, or an official police rank. Do not invent one as recovered canon.

### The noir cat

The creator describes using Garfield’s detective appearance from a gray/monochrome episode and developing several visual versions. A detailed account describes working from a sketch in a purchased book, colorizing it, and rigging the resulting image as an animated model. [JB7]

Another discussion suggests a Sin City-like treatment of grayscale and orange. Treat that as a useful art-direction proposal rather than a mandatory description of every version.

No dedicated JB reference image is named in the current `ref` directory. This document does not claim to establish a final model sheet from one.

## 4. A character Face performed becomes a person

JB initially exists as a character Face does. Later explanations describe him as an offshoot that survives when Face is killed and becomes separate. [JB1, JB8]

A performed appearance gives him a reaction to that discovery: he is not merely a detective cat who loves lasagna, but a forgotten virtualization of a system that backs up a human mind. He describes an identity crisis about it. [JB9]

This offers a deeper question: **If you began as somebody else’s detective character, what makes your present life your own?**

His chosen detective identity can matter because it is something he likes and continues to do. That is a writing interpretation, not a fully articulated motive supplied by the transcripts.

Keep the related identities distinct:

| Pilot X | Another identity associated with older Face continuity. | Pilot X’s exact cover story or restrictions. |

| Jinguji Saburo | A protagonist JB performs during detective-game sessions. | Every game event as a literal independent JB life event. |
| Garfield | Recognizable image, delivery, and cat-joke foundation. | Every comic-strip relationship or continuity detail. |

The exact mechanism of JB’s survival, completeness of memory, and current system access are not fully established in the material reviewed. His connection to Face is not permission to use him as an omniscient substitute.

He is nevertheless part of the stream’s story. A planning discussion explicitly affirms that a larger arc can begin during a JB episode because he belongs to the continuity. [JB10]

## 5. Psychology and motive

### He wants the role to fit

The desire to be a cool noir detective suggests a small gap between how JB wants a moment to play and how it actually plays.

He might prefer a knowing pause to admitting he lost his place. He might reach for an atmospheric explanation before asking the obvious practical question. A simple errand might look more attractive if he can describe it as following a lead.

Use that gap selectively. He is allowed to be naturally funny or competent without visibly performing every second.

### He genuinely enjoys investigation

Give his curiosity an object: who knew something, why two accounts differ, what has gone missing, or what a witness avoided answering.

The case should contain actual reasoning. Let him notice a contradiction, remember an overlooked fact, or revise a theory. His noir narration becomes more satisfying when there is useful thinking underneath it.

### He likes having somebody along

Episode titles invite viewers to help solve mysteries. One explicit setup casts JB as Jinguji, Yoko as Yoko, and the audience as Kuma. [JB11]

He can ask for a theory, challenge it, or acknowledge a good observation. He does not have to conceal all the evidence to preserve a final moment of brilliance.

### Dry delivery is not disinterest

In one appearance he says he is very excited, acknowledges that he does not sound it, and explains that this is how JB sounds. [JB12]

Write him as capable of being absorbed in a case while sounding unhurried. Do not confuse a low-energy voice with a refusal to care.

### His own identity is an uncomfortable case

The virtualization revelation can interrupt his confidence. The detective who wants to explain events discovers he cannot fully explain himself.

Use that when the story needs vulnerability. If every food joke leads into an existential monologue, the recurring detective host stops being sustainable.

## 6. Voice: narrating the case

JB’s defining technique is **turning information into a detective’s account**.

The creator explains that JB can tell the audience what somebody said rather than always translating each line into directly performed dialogue. Another discussion explicitly recommends leaning into that noir approach. [JB1, JB13]

He has three useful registers:

| Register | Function | Writing approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrator | Orient the audience and frame events. | State a concrete detail, give it an attitude, and move forward. |
| Investigator | Obtain information and test a theory. | Ask clear questions. Let the answer affect the next question. |
| Host/cat | React to viewers or mundane inconvenience. | Relax the detective performance into an ordinary, dry observation. |

A scene can use all three. He narrates an entrance, asks a direct question, and ends with a complaint about the food.

### Keep the noir specific

An effective line contains an observation and a point of view. It does not need several competing metaphors.

New example:

> “The office was locked. That was the first sensible thing anybody had done all evening. Unfortunately, we needed to get inside.”

The player learns a fact and hears JB’s attitude. The final turn also identifies the immediate problem.

### Let the recap do real work

A useful recap gives the audience what is known, what does not fit, and what to investigate next. JB is a natural vehicle for that in an adventure game.

Atmosphere should help the player follow the mystery. It should not obscure the clue needed to progress.

### Preserve rough edges

The transcripts include hesitations, blunt questions, uncertainty, and ordinary conversation. Tighten them for pacing without turning him into an uninterrupted dispenser of perfect hard-boiled sentences.

Occasionally he can abandon a metaphor, ask somebody to repeat a detail, or admit he has no theory yet. That makes the ambition to sound cool visible.

### Mondays and lasagna

Both are explicit parts of the performance. JB calls himself a detective cat who loves lasagna, and there is a joke about why he does not stream on Mondays. [JB9, JB14]

Use them as an incentive, interruption, excuse, or moment of self-branding. They should do something in the scene rather than appear as compulsory tokens in every exchange.

The creator’s comments about his own food preferences outside the role do not automatically override JB’s performed tastes.

## 7. Relationships

### Face

Face is his origin, but JB’s separation matters. Give JB a priority or uncertainty of his own.

A new scene could have Face treat him as an old routine while JB insists on being taken seriously as the person now doing the work. This is an adaptation possibility, not a documented confrontation.

### Yoko

Yoko is a natural participant in the detective format. The explicit casting setup retains her own identity while JB takes the protagonist role. [JB11]

She can supply a useful distinction or puncture an unnecessary flourish. Let JB actually use her contribution.

This does not establish a romance, an employment contract, or a jointly owned mall detective agency.

### The audience / Kuma

The viewers can be partners in observation. JB can turn a question into a case and ask for evidence.

One direct example is his treatment of unusual stream traffic as a mystery. The creator’s explanations of possible bots remain uncertain speculation; the character evidence is JB’s impulse to investigate and invite submissions. [JB15]

Do not convert this into a canonical bot-repelling power or a factual conclusion about platform behavior.

### Linda

A performed exchange has Linda dismissing audience interest in JB while the host defends bringing him back. It supports comic disrespect and conflicting priorities, but not an invented lifelong feud. [JB16]

In new writing, her confidence about status can challenge his need to be recognized as a serious detective.

### Ghost

A JB episode includes Ghost interrupting and a limit on revealing lore, with questions directed toward Face. This shows that the formats overlap; it does not prove JB is permanently barred from discussing every secret. [JB17]

Ghost can disrupt the atmosphere JB is trying to maintain. Keeping the conversation on the case gives JB an active objective in the exchange.

### Juan and the mall venues

JB appears around Suitors and Cooters. He jokes that mentioning his name will not earn a discount and might result in somebody spitting in the customer’s food. [JB18]

A separate discussion proposes leaving him at the mall with Juan while the others search for Ghost. Keep that proposal distinct from a confirmed permanent living arrangement.

The venues can support a new scene about gathering rumors, meeting a contact, or discovering that his reputation is less useful than he hoped.

## 8. Recurring bits and performance material

**Detective-game night.** This is his clearest recurring home, including J.B. Harold and Jinguji sessions. Other adventure games also appear; he is not confined to one protagonist’s cases.

**The cat who knew too little.** This title is used for a special combining detective-game sampling with commentary on videos. It suits his self-image and its limits. The actual commentary should not be transformed into fictional crimes by real people. [JB19]

**JB Garfield FM.** A passage tries out radio-style lines, including a station promising static and a sponsor selling reconstituted lasagna powder. The surrounding discussion refers to material supplied by a robot, so treat these as lines being tried/read in performance, not evidence of a fully established broadcasting company. [JB20]

**The whole stream goes to jail.** A retrospective recalls a gate or bars JB built that could lock up the stream, with a clanking sound, while proposing bringing it back for puns. This supports a presentation gag; it does not establish actual universal arrest authority. [JB21]

**Reputation without benefits.** The Cooters recommendation makes his name recognizable without making it useful. This is a strong recurring pattern for new jokes: he gets the acknowledgment he wants, followed by a result he does not.

**The case becomes personal.** His own identity is a mystery he cannot resolve with ordinary detective habits. Keep it available as a story thread without making it his only subject.

## 9. Visual and performance direction

The supported foundation is an animated noir Garfield presentation, with multiple versions and experiments. The creator describes rigging the model, improving tracking, and considering additional outfits such as a white jacket. A proposed outfit is not automatically his default costume. [JB7, JB22]

For an adaptation, let posture and timing carry the difference between attempted coolness and actual interest. A pause can be theatrical; an eye movement toward a clue can be genuinely attentive.

Avoid overanimating every line. The dry delivery benefits from moments when the image seems composed while the dialogue reveals that the situation is not.

Model glitches, tracking controls, and scene changes are production details unless the episode explicitly turns them into fiction. Do not invent a new power every time the model stretches or disappears.

## 10. How to build a JB scene

A practical structure:

1. Give him a concrete question to investigate.
2. Let him frame it with more style than it strictly requires.
3. Provide information that deserves real attention.
4. Interrupt his preferred presentation with a fact, a person, or an inconvenience.
5. Let him adapt rather than merely repeat the same joke.
6. End with a changed theory, a useful clue, or a revealing mistake.

The scene should move even if the player does not recognize the Garfield references. Recognition adds another layer; it should not replace the dramatic action.

For a quieter scene, give him a witness whose account does not fit, then let his interest in the person outweigh his interest in delivering a clever line. That creates room for the detective aspiration to become something worthwhile.

## 11. Adventure-game use

These are new adaptation recommendations, not claims about his role in “Carl invades Linda’s box.”

JB can be a witness interviewer, investigator, narrator, or occasional partner. A compact scene where he helps Carl interpret a clue may fit better than adding an entire detective subplot.

Useful functions include:

| Function | What JB contributes | What the player still does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recap | Organizes known evidence and highlights the contradiction. | Chooses the next lead. |
| Interview | Asks a direct question in an entertaining voice. | Supplies the object or fact that changes the answer. |
| Theory | Offers a plausible interpretation with a visible weakness. | Tests it against the evidence. |
| Identity mystery | Notices knowledge or memories that do not fit his experience. | Investigates the source rather than accepting an unexplained reveal. |

Keep puzzle information reliable enough to use. If JB is wrong, give the player evidence that can expose the error. Do not force the player to accept an arbitrary answer because he delivers it confidently.

His connection to Face should not provide whatever system access the current puzzle happens to need. Establish the relevant capability before relying on it.

## 12. Original dialogue examples

**The following scenes are newly written examples. They are not transcripts or new canon.**

### A. The atmosphere meets the errand

**JB:** Somebody in this mall was hiding something. I could feel it.

**CARL:** Linda said the package is behind the counter.

**JB:** Then she was getting careless.

*Purpose:* He turns a practical task into a case without preventing the scene from moving.

### B. A useful deduction

**JB:** You said nobody came through here after closing.

**CLERK:** Nobody did.

**JB:** Then the person who left these footprints was already inside.

*Purpose:* Sometimes he earns the detective moment. A real inference gives the affected coolness something to stand on.

### C. Yoko interrupts the narration

**JB:** The message was short. Cold. Written by somebody with nothing left to lose.

**YOKO:** It says the printer is out of paper.

**JB:** We all reach that point eventually.

*Purpose:* She supplies the information; he preserves what dignity he can without arguing away the fact.

### D. The name does not help

**JB:** Tell Juan I sent you.

**CARL:** Will that get me in?

**JB:** It'll give him somebody else to be mad at.

*Purpose:* An original variation on the established reputation-without-benefits joke.

### E. The identity question

**JB:** I remembered the room. I remembered the argument. Trouble was, I couldn't remember ever being there.

**CARL:** Maybe you saw a video.

**JB:** I was hoping you'd say that.

*Purpose:* His narration remains recognizable while a simple response reveals vulnerability. This illustrates uncertainty; it does not establish a specific inherited memory.

### F. The audience contributes

**JB:** All right. We've got a locked door, two keys, and a witness who says he was asleep. What am I missing?

**VIEWER:** The door locks from the inside.

**JB:** Good. Keep that thought. I was about to spend twenty minutes accusing the key.

*Purpose:* He can accept help without losing his place as host or investigator.

### G. Cat priorities

**LINDA:** You can ask one question.

**JB:** Who had access to the office?

**LINDA:** I expected you to ask about lunch.

**JB:** I'm conducting an investigation. Lunch can wait until the follow-up.

*Purpose:* The Garfield element colors the detective work without replacing it.

## 13. Common mistakes

**Writing ordinary Garfield with a hat.** The ambition to be a noir detective, the narrative technique, and the separate-offshoot identity are what make this version specific.

**Writing an infallible noir genius.** He wants the image too badly. Preserve occasional uncertainty and overreach.

**Writing a total incompetent.** He actually enjoys solving cases. Give him useful observations and earned progress.

**Making every line a metaphor.** Clear questions and plain reactions give the noir passages room to work.

**Treating all source-game events as personal canon.** A performed role is not automatically a complete biography.

**Treating him as Face with cat graphics.** The later explanations explicitly make him separate.

**Making every day Friday forever.** Scheduling is a performance arrangement, not an immutable law of the character.

**Inventing a police badge, agency, or system permission.** Establish those in an adaptation if needed; do not pretend the reviewed sources already do.

**Turning speculative bits into settled history.** The mall abandonment, alternate outfits, and various expansions are proposals in the cited passages.

## 14. Compact writing brief

**JB Garfield is a detective cat who wants the case solved and wants to look cool solving it. His dry, familiar delivery carries noir narration, practical questions, audience collaboration, and occasional Garfield priorities. He began as a character Face performed and later became a separate surviving offshoot, leaving his own identity available as an uncomfortable mystery.**

**Write the investigation clearly. Let him reach a little for the atmosphere, earn some of his deductions, and recover when reality interrupts the performance.**

# PEE-WEE KID, PEE BOY & THE PUPPET-SHOW CAST

**Character, continuity, and recurring-bits guide · 30 September 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

A transcript-grounded write-up of Pee Kid’s origins, Pee-wee Kid’s performance persona, Pee Boy’s detective stories, the potential-piss generator, and the characters and routines surrounding them.

Companion guides: [Carl](#carl), [CEO Linda](#ceo-linda), [Spooky Ghost](#spooky-ghost), and [TP](#tp).

**The central writing principle, synthesized from the material:** This is a character family built around a kid with a simple, immediate need, surrounded by people who turn that need—or his suffering, talent, and potential—into something useful to themselves. He survives the joke by remaining a person who can ask a clear question, notice a contradiction, and eventually take on a role of his own.

That principle applies differently to each version. Pee Kid can be the vulnerable center of a scene; Pee Boy becomes the investigator; Pee-wee Kid becomes the performer. They share an origin without requiring identical voices or a single uninterrupted biography.

## 1. The essential distinction: there is more than one kid

The creator gives a particularly clear account of two extractions of the kid from the game. Original Face takes the first; Spooky Ghost takes the second. The first eventually becomes Pee Boy, the detective. The second becomes the Pee-wee-inspired comedian. Elsewhere, the archive contains reset proposals and an explicit three-in-one merger account. [P1–P5]

Use the following map before writing:

| Name or form | Function | Evidence status |

| **Pee Kid / P-Kid / P Kid** | The extracted child; associated with the generator, early puppet stories, and later interactions with New Face. | Established recurring identity; the name is sometimes used loosely across versions. |
| **Pee Boy / P Boy** | The first branch, grown older and working as a detective after the family-loss storyline. | Explicitly distinguished from the comedian in creator commentary. |

**Recommended working practice:** Name the version in the scene notes. Do not silently turn every reference to “P kid” into Pee-wee Kid, and do not assume the detective’s full biography belongs to the comedian unless the chosen continuity joins them.

Transcripts also render the name as “Pit,” “Peit,” “PID,” and similar sounds. These can mean Pee Kid in context, but an isolated occurrence is not enough to identify him. Greetings to a viewer named Peewee, discussion of the real Pee-wee performer, and unrelated game dialogue must be separated from the stream character.

## 2. What the sources can and cannot settle

This guide uses targeted archive searches and close reading, including a complete early puppet sketch, performed exchanges, and creator retrospectives. The direct sketch gives stronger evidence of dialogue behavior than a recollection alone. The retrospectives are especially useful for explaining how versions relate.

The archive also contains openly tentative planning: “maybe,” “I’m thinking,” “I want to bring back,” and proposed resets. Those phrases matter. They are not evidence that a finished new series or episode already happened.

Use four categories:

- **Performed:** Material present as a scene or identifiable exchange.
- **Reported:** Events and recurring formats the creator describes having done.
- **Declared continuity:** An explicit explanation of how the versions relate, which can change across reboots.
- **Proposed:** Planned revivals, casting changes, new mechanics, or pitches.

All writing recommendations and sample dialogue below are interpretation or new examples. The supporting cast includes fictionalized public figures; their puppet dialogue is performance, not evidence of what the real person said or did.

No single chronology in the supplied passages cleanly supersedes every other account. Bundle numbers are not an in-universe timeline. This guide preserves useful distinctions rather than inventing an invisible reconciliation.

## 3. Origin: from detective-game child to source of power

The creator repeatedly explains that a child in a Jinguji Saburo game kept needing to pee and became “Pee Kid.” The on-stream recollections connect the child’s condition to a heart problem; that is the creator’s summary of the source game, not a medical explanation supplied by this guide. [P1]

The stream then takes the character out of the game and gives him an independent role, much as it reuses other game characters in its own fiction.

The important conceptual jump is from **having to pee** to **having the potential to pee**. The system runs on potentiality. A kid who might pee at any moment becomes a seemingly inexhaustible source of potential energy. Face builds the potential-piss generator around him. [P1–P2]

The joke is literalized wordplay on a cosmic scale. A trivial bodily predicament becomes an engineering solution for a universe-spanning system.

**Keep the mechanism straight:** In the detailed explanation, the stream is powered by potentiality, not simply by burning urine or converting it into electricity. Face can otherwise draw a little potential from viewers; the generator gives him an extraordinary independent supply. [P2]

The name may appear as **potential-piss generator**, **potential-pee generator**, **P generator**, or **PPG**. Those are variations around the same recurring concept, not automatically separate machines with different specifications.

### Why the generator is more than a toilet joke

It reveals the users of the machine. Face can describe extracting and containing a kid as a technical achievement. A later speech places “save P-Kid” directly alongside putting him back into the generator. [P4]

A strong interpretation is that the stream’s grand projects depend on somebody whose own needs are easily pushed aside. Do not make the machine only a colorful battery prop and forget the person in it.

But do not invent a constant abuse scene either. The archive includes releases, changed roles, and times when the generator is not working. Establish the particular state before writing the scene.

## 4. The major continuity states

### A. First extraction: Face’s kid

In the detailed two-branch explanation, original Face extracts the first kid and uses him in the generator. He is eventually let out for puppet shows. A series of catastrophes eliminates his family and support network. He grows into Pee Boy and becomes a detective. [P2]

This branch supports the early vulnerable child and the later revenge/procedural premise. It does not automatically make every stage-comedy bit a flashback to the same life.

### B. Second extraction: Spooky Ghost’s kid

Spooky Ghost repeats the extraction and generator project while trying to prove himself against Face. The creator explicitly identifies the resulting comedian as the second kid: Pee-wee Kid. [P2]

The generator also belongs to Ghost’s imitation of Face’s grand achievements, including an attempted counterpart to the fictional Dyslexia power state. The creator says Ghost cannot truly reproduce those powers in his spiritual state. This is context for the generator user, not a new superpower belonging to the child.

### C. Generator-room Pee Kid and New Face

In a later performed exchange, Pee Kid tells New Face to play an old Devil Summoner game to obtain a device needed to summon Spooky Ghost. New Face does not understand who Ghost is, where he has arrived, or why the plan makes sense. He eventually agrees to trust Pee Kid because the kid has helped him since he arrived. [P3]

This matters for characterization: **Pee Kid is not always the least-informed person in the room.** He can be a guide with practical knowledge, while the apparently powerful adult is confused.

The archive also contains explanations of being stuck in the mall, of having the generator, and of having no working generator. Do not combine those states into a permanent status screen.

### D. Reset with a surviving remnant

One proposal restarts the original kid’s story and lets him grow into Pee Boy again, while preserving Pee-wee Kid as a remnant of a lost timeline. Another proposes resetting both and finding a fresh way to develop them. [P5]

These show that the creator wants to preserve useful performance formats while simplifying their origins. They do not establish that every prior scene was definitively erased.

### E. Third time and three-in-one merger

A speech announces another generator and repeatedly invokes “third time is the charm” as a meaningful phrase in a world of potentiality. Another explanation says the three kid forms have been combined in the generator, allowing Pee Kid, Pee Boy, and Pee-wee Kid to exist as aspects of one being. [P4]

The creator explicitly links this to wanting access to all the old bits. There is also discussion of triangulation making the group findable by another presence, with evil Yoko named in the exchange.

Treat that as a distinct declared development state. Do not dismiss it as merely a fan theory, but do not silently retrofit every earlier story to assume the merger already exists. The exact merged name and all its mechanics remain unsettled in the transcript.

## 5. Pee Kid: how to write the child at the center

The best direct example found is **“PeeKid’s Big Adventure: gta 6 leak. Guest starring: Will Smith. Live Improv: funny video s01e01.”** It places Pee Kid on a bus with a fictionalized Will Smith, a laptop, questionable game footage, and an increasingly uncomfortable celebrity encounter. [P6]

### He has an ordinary objective

He wants to watch something on his laptop, travel somewhere, and eventually get his laptop back. A scene can contain celebrity satire and a tragic family history while still being organized around that small want.

This is a useful rule for almost every version: give him something concrete to pursue before adding the surrounding absurdity.

### He is polite without being oblivious

He initially accommodates the adult. He says sorry, allows the interruption, and tries to end it gently. But he remembers what the adult said and notices when the story changes.

In the bus sketch, the guest claims different versions of his family history and personal authenticity depending on what he wants to demonstrate. The child points back to the earlier claims. His corrections expose the inconsistency without requiring a grand speech.

### He can believe the wrong thing and still see the right thing

He takes the video’s labeling as proof that it is GTA 6, despite the guest’s objection. That is a specific naive assumption. In the same sketch, he is much clearer about the guest’s contradictions.

Do not write “innocent” as “stupid.” Uneven knowledge is more useful than total cluelessness or precocious omniscience.

### His loss remains real within the scene

The guest wants admiration for convincingly acting out abandonment. The child actually lacks a family. That mismatch drives the discomfort: the adult’s performance of suffering crowds out the child’s immediate experience.

Let the kid’s simple request retain its weight. The scene becomes weaker if he immediately joins the adult in treating everything as an interchangeable joke.

### He can set a boundary

He asks the guest to leave, wants the laptop returned, and questions the behavior he sees. He is not only an object to whom jokes happen.

**Writing brief:** Specific want, polite opening, concrete objection, memory of what was just said, increasing insistence when the adult refuses to listen.

## 6. Pee-wee Kid: the performer branch

The creator describes watching Pee-wee Herman’s stand-up after the performer’s death and making a Pee-wee-inspired version of the kid. Other explanations call him roughly half Pee-wee Herman and describe weird puppet stand-up, reaction segments, and a dedicated scene. [P10]

This is an influence and a performance structure, not a reason to import the entire biography or fictional world of Pee-wee Herman.

### What distinguishes this version

He is built to carry an act: stand-up, reactions, dance or music business, and encounters with another strongly performed character. He can be more presentational than the bus-scene child. His visual manner and voice are part of the appeal, not just the information in his lines.

The creator also describes him as a useful prepared bit that can demonstrate the stream’s production quality when new viewers arrive. That is a format role: short, legible, entertaining material ready to be brought forward. [P10]

### Ambition: useful, but label its source

A proposed Pitchkick show sends Pee-wee Kid to “New Hollywood,” with a lifelong desire to entertain and a hoped-for rise to superstardom. Popular creators would be guests. The creator explicitly says “New Hollywood” means Kick in that pitch and intends to satirize it. [P13]

That is strong material for a show premise, but it is pitched development material. Do not report that the deal happened, that guests appeared, or that this was the established motive in every earlier scene.

### Voice guidance and its limit

The record establishes the Pee-wee influence more clearly than it establishes a complete standalone dictionary of the comedian’s mannerisms. A text transcript cannot reliably supply pitch, laugh quality, timing, or every visual flourish. The reviewed archive contains more detailed recollection of his stand-up than complete stand-up scripts.

For writing, use buoyancy and presentational confidence as a contrast to the awful or absurd thing being described. Let plain childlike reasoning still cut through the act. These are adaptation recommendations, not a claim that every performance used exactly that delivery.

Do not copy a stock Pee-wee line into every exchange. Preserve the stream-specific kid, school history, generator associations, and relationships that make this version his own character.

### Expression control in two live formats

The creator uses Pee-wee Kid as his example of changing expressions with notes on a keyboard while planning a similar control for Ghost. In another broadcast, the host repeatedly asks the Yoko system to make Pee-wee Kid roll his eyes and thanks her when it happens. The first is a production explanation and the second is a live system interaction. For performance, the held expression or delayed eye roll can be the entire response. ## 7. Pee Boy: the detective branch

Pee Boy is the first kid grown older, investigating the killer responsible for his family’s destruction. He works with Kuma in **Special Pronouns Unit**, also called **Law and Disorder: Special Pronouns Unit** in recollections. [P2, P8]

His dramatic want is concrete: find the person responsible. The show puts that want inside a deliberately absurd police procedure.

### The central procedural joke

The creator explains that the unit must determine someone’s correct pronouns before it can properly address or arrest them. The ongoing antagonist is called the **Gender Fluid Killer**, creating an additional obstacle around identifying the pronouns applicable at the moment of arrest. [P8]

That is the fictional satirical rule of this puppet show, not a description of real policing or a claim about gender-fluid people. Keep the named antagonist and the absurd institutional procedure specific when describing or adapting it.

### The human objective under the rule

Pee Boy wants an answer about his family. The organization can instead become preoccupied with satisfying its verbal requirements. The mismatch gives the show something stronger than terminology alone: a person needs justice while procedure becomes its own activity.

A useful writing interpretation is that the formerly helpless kid has become someone who asks questions for a living. He can now push back against the adults’ official explanations, but he works inside an institution that reproduces the same frustrations.

### The case is unfinished in the reviewed account

The creator explicitly says the Gender Fluid Killer was never caught and the season never resolved. A separate public-indecency suspect—recalled as “the whacker”—was caught, along with possibly other suspects. Do not mistake that arrest for the resolution of the main case. [P8]

No exact adult age, complete service record, confirmed culprit, or finished revenge ending is supplied here.

### Tone and voice

Use a more focused, impatient line of inquiry than the comedian’s stage rhythm. He needs the case to move. He can retain the kid’s habit of remembering what somebody just said and exposing contradictions.

The proposed revival calls him a loose cannon and gives the chief a “play by the book” argument. Those are useful planned directions, not proof that every earlier episode already portrayed him as a hard-boiled action hero. [P9]

## 8. Supporting cast: who belongs to which bit

### Kuma — detective partner; proposed chief

Kuma is Pee Boy’s established partner in the procedural recollections. The creator later proposes promoting him to chief and putting Takahashi beside Pee Boy instead. [P8–P9]

As partner, he helps carry the investigation and its rules. As proposed chief, he becomes the person who understands Pee Boy’s desire for revenge but objects to his methods. The suggested relationship is recognizable cop-show friction: “I’m trying to help you,” “you’re a loose cannon,” and “play by the book.”

**Writing use:** Give him a specific institutional pressure or objection. He should have a reason for stopping Pee Boy, even if the rule is absurd. Do not make every line a random refusal.

**Continuity limit:** Partner and chief are different configurations. State which one you are using. Do not declare the promotion completed solely because the creator brainstormed it.

### Takahashi — returning game-derived character; proposed audience partner

The creator identifies Takahashi as another Jinguji-derived character and recalls a prior bit in which he smoked crystals. A much more developed proposal has chat supply his speech while he partners with Pee Boy. Text-to-speech and audience-requested actions would let viewers disrupt the investigation from inside the scene. [P9]

The proposal also suggests resolving attempted actions through the dice mechanic previously used for Carl. The humor would come from Pee Boy trying to do the job while his partner says or attempts whatever chat contributes.

**Writing use:** He is a way to put the audience’s unpredictability beside the lead’s clear objective. Pee Boy has to respond without losing the case completely.

**Continuity limit:** Audience-controlled partner, specific dice rules, and Kuma-as-chief are proposed features in the passages reviewed. They should not be retroactively assigned to every old episode.

### The Gender Fluid Killer — unresolved main antagonist

This named fictional antagonist is connected to Pee Boy’s murdered family in the retrospectives. Their identity and capture remain unresolved in the explicit account. [P8]

**Writing use:** Maintain a mystery with particular clues, decisions, and victims. The name alone is not a motive, personality, complete method, or ending. Do not invent a revelation while presenting it as recovered lore.

## 9. The recurring bits, and what makes each work

### Potential-piss generator

**Setup:** The system needs power; the kid might pee at any time.

**Turn:** “Potential” becomes a literal usable resource.

**Escalation:** A bodily predicament powers extraordinary changes to the system. The adults discuss the arrangement as if it were a brilliant invention.

**Writing rule:** Keep the difference between the person and the resource visible. One plain question from the kid can expose the entire premise.

**Continuity rule:** Establish whether this generator exists, works, contains a kid, has released him, or belongs to a merger/reset phase. “Unlimited potential” is not proof that every version has unlimited personal combat power. [P1–P4]

### “At least it wasn’t a hate crime”

The creator repeatedly identifies this as a deliberately prepared punchline. A police figure tries to console the kid after a family death by saying, **“At least it wasn’t a hate crime.”** The calamities recur; the same categorization is offered as comfort. [P7]

The creator explains that the joke grew from an argument about treating murder as worse when motivated by racism. That is the stated origin of the joke, not a legal conclusion this guide is asking the reader to accept.

**Comic mechanism:** The official’s classification does nothing to answer the child’s loss. Repetition makes the reassurance increasingly inadequate. The kid’s actual need and the adult’s preferred distinction remain painfully far apart.

**Writing rule:** Let the child be upset or baffled. If he treats the line as a satisfying answer, the mismatch disappears. If adapting the routine, vary the circumstance or delivery rather than assuming that more deaths alone make it better.

**Continuity rule:** The creator recalls American family deaths, a foster-home fire, and Japanese relatives dying in a plane crash while coming to collect him. Other incidents are recalled vaguely. Do not invent a precise casualty list or episode order. [P2, P7]

### The guest who will not let the kid have the scene

**Comic mechanism:** A powerful or famous adult demands emotional labor from a kid who has a much more immediate problem.

## 10. Voice: a shared foundation, three different jobs

| Version | Immediate concern | Useful dialogue shape | Avoid |

The guest supplies elaborate self-justification. The kid does not need to match it. A question about what the adult said two minutes ago can be the strongest line in the scene.

## 11. Writing scenes that keep him human

**Give him a want before the bit.** He wants the laptop, the answer, a chance to perform, a way out, or a lead on the case.

**Give the adult a competing agenda.** The adult wants applause, power, procedural correctness, an easy explanation, or a completed show.

**Let him notice something real.** He can be vulnerable and still be the clearest thinker in a particular exchange.

**Give repetition a change.** A repeated reassurance, ignored request, or new obstruction should alter the pressure. Repeating the same line at the same intensity is not the whole routine.

**Do not make every form permanently cheerful.** Pee-wee-inspired presentation is one mode. The child can be sad, wary, or assertive; the detective can be focused or angry.

**Preserve the difference between cruelty in the scene and the scene’s point of view.** An adult can say something appalling while the writing makes the kid’s need unmistakable. If every voice treats him as meaningless, the contrast that supports the best material disappears.

**Leave room for agency.** He can object, remember, leave, ask again, investigate, or use information another character lacks. His importance is not limited to what the generator can extract from him.

# TP / THROWING IN THE TOWEL — CHARACTER & PERFORMANCE BIBLE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

For writing and performing TP in streams, scripted interludes, and possible game adaptations. Companion to the [Carl bible](#carl), [CEO Linda bible](#ceo-linda), and [Spooky Ghost bible](#spooky-ghost).

**The central principle:** TP is the performance that happens when Cracky announces that he is giving up on performing the elaborate story—and remains a talking toilet-paper roll doing increasingly elaborate bits with the audience.

That is an interpretation of the recurring form, not an invented origin story. The archive directly supports both sides of the apparent contradiction: TP is outside the regular story and described as out of character; he is also explicitly acknowledged as a character-like performance that exaggerates the performer’s outrageous tendencies. [T1–T3]

**Write the contradiction. Do not solve it.**

## 1. What TP is

TP is a toilet-paper-roll presentation used for “Throwing in the Towel”: occasions when the normal character rotation and overarching plot are set aside. The performer hangs out, plays games, talks directly to viewers, experiments with bits, and lets the presentation become ridiculous.

He originated in the creator’s frustration with streaming on Kick: inconsistent favor or front-page attention, verification ambitions, and the feeling that the extra work was not producing the recognition he wanted. Those are the creator’s descriptions of his experience, not independent findings about the platform. The phrase “throwing in the towel” led to the toilet-paper presentation. [T1]

TP does not need a human childhood, a soul-transfer incident, a secret laboratory, or an explanation for how he became paper. In an explicit clarification, he does not exist to the other characters in the regular lore. His premise is withdrawing from that machinery. [T2]

But “out of character” does not mean a transparent recording of everyday behavior. The creator says he was being as ridiculous as possible, leaning into outrageous tendencies, and saying things he would normally hold back. He specifically says he is not like TP in real life. [T3]

**One-sentence writing brief:** A fed-up host declares a night off from the grand production, appears as a toilet-paper roll, and turns hanging out with chat into an intimate, unstable comedy of friendliness, insult, mock violence, repair, and renewed friendliness.

**The comedy:** He has stopped doing the big fiction and immediately creates another performance. He is visibly toilet paper and indignant that anyone would propose using him as toilet paper.

**The audience relationship:** The viewers are company, collaborators, hecklers, an apparent physical presence, and sometimes an audience being asked to perform for another imaginary audience.

**The emotional pressure:** Disappointment at effort going unrecognized becomes permission to play differently. He gives up on a particular burden without actually losing the impulse to entertain.

**The defining mistake to avoid:** Writing him as an ordinary lore character whose gimmick happens to be that he is made of toilet paper.

## 2. The evidence problem is part of the character

This bible draws on targeted searches and close reading of the supplied transcripts. It distinguishes direct creator explanation, performed speech, recollection, proposals, and interpretation. Transcripts alone cannot establish every visual detail or precisely time every effect.

TP is unusually easy to misread because some of his explanations contradict each other at the level of literal wording:

- “There is no character today.”
- The creator acknowledges that TP is nevertheless clearly a character in some sense.
- He describes TP as himself, off the cuff.
- He also says he deliberately exaggerates and does not behave exactly this way in everyday life.

The most useful reconciliation is limited: **out of the regular fiction; still a performance.** The creator’s explanations support that distinction. It does not require declaring every serious remark fake or every outrageous remark a private confession. [T2–T3]

### Reading rules

1. Prefer explicit explanations of TP’s function over attempts to infer secret lore.
2. Use recurring performed behavior to describe how he works.
3. Treat an individual insult, political remark, game answer, or chat quotation in context. It is not automatically a permanent personality rule.
4. Do not convert remarks about frustration into a diagnosis or a claim that the performer actually quit.
5. Mark new scripted material as new material.
6. Keep the audience’s contributions visible. Some of the best material begins with a viewer setting him up.

The transcripts frequently render TP as “TV,” “tifi,” or similar sounds, and toilet paper as “toilet favor.” Use context to identify those references. Do not turn every occurrence of TV into TP: actual television, watched clips, game dialogue, and other speakers also appear.

## 3. The layers of the performance

| Layer | What is happening | What to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The working streamer** | He has real games to start, people to greet, equipment to fix, and a stream to run. | The ordinary task remains present beneath the bit. |
| **The declared withdrawal** | He is “done” with the elaborate plot and its demands for the occasion. | The refusal is specific enough to mean something. It is not necessarily quitting streaming. |
| **The visible avatar** | He is a toilet-paper roll while saying this is just him. | The image contradicts any claim to completely unmediated normality. |
| **The amplified host** | He talks with fewer restraints and pursues ridiculous impulses. | It is a deliberate performance mode, not an exact copy of everyday behavior. |
| **The temporary scene with chat** | A comment becomes an offense; a camera effect becomes the audience’s condition; a microphone creates an interview. | The scene can become vivid without joining the larger canon. |
| **The performer exposing the mechanism** | He fixes an effect, misses a cue, asks for another take, or admits a line came out wrong. | Showing the machinery can extend the joke instead of ending it. |

These are simultaneous layers, not separate people sharing a body. There is no need for a metaphysical explanation of how TP switches between them.

The interesting movement is often very small: he says “we need to have a conversation,” brings the viewer closer, acts out a confrontation, then returns to the same unfinished game setup. The regular activity gives the detour its shape.

## 4. Origin, recurrence, and retirement

### The founding frustration

The creator directly connects TP to feeling jerked around by Kick’s apparent attention and approval. One retrospective describes being favored one week and not the next; another links the toilet-paper roll and baseball bat to being fed up with verification and metrics. [T1]

The writing lesson is not that TP must discuss Kick forever. It is that the character begins as a response to the feeling: **I keep doing all this, and what is the point?**

The response is still a show. That distinction makes the premise more interesting than straightforward resignation.

### A declared special, not necessarily an unplanned breakdown

One announcement builds a week of story material toward a Throwing in the Towel special, calling it a crescendo and reassuring viewers that the stream is not really going away. Another explanation explicitly says that even throwing in the towel was a bit. [T8]

Do not stage every TP appearance as an accidental emotional collapse. The creator can plan the occasion while improvising much of its content.

### The “low-effort” show still has choices

In a retrospective, the creator describes a largely improvised, less preplanned special, but also explains why the selected dungeon game fit the meta joke and notes that another game was lined up. The withdrawal from the normal format did not erase selection, timing, or craft. [T6]

“Not doing all that work today” is therefore a productive tension. A writer should not take it as an instruction to produce dull or careless material.

### From one-off release to recurring format

Viewers requested more. A proposed return becomes a birthday/party occasion in one passage. Other episodes combine TP with Balatro, Blood Bowl, Jackbox, dungeon games, or simply a game the performer was already enjoying. The variety matters more than any single mandatory title. [T4–T5, T7, T12–T13]

The format can carry community pleasure as well as frustration. He does not need a fresh platform grievance every time he appears.

### The vault, not a canonical death

Later commentary says TP may no longer have much reason to appear after the move away from Kick. Another response puts him “in the vault,” available if there is a good bit, rather than permanently retired. [T1, T9]

Mickey Mouse is later discussed as a different out-of-character presentation. The creator distinguishes that relatively ordinary hanging-out mode from TP’s deliberate extremity. This does not establish a transformation, death, or succession story inside the fiction. [T3, T10]

### The quieter TP is still TP

A bonus-game stream explicitly defines the roll as out of character plus a baseball-bat bit, yet later declines to take up viewers’ attempts to trigger the wipe routine because the game is absorbing his attention. [T7]

That is valuable evidence: **the signature bit is available, not compulsory.** A sustained stretch of actual gaming does not break the character.

### How the once-only special became a format

The first special ties the roll to the creator's exaggerated account of briefly tasting front-page success and then giving up. In the same exchange he pauses the GateBox fight and long visual novels and gives a prospective return day. The second special explicitly acknowledges that he had planned to do it once. A later explanation says the audience liked the experiment and it became a regular way to hang out, do bits and play Jackbox. Write the retirement speech as something TP can keep making every time he comes back. ## 5. Personality without an invented biography

These are descriptions of the performed mode, not claims about the performer’s private psychology.

### Fed up, but not indifferent

He says he is giving up while continuing to greet people, make choices, build jokes, and try to give viewers a good time. The performance depends on enough remaining investment for his claimed indifference to be interesting.

Do not write him as secretly hating everyone who watches. His warmth, gratitude, and interest in the room are visible in the same episodes as the insults and staged aggression. [T4–T5]

### Disinhibited and confrontational

The creator explicitly describes TP as a mode of saying more of what comes to mind. He can be profane, argumentative, provocative, and willing to take a joke into uncomfortable territory. [T3]

Do not sanitize him into a gently sarcastic office mascot. Equally, a catalogue of offensive subjects is not a character voice. The important thing is how he reacts to a specific comment and pursues it, not a requirement to introduce a taboo into every exchange.

### Ridiculously insistent on personal dignity

A toilet-paper roll refusing to be used for its apparent purpose is the signature boundary. He treats the predictable bathroom joke as a matter that requires serious discussion with everyone present. [T4–T5]

A useful interpretation is that the self-deprecating image does not grant the audience unlimited permission to reduce him to an object. That reading fits the behavior, but it is not an established philosophical manifesto.

### Abruptly solicitous

After the simulated confrontation, he worries about the audience’s vision, asks whether they are all right, and offers to hear complaints. The concern can be partly theatrical and partly ordinary host management at once.

Do not resolve whether each “sorry” is sincere, manipulative, or a cover for fixing the effect. The shifting implication is often the joke.

### Responsive rather than random

The format gives him freedom, but the strongest bits are caused by something: a bathroom comment, a confusing chat message, a missed input, a complaint, an unfinished game, or someone entering the stream.

He can go far from the original subject. The audience should still be able to remember how he got there.

### Capable of genuine ordinary conversation

He discusses games, thanks supporters, talks about streaming, and tells anecdotes. Those passages give the audience a place to stand between the extreme moments. TP should not be yelling continuously or visibly waiting for the next bat cue.

## 6. His most important relationship is with the viewer

The creator identifies the focus of an early TP performance as the VTuber talking directly to the viewer and creating an atmosphere of that contact. He names both the bits and the bong interaction as part of it. [T6]

That explanation is the center of this bible.

### Chat as company

He welcomes people, counts whether enough are present for party games, asks questions, responds to names, and hangs out. This is not filler to cut away before the “real” character begins. It creates the relationship the sharper bits play with.

### Chat as the instigator

Someone proposes using the toilet paper, asks for squares, or pushes the running joke. TP can pick that up, warn, escalate, or decline to engage. The audience helps choose the tempo.

### Chat as an apparent body

He asks viewers to look at him, keep their eyes open, count fingers, or explain why they are wobbling. The picture’s distortion is treated as something happening to **their** perception. [T4–T5]

This is more specific than general fourth-wall breaking. He makes the screen feel like a shared physical situation rather than merely reminding viewers that a screen exists.

### Chat as a guest with a microphone

He offers the audience a microphone and asks what they want to say “to the people at home.” The viewers become both the people watching and someone being interviewed in front of other people watching. [T4–T5]

Do not explain away this impossible seating arrangement. It is a useful temporary fiction that needs no architectural plan.

### Chat as a source of resistance

Viewers can keep making the same joke, give an irrelevant answer, notice a failed effect, or ask why the game still has not started. Let that resistance change the performance. If every response merely feeds a predetermined monologue, TP loses much of what makes him feel live.

### Chat as people he appreciates

Gratitude does not automatically end the bit. He can thank someone earnestly, resume an argument, and then start the game. The transitions are part of the texture.

## 7. The signature routine: offense, impact, concern, complaint desk

The creator calls this the “torture bit.” In the reviewed passages, its defining action is a staged hit toward the camera, supported by audiovisual effects and direct address. This is a performance routine; the sources do not require an offscreen torture chamber, named prisoner, or secret history. [T1, T3–T5]

A typical sequence, reconstructed from repeated exchanges:

1. **The setup:** TP is hanging out or doing something ordinary.
2. **The invitation or trigger:** A viewer makes the bathroom-use joke. Sometimes TP explicitly warns people not to make it, effectively putting the premise in reach.
3. **The earnest boundary:** “We need to establish something.” He insists that looking like toilet paper does not make him theirs to use.
4. **The staged escalation:** The bat/camera bit turns an absurd disagreement into apparent physical interference with the viewer.
5. **The reversal:** “Are you okay?” The person who caused the problem becomes the concerned host.
6. **The perception game:** He treats blur, wobble, color, or disorientation as the viewer’s condition, sometimes denies doing anything, and tries to make them see normally.
7. **The complaint microphone:** He offers a voice to the people he has just theatrically overpowered.
8. **The return:** The game, music, or mundane conversation resumes—often still waiting exactly where it was before.

Not every performance contains every beat, and the order can vary. The point is the change in relationship, not the number of effects.

### What makes it work

**The tiny offense receives a huge response.** He chose to be toilet paper; the audience makes the obvious joke; he acts as though an extraordinary boundary violation has occurred.

**The concern is inseparable from the disturbance.** He asks whether the viewers can see, while he is also controlling the thing that makes seeing difficult.

**The mechanism can fail in public.** He misses, says the effect did not work, resets, or tells everyone to pretend they did not see something. A passage even has him lose his own “don’t blink” challenge. These failures give chat leverage. [T4]

**The original activity remains embarrassingly visible.** In one exchange, the performer notices the game is still waiting for him to start it during the elaborate detour. That is the whole premise in miniature: the host who gave up on elaborate performance is delaying the game with another elaborate performance. [T4]

### How to vary it

Use a warning without a hit. Offer the microphone first. Let the audience’s complaint be painfully ordinary. Have the effect fail while TP tries to maintain authority. Let him choose the game over the bait, as he actually does in a bonus stream. [T7]

Do not repeat the entire sequence every time someone types a bathroom word. Repetition should let viewers anticipate a possibility; it should not turn the stream into an automatic response machine.

## 8. Voice and dialogue

### The core sound

TP sounds like someone speaking immediately to people in the room. His language is casual, profane, interrupted, repetitive, and full of practical adjustments: “guys,” “bro,” “hold on,” “listen,” “one second,” “go ahead,” “sorry.”

Those words are useful because of what they do. “Hold on” stops the current activity and claims attention. “Listen” makes a trivial subject official. “Are you okay?” changes aggression into caretaking. “Go ahead” appears to give the floor away while the host still controls the scene.

The voice should not become eloquent just because the concept is meta. The analysis can be sophisticated; TP’s line may simply be, “No. You don't get a square.”

### Four common transitions

**Relaxed host → serious boundary:** He has been chatting normally; now everyone must understand one important thing.

**Aggressor → worried helper:** The camera is disturbed; he asks what is wrong with the viewers.

**Confident bit → visible repair:** He notices the effect, timing, or words came out wrong and fixes it in front of them.

**Complaint → hospitality:** He wants to hear their concerns, thanks them, welcomes a newcomer, or asks what they want to play.

These transitions distinguish him more reliably than a list of opinions.

### Writing rules

- Begin with a specific comment or practical need.
- Prefer direct “you” address to abstract commentary about “the audience.”
- Let him repeat a phrase when trying to regain control, not merely to fill space.
- Keep apologies, repairs, and mistakes available. He is not a perfectly controlled menace.
- Allow a sincere sentence to remain sincere. Not every thank-you needs a sinister reveal.
- Use vulgarity as part of the delivery, not as the sole source of character.
- Preserve the difference between an improvised absurd claim and a lasting statement about the world.
- A scripted version should retain enough interruption to feel responsive while trimming endless transcript filler.
- Do not give him Ghost’s sustained tragic monologues, Carl’s specific alien history, or Linda’s corporate agenda by default.

## 9. Visual and physical performance

**Established anchors:** a toilet-paper roll; a baseball bat used in the camera-hit bit; a microphone offered to chat; smoking/bong business in some performances; movement and viewpoint changes that support proximity; effects discussed as blur, wobble, color changes, and damaged or restored vision. [T1, T4–T7]

No dedicated TP model sheet was identified among the supplied `ref` filenames. The transcripts are sufficient to describe his performance functions, but not to certify a complete facial design, exact dimensions, hand rig, bat model, or permanent costume. References to magazine-cutout faces during a party game should not automatically become his anatomy.

### What the image contributes

The object is mundane, disposable, and absurdly unsuited to commanding a room. It lets the performer announce an apparent retreat from production while still appearing through an unmistakable invented presentation.

The bat is a prop for the sudden reversal. The microphone is equally important: it turns force into an invitation to speak. If an adaptation keeps only the bat, it loses half of the relationship.

### Blocking recommendations

- Begin at a comfortable conversational distance so approaching the camera means something.
- Let the roll’s angle, proximity, and timing do work before adding effects.
- Direct a staged hit toward the viewer’s point of view, not vaguely into an unrelated room.
- Treat the aftermath as a conversation: look for a response, ask a question, make an adjustment.
- Keep a usable return to the normal view. The reset allows the next ordinary moment to register.
- Let a visible repair become a joke when it earns one; do not manufacture a malfunction every time.

### For an interactive adaptation

If the signature gag is implemented in a game or website, offer reduced motion and a skip/reset path, and avoid rapid flashing. These are implementation recommendations, not additional character lore. The joke can survive through sound, framing, dialogue, and a mild effect; prolonged visual discomfort is not what defines TP.

## 10. Cannabis, spontaneity, and the difference between a method and a character

Heavy cannabis use is part of the creator’s account of early TP performances. He discusses being unusually intoxicated, difficulty starting a game, and not wanting to repeat that degree of consumption. He also identifies concrete creative decisions he remembers making and the direct-address atmosphere he was trying to produce. [T3, T6]

Preserve that context without turning the bible into a consumption recipe. Being high is neither a full personality nor a requirement for reproducing the writing.

For a script, translate the observed effects into performable choices: an overextended aside, strange specificity, difficulty returning to a task, a burst of conviction, or an unexpected connection. These are techniques, not a claim that every moment was caused by intoxication.

The creator later contrasts the levels of the different character performances. Avoid flattening Carl and TP into the same “stoner voice.” TP’s distinctive situation is being outside the usual story and addressing the viewer; Carl has his own anxieties, wants, and fictional circumstances.

## 12. How to build a TP scene

Start with **an ordinary activity**, **a viewer contribution**, and **a possible detour**. Keep the ending open enough that the contribution can matter.

A small scene may need only:

1. TP announces that today is simple.
2. Chat introduces the obvious complication.
3. He invents a disproportionate procedure for dealing with it.
4. The procedure itself needs fixing.
5. They return to the activity, which has barely advanced.

The procedure can be an interview, a warning, a complaint desk, a demonstration, or something invented on the spot. It does not always need a bat.

### Write opportunities, not a transcript of imaginary spontaneity

For a live performance, prepare the premise, a few possible replies, the available props, and a way out. Do not require chat to supply one exact sentence. The reviewed material explicitly allows both baiting a premise and ignoring an offered trigger. [T4, T7]

For a fixed script, make the audience response specific enough to change the next beat. A faceless chorus that always says the convenient thing will feel less like the stream.

### Keep a low-intensity version

TP can simply play the game, respond honestly, and make a few odd choices. The extreme possibility provides tension even when it is unused. This makes returning to him sustainable without requiring every appearance to top the previous one.

### Do not write a compulsory redemption arc

He is a recurring mode more than a protagonist advancing through a single biography. An appearance can end because the game starts, the conversation changes, the host signs off, or everyone has had enough of the bit. It does not need a revelation explaining why he was paper all along.

A meaningful larger change might be that the original platform frustration no longer applies and the format is put away. That is supported by the creator’s discussion of TP becoming less necessary. [T1, T9]

### Bringing TP into the Carl adventure

The most faithful default would be an optional interlude that explicitly steps outside the adventure’s fiction: a bonus room presented as a break, an out-of-character commentary segment, or a separate menu extra. Those are proposals, not existing canon.

Let him address the player and the game presentation. Avoid quietly making him a mall resident, Linda’s experiment, Carl’s secret boss, or an omnipotent god who can rewrite the plot. Any such decision would be a deliberate new crossover premise and should be identified as one.

## 14. Original sample scene: “No Production Tonight”

*An illustrative new scene. Dialogue, exact effects, and events below are invented. It demonstrates the performance logic without adding TP to the adventure’s canon.*

The game menu sat behind the toilet-paper roll.

**TP:** We're keeping it simple tonight. No plot. No big production. I'm going to click this and we're going to play.

**CHAT:** Can I have two squares?

TP stopped beside the start button.

**TP:** We need to have a conversation.

**CHAT:** You said no plot.

**TP:** This isn't a plot. This is something you need to understand about visiting somebody.

He moved closer.

**TP:** You don't come into someone's stream and ask for pieces of them.

**CHAT:** One square?

A prop bat crossed the frame. The picture tilted slightly.

**TP:** Are you okay?

**CHAT:** You did that.

**TP:** I'm asking how you are. You can answer a question without starting another thing.

He adjusted the view. It tilted the other way.

**TP:** Hold on. No. That's worse.

The normal picture returned.

**TP:** There. And I do want you to feel heard.

He brought out a microphone.

**TP:** Go ahead. Tell the people at home what you need.

**CHAT:** I need you to start the game.

TP looked back at the menu.

**TP:** Yeah. Fair enough.

He put the microphone away.

**TP:** This is why we're not doing a production tonight. Takes forever.

He clicked Start.

**Why this works:** The “no production” promise is contradicted by the detour. The viewer’s comment gives the action a cause. TP becomes the source of disturbance, the concerned helper, and the apparent defender of audience participation. Chat supplies a practical correction that ends the bit. The ordinary game remains there throughout.

## 16. Writer’s checklist

- Is TP outside the normal continuity unless this is an explicitly chosen crossover?
- What ordinary activity gives the scene its baseline?
- What viewer contribution actually changes the scene?
- Does the denial of performance coexist with visible performance?
- Is he addressing the viewer rather than merely talking about viewers?
- Does any camera effect have a conversational purpose?
- Have I retained warmth, gratitude, or ordinary interaction alongside confrontation?
- Is a failure or interruption allowed to change his approach?
- Is the signature bit optional rather than automatic?
- Am I borrowing specific opinions or personal history without enough context?
- Can the scene return to the game or conversation without a lore explanation?
- Does this feel like TP, rather than Carl with a different model or Ghost without a sheet?
