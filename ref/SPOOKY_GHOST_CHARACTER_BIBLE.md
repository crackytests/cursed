# SPOOKY GHOST — CHARACTER BIBLE & WRITING GUIDE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

For writing Spooky Ghost in streams, scripted scenes, comics, and the Carl adventure game. Companion to the [Carl bible](J:/carladv/CARL_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md) and [CEO Linda bible](J:/carladv/CEO_LINDA_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md).

This guide draws on targeted searches across the supplied transcript archive and close reading of relevant creator explanations and performances. It separates established characterization, reported events, changing continuity, and suggested writing choices. It is comprehensive about the material reviewed, not a claim that every improvised remark across the archive has been reconciled.

**Central writing principle, synthesized from the evidence:** Spooky Ghost wants his life, death, and every subsequent inconvenience to add up to a great story about him. He has real talent and real feelings, but recognition is easier for him to pursue than responsibility.

The essential contradiction is explicit in the creator’s commentary: **he is the soul of the stream, even though he is self-centered and can be a douchebag.** Soul does not mean moral perfection. It means the person who supplies something indispensable is also an unreliable, theatrical, wounded individual. [S1]

## 1. Spooky Ghost at a glance

Spooky Ghost is the spirit of an alternate version of Cracky Reeds: a man who pursued magic and showmanship rather than following the original Face’s primarily technological path. The two collaborated on the system. Face supplies a backed-up human mind; Spooky Ghost supplies the soul and, in a detailed origin account, the knowledge that makes travel through time and realities possible. [S2–S3]

As a living man, he wanted to be a magician and musician. He wanted greatness, loved the road, and did not settle down when the woman who became CEO Linda wanted him to. He later wanted a different outcome, too late. Death did not end his longing, vanity, or domestic problems: he brought back his ex-wives, acquired the running problem of spooky alimony, and became entangled in the Lindas’ soul origins. [S3–S5]

He can be a co-inventor, presenter, haunted narrator, jealous rival, runaway, reluctant employee, antagonist, and the person the others are trying to rescue. His grand entrance can block the entire screen. His cosmic distress can turn out to concern being passed over for promotion. He can speak about his inner life at length and still refuse the simplest admission of fault. [S6–S10]

The creator’s direct character notes describe him as **angsty, self-reflective, somewhat of a loser, melodramatic, weird, and a showboat**. They also express affection for him. Preserve both halves: he is ridiculous, and he is worth spending time with. [S6]

**One-sentence brief:** A talented, lonely showman who became a real ghost keeps trying to turn disappointment into his next great performance, while the people and consequences he escaped keep catching up with him.

**Comic engine:** A supernatural being with access to extraordinary possibilities still cannot handle ordinary humiliation gracefully.

**Emotional engine:** He wants to matter, to be understood, and to be chosen; he often pursues those things in ways that make living with him harder.

**Dramatic danger:** His evasions can hurt people. He has more than a history of harmless boasting.

## 2. Evidence and canon rules

The archive mixes performance, creator explanation, audience suggestions, games being played, generated recaps, songs, and technical setup. A single transcript may contain all of them without useful speaker labels.

Use this order of confidence:

1. Direct creator explanation of the character, especially an explicit correction.
2. Repeated lore and clearly described story events.
3. Identifiable in-character performance, while accounting for jokes and unreliable claims.
4. Retrospective recollection, with its visible uncertainty preserved.
5. Proposals, audience suggestions, art variations, adaptations, and generated material.

**Labels in this bible:** established characterization; reported event; developing continuity; interpretation; proposal; original example. The emotional model and writing recommendations are synthesis, not a recovered private diary.

“Sweet Ghost,” “Swooie Ghost,” and similar forms frequently appear in the transcripts around plainly identifiable Spooky Ghost references. Treat them as likely transcription variations in those contexts, not proof of a separate cast of named ghosts. Do not automatically normalize every unrelated use of “ghost.”

A game protagonist’s marriage, dialogue, powers, or biography does not become Spooky Ghost’s because he is playing or narrating the game. Likewise, the performer’s real-world grievances should not automatically become a complete fictional biography. The stream deliberately transforms some of that material into character stories; look for the transformation rather than importing everything.

## 4. Human history and major story phases

This is a relative map, not a settled calendar. Transcript bundle numbers are not an in-universe timeline. Reboots and retrospective refinements change how some events are explained.

### A. The aspiring performer

The creator describes his human life through amateur magic, music, showmanship, and the pursuit of greatness. He pursued the black arts in an account that contrasts him with the scientifically oriented Face. The creator briefly reaches for celebrity as a description, then refines it toward wanting to be a musician and magician. [S3]

Write ambition more specifically than “he wanted fame.” He wanted to do something impressive and be seen doing it. The performance and the audience’s recognition both matter.

He loved the road. His first wife wanted to settle down; he did not at the time. The account later gives him a belated wish to settle down when it was too late. That is a strong source of regret, but it does not supply exact dates, venues, achievements, or the full history of every marriage. [S4]

### B. The woman who became CEO Linda

CEO Linda’s soul is identified as his first ex-wife, from the same reality. “Linda” and “Spooky Ghost” are not established as their literal names while alive. The creator uses those names to tell the backstory. [S5, S11]

He talked about marijuana growing, AI companions, and the company-name joke that she put into practice. She built businesses from material he had discussed but not executed. The creator explicitly distinguishes **her own Fascism Inc.** from the protagonists’ company. She did not simply steal their company. [S5]

Keep the distinction between inspiration and accomplishment. He may feel that he deserves credit; the narrative should still recognize her labor and decisions. Conversely, his failure to execute these business ideas does not cancel his separate, significant contribution to the system.

### C. The collaboration with Face

The original technological project sought a way to fight the future AI threat through survival as a backed-up mind and/or travel through time. Different versions of the underlying person pursued complementary routes. [S2]

A detailed explanation says Ghost’s human antecedent discovered travel across time and realities through specific formulas and sigils, described as working rather like mathematics. He helped translate that knowledge into the system, which was built in a way that needed him. This is genuine competence, not merely fraudulent stage magic. [S3]

The same account describes the collaborators as old men finishing their work, with ritual attempts to secure a soul. The original Face attempt fails and becomes the Curse; Ghost’s soul supplies the successful connection. Other brief retellings simplify this to capturing his soul when he dies, and one hesitates over whether the human death was chosen. Preserve the successful soul connection without inventing an exact, agreed death scene. [S2–S3, S4]

### D. The afterlife does not fix his love life

He brings back his ex-wives because he is lonely, in both retrospective lore and a first-person performance. Face warns him against doing it. Ghost ignores the warning, then ends up complaining about the consequences. [S4, S7]

The ex-wife souls become available for the Linda systems in the wider lore. Spooky alimony becomes a long-running burden he tries to avoid. CEO Linda has a more specific self-built-system account as well; her bible records the unresolved tension between that account and generic captured-system explanations. Do not repair the cosmology by erasing either the recurring ex-wife-soul premise or her individual accomplishments. [S1, S5]

The comedy of alimony does not establish a complete supernatural legal code. Nor does it prove every wife is equally culpable, identical, or interchangeable. His version of the story is self-serving.

### E. Taking Face’s place

Several retrospective accounts say Ghost killed or displaced Face, took his place, and appeared as a strange red Face. Escaping his ex-wives and alimony is given as a motive. Yoko is described as bringing Face back. Some retellings qualify “killed”; keep the reported harm and the revival rather than flattening it into either harmless costume play or a permanently final death. [S4, S8]

A later creator explanation adds an important motive: Ghost was trying to show up old Face by doing the things Face had done. It also links that rivalry to managing his guilt, while saying he knew Face would return. He repeats an earlier Pee Kid-related feat and makes his own potential-piss generator in that account. [S9]

The strongest writing point is not the gadget’s name. It is that he tries to prove he deserves the position he took. Do not turn that into secret justification for hurting his collaborator.

### F. Recognition, departure, and the search

The stream has a Revenge/Return of Spooky Ghost storyline associated with his grievance about Kick and front-page attention. A later Christmas appearance still has him complaining about it after the host says they have moved on. He suspects haters in chat and demands space for his presence. [S10]

This is useful evidence of how a grievance outlives its practical relevance. Do not treat every accusation against a real platform or individual in these performances as independently verified fact.

A retrospective describes him floating away into space and saying goodbye to Carl, followed by a search likened to the search for Spock. Carl infiltrates Linda’s system to obtain material needed to find or save Ghost. One recollection is vague about the stolen plans; another specifies that Linda had a way to track him that the others lacked. [S14]

**For the adventure adaptation:** Ghost is the reason the retrieval matters. It is not established that he personally designed every step of Carl’s infiltration or that the raid itself immediately completed the rescue.

### G. The attempt to avoid Linda and the mall trap

A clear recap separates two intentions. Ghost wanted to go back and prevent himself from meeting the original Linda. Face intended to kill her as a toddler. They did not reach that past: Linda’s trap diverted them into the Mall of the Future. Do not attribute Face’s intended child murder to Ghost. [S15]

The mall contains an eighties department. A later idea has current Ghost and current Linda living out a fake-eighties relationship there. That is explicitly proposed material in the passage reviewed, not a confirmed reconciliation. [S15]

Ghost has also been made to work the mall intercom, according to a recollection. A proposal about who attaches him to the mall remains unsettled in its own exchange. Being stuck, being needed by the system, and being assigned work are related story pressures, but they should not be collapsed into one invented binding spell. [S3, S6]

### H. Reboots and changing present circumstances

New Face is explicitly described as not initially knowing Ghost. In that version, New Face learns time travel through recovered Yoko technology, rather than through the original Ghost partnership. He has unexplained or blended memories of the old stream, which the creator discusses through a merging-of-versions premise. [S11]

Other passages propose Ghost as an occasional host, narrator, game master, or a character attached to the back of the screen. Treat these as different presentation states and plans. Before writing an episode, choose which Face exists, whether Ghost is present or missing, and what connection to the system is currently functioning. [S12, S16]

## 5. His inner logic

The following is a writing interpretation grounded in the repeated behavior and direct character notes. It is not a psychological diagnosis.

### He wants recognition to confirm that the dream was worth it

He wants to be seen as a great performer, an essential contributor, or the person who can do what Face did. Applause offers an answer to the fear that his choices did not amount to enough.

This gives his vanity stakes. A missing guest, a failed entrance, or an audience that does not appreciate him can feel larger to him than it looks to everyone else. The writer does not have to agree with his sense of scale.

### He wants connection, but struggles with its obligations

The man who would not settle down becomes a ghost lonely enough to bring his ex-wives back. He wants company, but the renewed relationships produce claims on him. His response often becomes escape, complaint, or a revised story about who is to blame.

Do not write him as incapable of affection. His problem is more interesting if the affection is real and his behavior still hurts people.

### He studies himself without reliably changing

Self-reflection and self-awareness are different. He can narrate his disappointment, recognize a pattern, or briefly admit Face knew something, then retreat into “whatever.” [S7]

Let an insight be genuine even if he fails to act on it. He is not necessarily pretending to feel. He may simply prefer the moving speech about learning to the uncomfortable action learning requires.

### He needs to be impressive, not merely useful

The soul connection and his role in time travel make him useful. But usefulness behind the scenes is not always enough for the showman. He wants a turn at the front, credit for the result, and perhaps proof that the others could not have done it without him.

Do not assume he deliberately sabotaged the system to force that dependency. The source says it needs him; the motive for that design is not established.

### He fears an ordinary verdict on an extraordinary life

A strong inferred fear is that people will look at the magic, the time travel, the death, and the speeches, and still conclude that he failed to show up for someone. He can make the circumstances cosmic, but the complaint remains personal.

Use that pressure selectively. Not every scene needs an exposed wound. Sometimes he simply wants his music played and the chat to stop interrupting.

## 6. Relationships: a different need from each person

### Original Face: collaborator, audience, rival, person he wronged

They are not merely the sensible one and the idiot. Face’s technical work and Ghost’s supernatural contribution complement one another. Ghost can understand something Face needs. Their relationship has a real foundation beneath its rivalry. [S2–S3]

That makes the takeover more consequential. Ghost does not simply want an empty throne; he wants to demonstrate he can occupy Face’s role. Repeating Face’s achievements can become a substitute for dealing with what he did to him. [S9]

**Write:** Practical collaboration interrupted by a dispute over credit, a warning Ghost resents, or the need to prove himself. Let Face sometimes require Ghost’s expertise and Ghost sometimes resent being asked only when that expertise is useful.

**Avoid:** Ghost as Face’s powerless mascot; Face as a permanently innocent moral authority; immediate forgiveness because Ghost expected him to return.

### New Face: familiarity without a simple shared past

The archive explicitly says New Face initially does not know Ghost. That is an opportunity for uneven recognition, but it is not permission to invent every person’s memories. Establish what each character knows in the chosen phase. [S11]

A useful scene might have Ghost expecting an old response and discovering this Face does not provide it. That is an adaptation possibility, not a recovered event. Do not write a decades-long friendship into their first meeting by accident.

### CEO Linda: the person who remembers what his dream cost

She is his first ex-wife, but she is also an accomplished person who followed through on ideas he discussed. Their conflict can combine affection, resentment, embarrassment, credit, autonomy, and the practical question of who now controls their circumstances. [S4–S5]

He may wish to erase the meeting rather than kill her. He may insist he never wants to see her again. He may still have feelings in a tentative explanation. These are not all a single stable confession. Let the immediate scene choose which impulse is active without pretending the others cannot exist. [S4, S7, S15]

**Write:** He describes the possibilities; she asks what he actually did. Or she reduces his grand performance to work he must finish. He can push back on her control without becoming right about every part of their history.

**Avoid:** Treating Linda as an unfair burden solely because he complains; treating him as the true owner of her achievements; reducing their whole relationship to a generic nagging-wife routine; automatic reconciliation.

### The other ex-wives and Lindas: consequences are people

He brought back former partners because he wanted company and was warned against it. His first-person account admits that much before deflecting again. [S7]

Give his evasions comic energy, but do not let his adjectives become the bible’s verdict on the women. The number of marriages, identities, and the full mapping of souls to Lindas are not fixed by these excerpts. Spooky child-support jokes are not evidence that he has children; a performed exchange instead says he has none. [S17]

### Carl: the unlikely person who came looking

Carl’s role in the search gives their relationship a concrete emotional fact: the character Ghost might dismiss as chaotic was involved in helping him. The retrospective reaction, “Wait, Carl saved me?”, is a useful pivot between gratitude, surprise, and wounded self-importance. It is a reaction in a recollection, not proof that Ghost permanently forgot the event. [S14]

**Write:** Ghost wants his predicament treated with appropriate grandeur; Carl wants the practical problem solved or his immediate needs met. Carl can puncture the performance without being cruel. Ghost can appreciate help while struggling to say a plain thank-you.

**Avoid:** Making Carl his obedient servant, making Ghost omniscient about Carl’s mission, or turning their affection into a sentimental relationship the sources have not established.

### Yoko: essential help, independent presence

Yoko is described as restoring Face after the takeover. In the wider system explanation, she was also an earlier attempt to solve the soul problem, though she did not supply the needed stabilizing function in that account. These are her roles; they do not make her simply Ghost’s replacement or an inferior copy of him. [S1, S3, S8]

In performance he asks her for assistance, reacts to her apparent unresponsiveness, and tries to make the show continue. A hosting scene makes her silence part of his unraveling evening. [S7, S18]

**Write:** He needs an answer, a cue, or help preserving the performance. Let Yoko’s actual behavior matter. Do not invent a romance or permanent rivalry from his general complaints about “lady trouble.”

### Audience: witness, accomplice, critic

He addresses viewers directly, wants them to appreciate his show, and can interpret criticism as evidence of haters. A failed entrance becomes more painful because they saw it. In a hosting performance, a letter about performing for his own reflection prompts him to argue that nobody can appreciate him better than himself. [S10, S17–S18]

The audience can enjoy him while refusing his preferred account of himself. That is a productive relationship: the viewers do not need to hate him, and he does not need to correctly recognize their affection every time.

### JB Garfield and the surrounding cast

One Garfield performance describes liking Ghost’s episodes while finding him “a bit too much,” and distinguishes seeing him pass by from actually socializing with him. That is a useful limit on inventing close friendships among everyone in the same setting. [S17]

Ghost can be a conspicuous presence in somebody else’s life without being their confidant. The degree of familiarity should be established in the scene.

## 7. Voice: the grand feeling and the ordinary complaint

### The core sound

His voice is conversational underneath the melodrama. He says “bro,” corrects himself, repeats points, argues with chat, swears, and moves between presenting and complaining. A theatrical turn should grow out of a recognizable person talking, not replace him with a permanently archaic phantom.

The creator explicitly calls him self-reflective and melodramatic, and says he suits horror narration. Another explanation calls him a showboat. That combination is more useful than a generic “spooky voice.” [S6, S16]

**Default rhythm:** Frame an experience as profound → explain why it concerns him → encounter a mundane interruption → defend his dignity in plainer, shorter language.

Not every line should follow that rhythm. A whole scene of eloquent monologues will miss his interruptions, irritation, and practical confusion.

### Four useful registers

**The presenter.** He announces, introduces, promises, waits for the music, and tries to get the audience into the right mood. He may have a guest, a show, or a special appearance to sell. Confidence can be fragile beneath the format.

**The reflective ghost.** He thinks aloud about what his experiences mean. Use an image or concrete memory before an abstract conclusion. Let a thought land occasionally; constant undercutting makes his emotional life weightless.

**The aggrieved performer.** He repeats the unfairness, appeals to the audience, suspects insufficient appreciation, and expands a setback into a larger story. The grievance can remain active after everyone else has moved on.

**The exposed person.** An admission comes through briefly: he was lonely, ignored a warning, needed someone, or wanted the dream to work. Do not immediately cover every admission with a joke, but do not make one moment of honesty a complete personality cure.

### Sentence-level rules

- Give the thought a subject: a failed show, an old promise, being stuck, someone else getting credit.
- Let “I” and “me” recur because his experience is the frame. Do not make every sentence explicitly announce his greatness.
- Use repetition when he is defending, stalling, or trying to regain control.
- A self-correction can expose the motive he hoped to conceal.
- Ground elevated language with a mundane detail. He may lament eternity while waiting for a cue.
- He can speak competently and precisely about something he knows. Do not make all explanations nonsense.
- Profanity is part of the conversational texture; escalating volume is not a substitute for a joke.
- Avoid constant ghost puns, “boo,” chains, or faux-Victorian diction unless a specific performance calls for them.
- Keep enough hesitation to suggest live thought, but edit transcript filler for scripted work.
- Distinguish the line he would say from the creator’s explanation of why he says it.

**Quick test:** If removing “ghost” leaves an interchangeable sad wizard, add showmanship, a practical grievance, a particular relationship, or a need for credit.

## 8. Appearance and performance

There is no dedicated Spooky Ghost portrait identified among the supplied `ref` filenames. The current files explicitly provide Carl and Linda references plus infiltration slides. This bible therefore does not invent a definitive color palette, facial anatomy, clothing design, or human likeness for Ghost.

The transcripts do establish a recognizable ghost presentation. Creator comments describe the sheet-like appearance as a ghost and reject viewers’ repeated attempts to interpret it as a racist costume. Another passage explains that the model squishes, stretches, and moves across the screen through manually built head tracking. [S19]

The original interruption bit has him descend from above, shake to communicate, and take up enough screen space to obstruct the host. The host voices or interprets the other side of the exchange. That is a presentation mode, not a universal rule that Ghost cannot speak: he also hosts in first person. [S17, S19]

**Staging rules:**

- His entrance is an event to him. Let its scale sometimes exceed its importance to everyone else.
- Use floating, sliding, squash, and stretch to convey his reactions where appropriate to the model.
- A great pose held too long can become funny when the music runs out.
- Blocking the host or the screen makes attention-seeking physical.
- When the act fails, reduce the grandeur: a pause, an ordinary complaint, an attempt to restart.
- For a sincere beat, stillness can be more effective than adding more effects.

Santa hats, Halloween outfits, robot variants, and other art treatments occur or are discussed. They do not automatically define his standard costume or prove that a new incarnation has entered the story. One art discussion explicitly leaves alternate-world interpretations open. [S6, S13, S19]

**Practical constraint for the game:** Decide why the current problem requires Carl. Distance, a damaged connection, unavailable information, or a specific barrier could work, but those are adaptation choices until established. “He is a ghost” alone does not answer whether he can enter Linda’s system, retrieve information, or escape it.

The best limit is one that creates a decision or dependency. If Ghost can solve the situation with no cost and no obstacle, the writer must explain why he has not done so. Vanity can motivate a bad choice; it should not be the only explanation for every technical limitation.

## 10. Comedy, sympathy, and harm

### The entrance is more organized than the life

He can care intensely about a cue, an image, or a dramatic return while the actual plan remains shaky. The transcript’s hosting sequence gives him cue cards, a missing guest, awkward sponsorship, and insulting fan mail. He repeatedly attempts to obey the show’s format rather than confront its collapse. [S18]

Let some of the organization work. An entrance that succeeds makes the eventual mundane objection sharper. If every trick fails, he stops feeling like a performer with a fragile ego and becomes a random incompetent.

### A cosmic escape from an ordinary obligation

A ghost trying to avoid alimony, a time traveler trying to prevent an awkward relationship, and a soul trying to get better billing all put extraordinary means beside ordinary motives. The ordinary motive keeps the joke legible.

Do not over-explain the supernatural bureaucracy. The recurring obligation is enough unless a story actually needs its rules.

### He narrates his own defense

He can tell the whole story accurately except for the moral emphasis. He was lonely; he brought people back; he was warned. Somehow the conclusion he wants is still that everyone is being unfair to him.

Let another character expose the missing implication with a concrete question. They do not need to out-monologue him.

### He can be moving without becoming admirable

A person may have real regret and still dodge accountability. Give him a moment where he recognizes the cost of something. Whether he acts on it is the next dramatic question.

His reported harm to Face should not disappear because the bit is funny. Likewise, Face’s own dark intentions should not be reassigned to Ghost merely to make him the designated bad one. Keep individual actions attached to the person who took or proposed them.

### He deserves more than humiliation

The creator likes him and recognizes his reflective, strange appeal. Let him discover something, hold an audience, make an accurate observation, or help someone. His vanity becomes more interesting when he actually has something to be proud of.

Do not give every scene the same ending where everyone laughs and he leaves. He can win the moment, lose the larger argument, or quietly make a better choice than expected.

## 11. Building a Spooky Ghost scene

Start with two questions: **What does he need to happen, and how does he want people to remember it?** Those goals may conflict.

A useful scene shape:

1. He frames the situation as a performance, revelation, injustice, or personal turning point.
2. A practical fact interrupts that framing.
3. He tries a real skill, a persuasive explanation, or a theatrical distraction.
4. Another person identifies what he is avoiding.
5. He either accepts a limited responsibility or preserves his self-image at a visible cost.

This is a tool, not a formula for every appearance. Sometimes all the scene needs is his entrance, one grievance, and the host turning the music off.

### As a quest-giver or missing character

For the Carl game, the search gives him importance before he appears. A message can be useful and self-dramatizing at once. Let the useful part be clear enough that the player can act; character flavor should not obscure every instruction.

If he is missing, use evidence of him sparingly: a known performance cue, an unfinished message, or somebody’s recollection. New recordings and clues are adaptation material. Do not present invented lines as transcript discoveries.

When he returns, allow the fact that Carl helped to alter the scene. A reluctant thank-you, an attempt to reframe the rescue, or a genuinely helpful response can each fit. Choose based on the intended arc rather than defaulting to total ingratitude.

### As an ally in a puzzle

Give him expertise the player lacks and a constraint the player can address. His occult knowledge can explain the mechanism; Carl can handle a physical task or take an approach Ghost overlooked. Specify those permissions in the game rather than relying on generic ghost lore.

His vanity can complicate cooperation: he wants the credit, the correct introduction, or the chance to demonstrate the impressive part himself. Avoid making the player endure repeated unskippable speeches to communicate that trait.

### As a rival or antagonist

Use a motive connected to his history: escape, recognition, avoiding an obligation, proving he is as capable as Face, or preventing another humiliation. Let him pursue a coherent plan with an ethically bad premise.

He need not become a cackling destroyer. A seemingly reasonable explanation of why he deserves this chance can be more specific and more uncomfortable.

### As a horror narrator

The creator explicitly considers him suited to scary, reflective narration. Let him attend to an unsettling detail and what it means to him. The supernatural can be familiar without being completely understood. [S16]

Do not make him the authoritative answer to every mystery. He can know that something is wrong and still misread its cause. His own unresolved state gives him a reason to care about questions of selfhood, survival, and memory.

### In a human flashback

Begin before the verdict. Let the magician be excited about a technique, a show, the road, or a shared future he thinks he can somehow postpone without losing. The audience may see the flaw more clearly than he does.

Do not write every promise as a calculated lie. An honestly meant promise he does not fulfill can be more faithful to the dreamer than a secret plan to abandon everyone.

### In an actual moment of growth

Give him a small action that cannot be substituted with a speech: stay, finish the task, acknowledge someone’s contribution, admit what happened without a qualification, or ask rather than assume.

These are proposed directions for writing, not completed canon. Growth should cost him something he values, such as the flattering version of the story. It does not require deleting his theatricality.

## 13. Original sample scene: “Before the Music”

*An illustrative new scene, not a recovered episode. The object, mechanism, and events are invented. It demonstrates a possible post-retrieval encounter rather than declaring the canonical ending of Carl’s raid.*

Spooky Ghost hovered above the inactive console.

**GHOST:** There comes a point, Carl, when a man has to accept that nobody is coming for him.

**CARL:** I came for you.

**GHOST:** I know. I'm talking about the point before that.

Carl put a small black case on the console.

**CARL:** Linda had this.

Ghost came closer.

**GHOST:** You got it?

**CARL:** Yeah.

**GHOST:** You actually got it.

**CARL:** I said yeah.

**GHOST:** Bro. I had a whole thing prepared.

**CARL:** You can do it after.

Ghost examined the markings inside the case.

**GHOST:** Don't turn that one. The order matters.

**CARL:** This one?

**GHOST:** The one you're touching, yes. Stop touching it.

He read the sequence aloud. Carl worked the switches. The console lit up.

**CARL:** See? We did it.

**GHOST:** We did.

Ghost watched the lights for a moment.

**GHOST:** Face would have had you try the other sequence.

**CARL:** He wasn't here.

**GHOST:** I'm just saying.

**CARL:** You were here. I got the thing. It worked.

Ghost started to reply, then stopped.

**GHOST:** Yeah. It worked.

**CARL:** Can we go now?

**GHOST:** Thank you, Carl.

**CARL:** Okay. Can we go?

**GHOST:** I did have music for this.

**CARL:** Play it while we're leaving.

**GHOST:** Yeah. All right. That could work.

**Why this works:** His emotional framing runs ahead of the practical situation, but his expertise is real. Carl provides both the object and the correction to Ghost’s story about himself. Ghost reaches for rivalry, then manages a small, uncomplicated thank-you. He keeps his taste for performance without making everyone wait for it. This is a possible growth beat, not proof that every Ghost scene should end warmly.

## 15. Writer’s checklist

Before approving a scene, check:

- What does Ghost need, and what story does he want to tell about getting it?
- Is there a concrete situation beneath the melodrama?
- Does he have a real skill, contribution, or observation?
- Have I confused introspection with accountability?
- Does another person get to exist outside his interpretation of them?
- Is the scene’s Face original Face, New Face, or another clearly identified version?
- Have I kept the separate souls, the Curse, and the future robot variants distinct?
- Am I giving Linda credit for what she actually built?
- If Carl helps, does that fact register, even awkwardly?
- Is the dialogue conversational enough to sound performed rather than carved on a tomb?
- Does the humor allow affection without excusing harm?
- Have I made the necessary power limits clear?
- Have I labeled new events, abilities, and sample dialogue as adaptation material?
- Is the ending more specific than everyone calling him a loser?

### Compact brief to paste above a script

> Spooky Ghost is an alternate Cracky Reeds who pursued magic, music, and showmanship, then became the soul associated with the system. He contributed real knowledge of time and reality travel, but he is also angsty, self-centered, reflective, melodramatic, and hungry for recognition. He loved the road, disappointed the woman who became CEO Linda, brought back ex-wives out of loneliness, evaded spooky alimony, and has a serious history of rivalry and harm toward original Face. Write a conversational showman whose great speeches meet ordinary consequences. He can understand himself without changing, and he can be sincerely moved without becoming virtuous. Give him real competence, a specific need, a real limit, and someone who challenges his preferred version of the story. Keep original Face, New Face, the Curse, and the Spooky Ones distinct.
