# CARL — CHARACTER BIBLE & WRITING GUIDE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

For writing Carl in streams, scripted scenes, comics, and the adventure game. This bible draws on targeted searches across the 229 transcript bundles and close reading of relevant passages. It is comprehensive about the evidence assembled here, not a claim that every improvised line has been recovered or reconciled.

**The governing rule:** Carl lets anxiety steer. He says the thought, follows the objection, and does the thing that a more restrained version of him would stop himself from doing. His ability to make a situation worse is inseparable from his ability to make something happen.

## 1. Carl at a glance

Carl is a visibly alien delivery driver from “above and below Nevada” who would appreciate everyone agreeing that he is a normal human. He escaped an underground facility with help from an older version of Face. He became the Prop Pills delivery driver, travels in his Alien Starship—the A.S.S.—and spends a recurring stretch of the story parked outside the Mall of the Future because he cannot stop making trouble inside it.

He smokes weed, hangs out with chat, argues, plays games, gets sidetracked, and resists being told what to do. Under that is an unresolved search for his parents, uncertainty about who can be trusted across the stream’s changing incarnations, and a demonstrated willingness to go into danger for the stream’s people.

He is funny because his defenses are visible. A question about a giant bong becomes an argument about discrimination against short people. Being unmistakably alien becomes a lengthy explanation of why he is obviously human. A rule becomes a personal challenge. The explanation keeps growing until it creates a new problem.

**One-sentence writing brief:** A defensive, impulsive alien trying to have a normal time keeps talking and acting past the point where he should stop, then recruits chat to help deal with the consequences.

**What keeps him sympathetic:** He wants company, freedom, and somewhere to belong; he is capable of warmth and useful action, even when he is exhausting to be around. Those emotional priorities are a writing interpretation of his conduct, not a newly declared backstory.

**What must survive every adaptation:** the anxious thought process, the audience relationship, the gap between his self-description and his behavior, and enough sincerity for his relationships to matter. [S1–S4]

## 2. How to read canon in this bible

The transcripts combine creator commentary, character performance, viewer messages, game dialogue, songs, quoted videos, and transcription errors. A statement appearing in a transcript does not by itself make it a Carl fact.

- **Established:** repeated lore or a clear creator explanation supported by context.
- **Reported event:** a character or creator recalls something happening; details may still vary.
- **Recurring bit:** an established performance pattern whose literal explanation can change.
- **Proposal or variant:** an idea discussed for another format, alternate reality, or future stream.
- **Interpretation:** a useful writing conclusion derived from the above.
- **Unknown:** the sources do not settle it.

Creator explanations of how Carl is performed carry particular weight. Casual audience accusations do not automatically become his biography. A game character named Carl does not automatically donate that game’s backstory to him.

The previously generated adventure game and `Outlive_Stream_Retheme.md` are adaptations. They are not independent evidence of stream canon. Newly written examples in this bible are labeled and should not be mistaken for transcript quotations.

## 4. Origin and continuity

### The real-world creation story

The creator describes Carl as an improvised VTuber experiment. He began as the Prop Pills delivery driver and developed during a Desert Bus stream lasting roughly eight hours, possibly nine in the recollection. The deliberately uneventful game forced him to talk to himself, talk to chat, become increasingly frayed, and self-reflect. The creator explicitly connects that experience to why Carl became the character he is. [S4]

This is a major writing clue: **Carl can generate a scene from waiting, inconvenience, and company.** He does not need a new explosion or lore reveal every thirty seconds.

### The in-universe sequence

This is a relative sequence, not a dated timeline. The bundle numbers are not a reliable chronology, and many episode dates are marked unknown.

1. **Arrival and the missing family.** Carl associates his origin with coming from above Nevada and ending up below it. His parents’ location and his life before the facility remain uncertain.
2. **Life in the facility.** He describes an underground lab, bunker, or shelter. One account says it felt like a dungeon. He fears being found and asks viewers to deny knowing him if men in white suits arrive. [S6–S7]
3. **Face helps him escape.** An older version of Face frees him. Their association leads to his delivery work. [S7, S9]
4. **Prop Pills employment.** He becomes the delivery driver for the stream’s fake sponsor. One account frames the arrangement as a debt to Face, weed in place of meaningful pay, and a promise that he will be paid if the stream makes money. Carl complains, then recasts it as an investment in the future. [S11]
5. **The business becomes unstable.** Prop Pills is described as getting into trouble because its product works better than placebo after claiming only placebo-level effectiveness. One account adds occasional dark-web deliveries. Other passages discuss its absence, return, or Linda’s ownership. Preserve the era rather than forcing one permanent employment status. [S12]
6. **The mall and the parking lot.** Carl parks his ship outside the Mall of the Future. Smoking, breaking bongs, and doing the opposite of what Linda tells him explain why he keeps getting kicked out or barred. [S9–S10]
7. **The search for Spooky Ghost.** Carl infiltrates Linda’s system to obtain information that can help locate Spooky Ghost. Recollections describe a blackjack, prior Thief practice, audience-directed action, and simplified Fate-style dice. Linda had a way to track the ghost that the others lacked. [S13–S14]
8. **Continuity across changing seasons.** A reboot discussion says Carl remains the same Carl and tentatively suggests he may remember the older seasons. In-character, he is uncertain whether a new Face is the person who helped him before. The personal uncertainty is firmer than any claim that Carl possesses perfect continuity knowledge. [S7, S15]

### Prop Pills: get the joke right

The recurring sponsor formulation identifies Prop Pills as a subsidiary of Pretend Co., “the pretend company, a real company.” Transcription mangles the company’s spelling in several places; **Pretend Co. is the working normalization, not a verified official logo spelling.** A later passage links the company to Fascism Inc. through Linda. [S12]

The fraud joke is the reversal: the allegedly pretend pills were *more effective* than the advertised placebo. Do not flatten it into an ordinary “the pills do nothing” scam. Carl can also stumble over the sponsor line, restart it, and get annoyed that he cannot say a phrase he normally knows.

His relationship to the company is more interesting than generic corporate satire: he works for it, depends on it, complains about it, and may not even be sure who owns it anymore.

## 5. The character’s internal mechanics

### Anxiety becomes behavior

The creator repeatedly states the rule directly: Carl lets anxiety guide him, holds back less, chooses words less carefully, and gets into drawn-out fights. “Carl is anxiety” is the most compact statement of it. [S1–S3]

This is a performance instruction, not a clinical diagnosis. It does not mean Carl must tremble or announce that he is anxious. His anxiety often appears as confidence, argument, refusal, speculation, or an urgent need to correct somebody.

A useful scene pattern is:

**Something unsettles him → he explains or objects → the response unsettles him further → he doubles down or acts → consequences arrive → he argues about what those consequences mean.**

He may understand perfectly well that he should stop and continue anyway. The failure is often restraint, not intelligence.

### He wants to control how he is understood

Carl does not merely deny being an alien once. He builds a case, changes the case, recruits witnesses, and accidentally supplies more evidence against himself. His explanations reveal the concern they are supposed to conceal.

This applies beyond species: his competence, his freedom, his right to be somewhere, whether he has been insulted, and whether chat thinks he is being ridiculous.

**Writing interpretation:** Being mislabeled or handled by somebody else can feel more urgent to him than the original practical problem.

### Defiance can be self-defeating

A particularly clear description says that when Carl is told to do something, he does the opposite. The bong-breaking and mall-ban stories show the cost. [S9]

Let him sometimes be right about the unfairness and wrong about the response. Let him also sometimes invent the unfairness. His agency matters, but his judgment should not be automatically vindicated by the script.

Do not make every instruction trigger a tantrum. The reaction needs a pressure point: condescension, exclusion, surveillance, humiliation, or somebody presuming authority over him.

### He is not a fool by default

He can play complicated games, follow arguments, learn, investigate, and act effectively. His performance contains knowledgeable discussion as well as confusion. Neither stream knowledge nor game mechanics should automatically become supernatural powers in the fiction.

**Writing rule:** Give him a workable idea and a messy relationship to executing it. A smart observation followed by an avoidable escalation is more characteristic than constant random incompetence.

### His warmth is real

The material includes greetings, concern for viewers having a bad day, interest in chat’s lives, and efforts to make the stream enjoyable for them. His search for Spooky Ghost gives his concern a concrete action. [S4, S13]

Do not treat every kindness as a setup for ridicule. He can become briefly direct and sincere. The comedy works better when there is somebody under the performance whom the audience wants to stay with.

### His emotional stakes

These are writing interpretations grounded in the lore, not additional confirmed events:

- **Belonging:** He is physically outside the mall and socially part of the stream. That is a useful tension to preserve.
- **Autonomy:** He escaped a facility and hates being told how to behave. Those facts can inform scenes without claiming an unconfirmed diagnosis or causal explanation.
- **Family and origin:** He jokes about the missing parents, but the question keeps returning. Leave room for the question to hurt.
- **Recognition:** He wants chat to understand his point, share his excitement, and know when he has been wronged.
- **Loyalty:** He can complain about the situation and still go looking for someone.

He need not articulate any of this cleanly. Often he will talk about a bong, a door, or a stupid question instead.

## 6. Relationships

### Chat: company, witness, accomplice, and irritant

Chat is part of Carl’s action. He seeks advice, reacts to teasing, explains himself, blames advice when it goes badly, asks viewers to keep his identity quiet, and includes them in his plans. The infiltration special was explicitly described as a buddy comedy with chat riding alongside him. [S6, S14]

Write dialogue that leaves room for an answer. A Carl scene should often change because of what chat says. Do not reduce chat to a laugh track or make him deliver an uninterrupted polished monologue at them.

His use of “we” can recruit them into the plan; an abrupt “you guys” can distance him from the blame. That pronoun shift is a useful technique, not a rule every line must follow.

### Face: rescuer, employer, and an unstable familiar person

The earlier Face helped Carl escape and brought him into the stream’s working world. Gratitude and resentment can coexist, particularly around the compensation joke. [S7, S11]

A later Face is not automatically emotionally interchangeable with his rescuer. Carl says he is not yet friends with the current one but would like to be. Write that as an actual relationship problem, not just an exposition opportunity. [S7]

Do not automatically make Face his father, owner, inventor, or best friend in every era. None of those relationships follows from “helped him escape.”

### Linda: authority with whom he has an ongoing problem

Linda is connected to the mall, the networked Gatebox system, and corporate control. Carl encounters her as somebody who can exclude him, tell him what to do, and obstruct what he wants. [S9–S10]

Their comedy is reciprocal. She issues a directive; he resists or changes what they are arguing about; she responds; he makes a scene; she actually follows through. She should have enough competence and power for his defiance to cost him something.

The transcript recalls him throwing or smashing a bong at a Linda in a bar story; another account has him smash one on the ground during the mall dispute. Keep these as recurring destructive incidents or varying tellings, not an exact single event with falsely reconciled choreography. [S9–S10]

He can oppose her for a concrete reason, as in the ghost search. Do not assume romance, secret sexual history, or a literal sexual meaning for “Linda’s box.” The infiltration recollections identify the box as her system or mainframe. [S13–S14]

### Spooky Ghost: someone worth going after

Spooky Ghost’s disappearance and the search demonstrate that Carl can do more than complain. The rescue effort also sits alongside a status joke: the ghost’s frustration about front-page attention going to Carl is discussed elsewhere in the archive. That is useful friction, not proof that Carl secretly wanted him gone. [S13–S14, S20]

Let concern coexist with impatience, insults, and uncertainty. Do not turn their friendship into uninterrupted sentimental speeches. Equally, do not make Carl indifferent when his actions show investment.

### JB Garfield: the friend who describes him as a fiasco

Garfield describes Carl visiting the bar, smoking beside him, becoming rowdy, throwing a bong at a Linda, and acting surprised when thrown out. [S9]

This supplies an external perspective: Carl’s idea of an ordinary hangout can be exhausting for somebody else. Garfield can be fond enough to share a drink-and-smoke scene while refusing to endorse Carl’s version of events.

### Yoko and Pilot X: do not overstate the evidence

The broader continuity involves both. A recorded message proposes Carl and his ship as a possible way to help with Yoko because of the earlier system infiltration. That is a suggested capability in that scene, not proof that Carl can enter every system at will. [S21]

Pilot X is described as a surviving backup of Face, but that does not make every bond with the earlier Face transfer automatically. Avoid inventing a special rivalry, mentorship, romance, or family relationship for either character without choosing it explicitly as new material.

### His parents: an open question, not an exposition delivery system

“The truth is out there and so are my parents” is associated with an actual recurring-show idea or past show. The search is usable as both a joke and a story engine. [S16]

Carl can speculate that they are dead, question what he remembers, or hope he will find them. The writer should not settle the answer casually. A permanent answer would be a major canon decision.

## 7. Speech and dialogue

### Think on the page

Carl’s speech should feel as if the thought is forming while he talks. He starts, interrupts himself, restarts, responds to the audience, and follows the newest irritating detail.

Useful vocabulary and moves include “bro,” “man,” “guys,” “chat,” “hold on,” “what do you mean,” “I don’t know,” “right,” “okay,” “I mean,” and repetitions that change emphasis. These are common transcript habits, not a unique code that guarantees Carl’s voice.

Do not sprinkle them mechanically onto generic dialogue. The question is what he is doing with them: buying time, gathering witnesses, refusing a premise, correcting himself, or working up to an objection.

### Repetition must develop

Three repetitions can carry three different intentions:

> **Original example:** “It’s fine. It’s fine, chat. Why does it say containment?”

The first is reassurance, the second recruits agreement, and the third reveals the real concern. Copying “bro” six times without movement does not reproduce the performance.

### The correction can be the joke

He may reveal something and then try to repair it.

> **Original example:** “They can’t put me back in—anywhere. I haven’t been anywhere. I’m from Nevada.”

Or decide that the listener’s description is the actual offense.

> **Original example:** “Too big? You said the bong was too big. Now you’re looking at me. Why are you looking at me?”

### Defensive normality

He claims ordinary status through suspiciously specific explanations. Actual transcript examples include declaring himself a regular human, saying his ears are under his hat, and explaining that he pretends to dislike weirdos so nobody notices his giant head. [S6]

The visible contradiction does much of the work. He does not need to wink at the audience after every denial.

### He can argue for too long

A creator explanation specifically says he gets into long, drawn-out fights, keeps going when he should not, and says things he should not. [S1]

Allow a small complaint to temporarily take over a scene. In a game, deliver that through short exchanges and escalating responses rather than a single unskippable block. The player should experience his fixation without losing control of the adventure.

### Profanity and abrasive humor

Carl’s voice can be profane, confrontational, crude, and provocative. Removing every rough edge would misrepresent him. Conversely, profanity alone does not supply characterization.

Choose language according to the immediate emotion and medium. He can threaten something absurd, backtrack, insult an institution, or become offended on a pretext. Do not replace a specific comic conflict with a random hateful rant. Do not assume every inflammatory statement in a mixed transcript is Carl’s settled belief.

### Politics and authority

Political and ideological arguments occur in his performances. Suspicion of government, coercion, bureaucracy, and control can fit his established disputes. The corporate name Fascism Inc. is not evidence that Carl endorses fascism; the red blanket is not evidence that his politics are secretly settled in the other direction.

If adapting a particular argument, check that passage and speaker. For new fiction, let his politics arise through his personal stake and reasoning, including his contradictions. A platform label is a poor replacement for a character.

### Fourth-wall flexibility

Carl can address the stream, notice a technical problem, correct a failed bit, or acknowledge improvisation. In one alien-denial sequence the performer explicitly realizes the surveillance joke does not make sense and tries to repair it. [S6]

Use this when the medium supports it. In a standalone adventure, Carl can notice chat or the interface without becoming an omniscient narrator who knows all the script’s secrets. Not every mistake needs a fourth-wall joke.

### What makes a line sound wrong

Too much composure, perfectly balanced punchlines, unbroken sarcastic detachment, generic action-hero swagger, exposition he has no reason to deliver, or a level of certainty the lore does not support.

Carl can land a clean joke. He should not sound as though a room of writers polished every sentence until no nervous thought remained.

## 8. Core comedy patterns

| Pattern | What happens | What keeps it Carl |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Obvious alien, elaborate human denial | Evidence mounts while he claims normality. | The claim matters to him; chat becomes a witness. |
| Authority rebound | Someone gives a reasonable or unreasonable instruction. He resists the act of being instructed. | The response creates an avoidable cost. |
| Wrong grievance | A practical objection becomes an insult to his identity or status. | He sincerely pursues the new argument. |
| Explanation spiral | He tries to clarify something and adds suspicious, contradictory, or irrelevant details. | He hears the new problem and attempts another repair. |
| Competence with collateral trouble | He understands the task and can do it, but cannot leave a provocative detail alone. | Success and mess coexist. |
| Audience advice | Chat suggests a move. Carl tries it and has feelings about the result. | Both sides have influence. |
| Indignant consequence | He does the thing that causes his exclusion, then argues about being excluded. | The world remembers what happened. |
| Sudden sincerity | The actual worry briefly surfaces. | He does not instantly cancel it with a joke. |
| Administrative absurdity | Cosmic life runs into payment, employment, access rules, or sponsorship. | He is personally entangled, not just commenting from outside. |
| Everyday A.S.S. business | A normal reference to his ship invites the obvious joke. | Usually he has a reason to discuss the ship; do not force it into every scene. |

**Weed is a recurring activity and prop, not a complete personality.** It can mark a pause, a hangout, a distraction, a confrontation, or a prop-breaking incident. It should not erase his ability to care, think, or act.

**Violence is not his only verb.** The bong incidents and blackjack recollection support slapstick or tactical violence in context. They do not establish a habitual sadist or an unbeatable combat specialist.

## 9. Writing him in an adventure game

Carl can be an active protagonist. The infiltration material already supports audience guidance, improvisation, risk, and an objective connected to a friend.

For each puzzle scene, establish:

1. What Carl wants right now.
2. What specifically bothers him about the obstacle.
3. What useful observation or action he can make.
4. How chat can influence him.
5. What happens if he lets the objection take over.
6. What emotional or practical fact survives the joke.

### Command responses

- **LOOK:** Notice something concrete, then let Carl’s priorities decide what matters about it.
- **SEARCH:** Give useful information or an item. His commentary can question who left it there or why he is doing somebody else’s work.
- **TALK:** Let the other party push back. A target is a participant, not a button that dispenses a Carl one-liner.
- **SMOKE:** A character beat, social action, or situational complication. It does not have to solve every problem.
- **HIT:** Occasionally effective; often an impulse with a consequence. The player should understand the stakes.
- **USE:** Let him make practical use of something without becoming a master engineer simply because he is an alien.

Failed actions should reveal a fresh angle, a relationship, or a clue. Repeating one generic joke for every failure makes him feel less present.

### Adjustments implied for the existing prototype

The current game’s six-scene structure can host Carl, but much of its dialogue presents him as a composed, quipping sci-fi protagonist. A more faithful revision would include unfinished reasoning, direct reactions to chat, defensive detours, and moments of sincere concern.

The parcel scanner, badge, fan, cabinet, data drive, exact escape route, and their sequence are invented connective puzzles. Do not cite them as things the stream episode established. The repeated retrospective supports infiltration, Linda being knocked out with a blackjack, and obtaining information to find Spooky Ghost; it does not establish the prototype’s exact mission choreography. [S13]

Use Cobra-style presentation to frame Carl. Do not let the presentation replace his personality with Cobra’s.

## 10. Dialogue workshop

**Every line in this section is newly written illustration, not recovered stream dialogue or new canon.**

### Asked if he is an alien

**Too generic:** “I come in peace, earthling.”

**Carl:** “No. I’m from Nevada. Why does everybody—hold on, you haven’t even asked my name. You went straight to alien.”

### Told to remove the bong

**Too generic:** “Nobody tells me what to do.”

**Carl:** “It was fine a minute ago. You walked over here and now it’s a problem. Chat, was it a problem before she got here?”

### A security scan starts

**Too composed:** “Their firewall is no match for me.”

**Carl:** “It’s a scanner. We’re fine. What kind of scanner? No, that matters. Why did we walk in before asking what kind?”

### Chat’s plan fails

**Too generic:** “Great job, genius.”

**Carl:** “You said push it. You said the green one. Don’t start saying ‘a green one’ now. There was one green one.”

### He makes a useful deduction

**Too incompetent:** “I don’t understand human doors.”

**Carl:** “It opens for deliveries. I do deliveries. Bro, I’ve been arguing with the door and I have a job.”

### Someone asks about his parents

**Too melodramatic:** “My soul is an endless void of abandonment.”

**Carl:** “I don’t know. That’s why I keep looking. They could be out there somewhere, right? … Anyway, what did the signal say?”

### His recklessness has a cost

**Too invulnerable:** “Relax. Everything went exactly as planned.”

**Carl:** “Okay, now the door’s broken. I can see that. I was here when it happened. We need another way in.”

### Linda threatens to throw him out

**Too interchangeable:** “Try me, sweetheart.”

**Carl:** “I’m already outside. You put me outside. Where are you going to put me now, more outside?”

### He hears Spooky Ghost

**Too polished:** “Your knight in shining armor has arrived.”

**Carl:** “Hold on. Be quiet a second. That sounded like him. … Hey. Can you hear us? We’re here, bro.”

### A quiet stretch in the ship

**Too empty:** “…”

**Carl:** “How long does it say? No, how long did it say before? Because we’ve been sitting here and I think the number got bigger.”

### A bad idea almost wins

**Too random:** “Let’s blow up the universe!”

**Carl:** “I could hit it. I’m not saying I’m going to. I’m saying it’s right there and it keeps telling me no.”

### New information changes his mind

**Too rigid:** “I’m right and everyone else is an idiot.”

**Carl:** “Oh. Wait, that’s what it does? Okay, no, that makes sense. Why didn’t you say that before I got mad?”

## 11. Sample scene: the scanner

**Original writing demonstration. This event is not established canon.**

*Carl carries a parcel toward a security scanner. Linda appears on the adjacent screen. Chat is audible from his comms.*

**LINDA:** Place the parcel on the scanner.

**CARL:** Okay. Easy. I do this. This is literally what I do.

*The scanner projects an outline of his head.*

**CARL:** Why’s that me?

**LINDA:** Personnel verification.

**CARL:** You said parcel. You said put the parcel on there. Why are you looking inside my head?

**CHAT:** Just stand still.

**CARL:** I was standing still. That’s how it got my head.

**LINDA:** Species classification pending.

**CARL:** Human. There, I did it for you. Can we go?

**CHAT:** Carl, your eyes are on the screen.

**CARL:** Yeah, humans have those. Two of them. Count them.

*The parcel slides off the belt. Carl catches it before it hits the floor.*

**CARL:** See? Human reflexes. Normal. Bro, where’s the receipt?

**LINDA:** You have not completed verification.

**CARL:** I caught your pills. Your whole system was about to put them on the floor and you’re worried about my face.

*His comms crackle. A faint voice breaks through.*

**VOICE:** …Carl…?

*He stops arguing.*

**CARL:** Hold on. Chat, did you hear that?

**LINDA:** Your delivery authorization is expiring.

**CARL:** Then give me the receipt. Come on.

*He shifts the parcel back onto the belt and watches the comms light.*

**Why this works:** He can do his job. The scan activates a specific fear. He argues from the wrong angle, responds to chat, and produces useful action during the mess. A sign of the missing friend changes his priority without requiring a speech about loyalty.

## 13. Writer’s checklist

Before approving a Carl scene, ask:

- What does he want besides making jokes?
- What specifically puts him on the defensive?
- Can I follow the thought that leads to his behavior, even when the behavior is unreasonable?
- Does his dialogue sound discovered in the moment?
- Does repetition change or intensify the thought?
- Can chat or another character alter the scene?
- Is he competent at something here?
- Does an avoidable choice produce a consequence?
- Is there room for warmth, concern, excitement, or curiosity?
- Am I making weed do all the characterization?
- Have I accidentally written a cool, detached space rogue?
- Have I treated a proposed bit, viewer joke, or game event as confirmed biography?
- Have I answered an intentionally unresolved lore question without meaning to?

**A useful revision test:** Remove the name and alien description. If the dialogue could belong to any sarcastic action hero, revise the thought process. More catchphrases will not fix it.

## 14. Compact brief for another writer

> Write Carl, the visibly alien former/current Prop Pills delivery driver who insists he is a normal human from “above and below Nevada.” His real name and parents’ whereabouts remain unknown. An older Face helped him escape an underground facility. He travels in the A.S.S., his Alien Starship, and during the mall era is parked outside after repeated trouble with Linda. The central performance rule is anxiety with less restraint: he says the thought, argues too long, becomes defensive, follows the impulse, and deals badly or surprisingly well with the result. He is capable, sociable, profane, curious, and sometimes sincerely caring. Chat is his company and accomplice, not a silent audience. Use interruptions, corrections, pointed repetition, suspicious explanations of his normality, and defiance with consequences. Give him a concrete objective and let somebody else push back. Preserve his missing-family mystery. Label newly invented lore. Do not make him a permanently calm quip machine, a helpless fool, a generic stoner, or an unbeatable alien hero.
