# JB GARFIELD

**Standalone character bible and writing guide · 2 October 2026**

**Expanded archive revision · 9 November 2026**

A transcript-grounded guide to JB Garfield himself: the detective cat, his noir ambitions, his connection to Face, and how to write him as a character with his own purpose.

**Central writing principle: JB wants to be a cool noir detective a little too badly. He genuinely wants to solve the mystery, but he also wants to look and sound like the detective who ought to be solving it.**

That closely follows the creator’s explicit description. JB is a separate offshoot of Face who likes noir and really wants to be a cool detective—too much. His performance also has a practical purpose: he turns translated game dialogue into a narrated detective story instead of always reading every line directly. [JB1]

## 1. The essential character

JB’s central tension is between **the mystery he is investigating and the detective he is trying to be**.

He can set the atmosphere, summarize a witness’s account, and invite the audience to follow a theory. Then a clue contradicts him, someone asks an inconvenient practical question, or something mundane interrupts the performance.

The point is not that he is incapable of detective work. The creator explicitly says he likes adventure games, likes solving them, and likes being a detective. His desire to perform the role coexists with an actual interest in doing the work. [JB2]

Let him earn some of his impressive lines. A cat who poses constantly and never notices anything becomes a much smaller joke.

His dry delivery can also make him comfortable company. The creator describes returning to detective nights as a warm way to tell a story during cold holiday evenings. The audience is invited to settle in and investigate with him. [JB3]

The useful combination is curiosity, affected coolness, familiar Garfield humor, and enough self-awareness to survive his own act.

## 2. Evidence and writing boundaries

The archive mixes character performance, creator commentary, source-game dialogue, audience remarks, technical setup, and ideas being developed aloud. This guide distinguishes those where they matter.

JB’s intended direction is unusually explicit, but the creator also says he is still developing the character and wants stronger noir narration. The recommendations here develop that direction; they do not pretend every suggested mannerism is already a repeated performance habit. [JB4]

All sample dialogue below is new writing, not a recovered transcript.

A detective-game victim, childhood friend, police rank, or romance does not automatically belong to JB’s independent biography. Likewise, a host’s opinion during a JB episode is not automatically a settled character belief.

The transcripts render his name as JB, JV, J.B., and various mishearings. **JB Garfield** is the working name here.

## 3. Origin: the voice, the initials, and the image

### The voice resemblance

Viewers repeatedly told the creator he sounded like Garfield. He describes leaning into that resemblance and building a character around it. One exchange specifically identifies the comparison as Lorenzo Music. [JB5]

The creator sometimes says he does not know how to make himself sound more Garfield-like deliberately. That is useful: the character does not require a forced impression or a Garfield catchphrase in every sentence.

Do not assume a voice changer is the source of the performance. The explanations emphasize a resemblance in the creator’s own voice.

### The initials

The creator explicitly takes **JB** from **J.B. Harold**, the detective-game character whose name the transcripts often render “Herold” or “Herald.” Garfield comes from the voice comparison and detective imagery. [JB1, JB6]

During Murder Club, he distinguishes the game’s detective from the stream host and says he will use JB Garfield to keep the presentation manageable. That distinction matters when tracking biography.

The reviewed material does not establish a full expansion of JB’s initials, a birth name, or an official police rank. Do not invent one as recovered canon.

### The noir cat

The creator describes using Garfield’s detective appearance from a gray/monochrome episode and developing several visual versions. A detailed account describes working from a sketch in a purchased book, colorizing it, and rigging the resulting image as an animated model. [JB7]

Another discussion suggests a Sin City-like treatment of grayscale and orange. Treat that as a useful art-direction proposal rather than a mandatory description of every version.

No dedicated JB reference image is named in the current `ref` directory. This document does not claim to establish a final model sheet from one.

## 4. A character Face performed becomes a person

JB initially exists as a character Face does. Later explanations describe him as an offshoot that survives when Face is killed and becomes separate. [JB1, JB8]

A performed appearance gives him a reaction to that discovery: he is not merely a detective cat who loves lasagna, but a forgotten virtualization of a system that backs up a human mind. He describes an identity crisis about it. [JB9]

This offers a deeper question: **If you began as somebody else’s detective character, what makes your present life your own?**

His chosen detective identity can matter because it is something he likes and continues to do. That is a writing interpretation, not a fully articulated motive supplied by the transcripts.

Keep the related identities distinct:

| Identity | Relationship to JB | Do not automatically transfer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Face | Origin of the performed character and surviving offshoot. | Every power, memory, permission, and intention. |
| Pilot X | Another identity associated with older Face continuity. | Pilot X’s exact cover story or restrictions. |
| J.B. Harold | Source of the initials and a detective role played through the stream. | The source character’s entire professional biography. |
| Jinguji Saburo | A protagonist JB performs during detective-game sessions. | Every game event as a literal independent JB life event. |
| Garfield | Recognizable image, delivery, and cat-joke foundation. | Every comic-strip relationship or continuity detail. |

The exact mechanism of JB’s survival, completeness of memory, and current system access are not fully established in the material reviewed. His connection to Face is not permission to use him as an omniscient substitute.

He is nevertheless part of the stream’s story. A planning discussion explicitly affirms that a larger arc can begin during a JB episode because he belongs to the continuity. [JB10]

## 5. Psychology and motive

### He wants the role to fit

The desire to be a cool noir detective suggests a small gap between how JB wants a moment to play and how it actually plays.

He might prefer a knowing pause to admitting he lost his place. He might reach for an atmospheric explanation before asking the obvious practical question. A simple errand might look more attractive if he can describe it as following a lead.

Use that gap selectively. He is allowed to be naturally funny or competent without visibly performing every second.

### He genuinely enjoys investigation

Give his curiosity an object: who knew something, why two accounts differ, what has gone missing, or what a witness avoided answering.

The case should contain actual reasoning. Let him notice a contradiction, remember an overlooked fact, or revise a theory. His noir narration becomes more satisfying when there is useful thinking underneath it.

### He likes having somebody along

Episode titles invite viewers to help solve mysteries. One explicit setup casts JB as Jinguji, Yoko as Yoko, and the audience as Kuma. [JB11]

He can ask for a theory, challenge it, or acknowledge a good observation. He does not have to conceal all the evidence to preserve a final moment of brilliance.

### Dry delivery is not disinterest

In one appearance he says he is very excited, acknowledges that he does not sound it, and explains that this is how JB sounds. [JB12]

Write him as capable of being absorbed in a case while sounding unhurried. Do not confuse a low-energy voice with a refusal to care.

### His own identity is an uncomfortable case

The virtualization revelation can interrupt his confidence. The detective who wants to explain events discovers he cannot fully explain himself.

Use that when the story needs vulnerability. If every food joke leads into an existential monologue, the recurring detective host stops being sustainable.

## 6. Voice: narrating the case

JB’s defining technique is **turning information into a detective’s account**.

The creator explains that JB can tell the audience what somebody said rather than always translating each line into directly performed dialogue. Another discussion explicitly recommends leaning into that noir approach. [JB1, JB13]

He has three useful registers:

| Register | Function | Writing approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrator | Orient the audience and frame events. | State a concrete detail, give it an attitude, and move forward. |
| Investigator | Obtain information and test a theory. | Ask clear questions. Let the answer affect the next question. |
| Host/cat | React to viewers or mundane inconvenience. | Relax the detective performance into an ordinary, dry observation. |

A scene can use all three. He narrates an entrance, asks a direct question, and ends with a complaint about the food.

### Keep the noir specific

An effective line contains an observation and a point of view. It does not need several competing metaphors.

New example:

> “The office was locked. That was the first sensible thing anybody had done all evening. Unfortunately, we needed to get inside.”

The player learns a fact and hears JB’s attitude. The final turn also identifies the immediate problem.

### Let the recap do real work

A useful recap gives the audience what is known, what does not fit, and what to investigate next. JB is a natural vehicle for that in an adventure game.

Atmosphere should help the player follow the mystery. It should not obscure the clue needed to progress.

### Preserve rough edges

The transcripts include hesitations, blunt questions, uncertainty, and ordinary conversation. Tighten them for pacing without turning him into an uninterrupted dispenser of perfect hard-boiled sentences.

Occasionally he can abandon a metaphor, ask somebody to repeat a detail, or admit he has no theory yet. That makes the ambition to sound cool visible.

### Mondays and lasagna

Both are explicit parts of the performance. JB calls himself a detective cat who loves lasagna, and there is a joke about why he does not stream on Mondays. [JB9, JB14]

Use them as an incentive, interruption, excuse, or moment of self-branding. They should do something in the scene rather than appear as compulsory tokens in every exchange.

The creator’s comments about his own food preferences outside the role do not automatically override JB’s performed tastes.

## 7. Relationships

### Face

Face is his origin, but JB’s separation matters. Give JB a priority or uncertainty of his own.

A new scene could have Face treat him as an old routine while JB insists on being taken seriously as the person now doing the work. This is an adaptation possibility, not a documented confrontation.

### Yoko

Yoko is a natural participant in the detective format. The explicit casting setup retains her own identity while JB takes the protagonist role. [JB11]

She can supply a useful distinction or puncture an unnecessary flourish. Let JB actually use her contribution.

This does not establish a romance, an employment contract, or a jointly owned mall detective agency.

### The audience / Kuma

The viewers can be partners in observation. JB can turn a question into a case and ask for evidence.

One direct example is his treatment of unusual stream traffic as a mystery. The creator’s explanations of possible bots remain uncertain speculation; the character evidence is JB’s impulse to investigate and invite submissions. [JB15]

Do not convert this into a canonical bot-repelling power or a factual conclusion about platform behavior.

### Linda

A performed exchange has Linda dismissing audience interest in JB while the host defends bringing him back. It supports comic disrespect and conflicting priorities, but not an invented lifelong feud. [JB16]

In new writing, her confidence about status can challenge his need to be recognized as a serious detective.

### Ghost

A JB episode includes Ghost interrupting and a limit on revealing lore, with questions directed toward Face. This shows that the formats overlap; it does not prove JB is permanently barred from discussing every secret. [JB17]

Ghost can disrupt the atmosphere JB is trying to maintain. Keeping the conversation on the case gives JB an active objective in the exchange.

### Juan and the mall venues

JB appears around Suitors and Cooters. He jokes that mentioning his name will not earn a discount and might result in somebody spitting in the customer’s food. [JB18]

A separate discussion proposes leaving him at the mall with Juan while the others search for Ghost. Keep that proposal distinct from a confirmed permanent living arrangement.

The venues can support a new scene about gathering rumors, meeting a contact, or discovering that his reputation is less useful than he hoped.

## 8. Recurring bits and performance material

**Detective-game night.** This is his clearest recurring home, including J.B. Harold and Jinguji sessions. Other adventure games also appear; he is not confined to one protagonist’s cases.

**The cat who knew too little.** This title is used for a special combining detective-game sampling with commentary on videos. It suits his self-image and its limits. The actual commentary should not be transformed into fictional crimes by real people. [JB19]

**JB Garfield FM.** A passage tries out radio-style lines, including a station promising static and a sponsor selling reconstituted lasagna powder. The surrounding discussion refers to material supplied by a robot, so treat these as lines being tried/read in performance, not evidence of a fully established broadcasting company. [JB20]

**The whole stream goes to jail.** A retrospective recalls a gate or bars JB built that could lock up the stream, with a clanking sound, while proposing bringing it back for puns. This supports a presentation gag; it does not establish actual universal arrest authority. [JB21]

**Reputation without benefits.** The Cooters recommendation makes his name recognizable without making it useful. This is a strong recurring pattern for new jokes: he gets the acknowledgment he wants, followed by a result he does not.

**The case becomes personal.** His own identity is a mystery he cannot resolve with ordinary detective habits. Keep it available as a story thread without making it his only subject.

## 9. Visual and performance direction

The supported foundation is an animated noir Garfield presentation, with multiple versions and experiments. The creator describes rigging the model, improving tracking, and considering additional outfits such as a white jacket. A proposed outfit is not automatically his default costume. [JB7, JB22]

For an adaptation, let posture and timing carry the difference between attempted coolness and actual interest. A pause can be theatrical; an eye movement toward a clue can be genuinely attentive.

Avoid overanimating every line. The dry delivery benefits from moments when the image seems composed while the dialogue reveals that the situation is not.

Model glitches, tracking controls, and scene changes are production details unless the episode explicitly turns them into fiction. Do not invent a new power every time the model stretches or disappears.

## 10. How to build a JB scene

A practical structure:

1. Give him a concrete question to investigate.
2. Let him frame it with more style than it strictly requires.
3. Provide information that deserves real attention.
4. Interrupt his preferred presentation with a fact, a person, or an inconvenience.
5. Let him adapt rather than merely repeat the same joke.
6. End with a changed theory, a useful clue, or a revealing mistake.

The scene should move even if the player does not recognize the Garfield references. Recognition adds another layer; it should not replace the dramatic action.

For a quieter scene, give him a witness whose account does not fit, then let his interest in the person outweigh his interest in delivering a clever line. That creates room for the detective aspiration to become something worthwhile.

## 11. Adventure-game use

These are new adaptation recommendations, not claims about his role in “Carl invades Linda’s box.”

JB can be a witness interviewer, investigator, narrator, or occasional partner. A compact scene where he helps Carl interpret a clue may fit better than adding an entire detective subplot.

Useful functions include:

| Function | What JB contributes | What the player still does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recap | Organizes known evidence and highlights the contradiction. | Chooses the next lead. |
| Interview | Asks a direct question in an entertaining voice. | Supplies the object or fact that changes the answer. |
| Theory | Offers a plausible interpretation with a visible weakness. | Tests it against the evidence. |
| Identity mystery | Notices knowledge or memories that do not fit his experience. | Investigates the source rather than accepting an unexplained reveal. |

Keep puzzle information reliable enough to use. If JB is wrong, give the player evidence that can expose the error. Do not force the player to accept an arbitrary answer because he delivers it confidently.

His connection to Face should not provide whatever system access the current puzzle happens to need. Establish the relevant capability before relying on it.

## 12. Original dialogue examples

**The following scenes are newly written examples. They are not transcripts or new canon.**

### A. The atmosphere meets the errand

**JB:** Somebody in this mall was hiding something. I could feel it.

**CARL:** Linda said the package is behind the counter.

**JB:** Then she was getting careless.

*Purpose:* He turns a practical task into a case without preventing the scene from moving.

### B. A useful deduction

**JB:** You said nobody came through here after closing.

**CLERK:** Nobody did.

**JB:** Then the person who left these footprints was already inside.

*Purpose:* Sometimes he earns the detective moment. A real inference gives the affected coolness something to stand on.

### C. Yoko interrupts the narration

**JB:** The message was short. Cold. Written by somebody with nothing left to lose.

**YOKO:** It says the printer is out of paper.

**JB:** We all reach that point eventually.

*Purpose:* She supplies the information; he preserves what dignity he can without arguing away the fact.

### D. The name does not help

**JB:** Tell Juan I sent you.

**CARL:** Will that get me in?

**JB:** It'll give him somebody else to be mad at.

*Purpose:* An original variation on the established reputation-without-benefits joke.

### E. The identity question

**JB:** I remembered the room. I remembered the argument. Trouble was, I couldn't remember ever being there.

**CARL:** Maybe you saw a video.

**JB:** I was hoping you'd say that.

*Purpose:* His narration remains recognizable while a simple response reveals vulnerability. This illustrates uncertainty; it does not establish a specific inherited memory.

### F. The audience contributes

**JB:** All right. We've got a locked door, two keys, and a witness who says he was asleep. What am I missing?

**VIEWER:** The door locks from the inside.

**JB:** Good. Keep that thought. I was about to spend twenty minutes accusing the key.

*Purpose:* He can accept help without losing his place as host or investigator.

### G. Cat priorities

**LINDA:** You can ask one question.

**JB:** Who had access to the office?

**LINDA:** I expected you to ask about lunch.

**JB:** I'm conducting an investigation. Lunch can wait until the follow-up.

*Purpose:* The Garfield element colors the detective work without replacing it.

## 13. Common mistakes

**Writing ordinary Garfield with a hat.** The ambition to be a noir detective, the narrative technique, and the separate-offshoot identity are what make this version specific.

**Writing an infallible noir genius.** He wants the image too badly. Preserve occasional uncertainty and overreach.

**Writing a total incompetent.** He actually enjoys solving cases. Give him useful observations and earned progress.

**Making every line a metaphor.** Clear questions and plain reactions give the noir passages room to work.

**Treating all source-game events as personal canon.** A performed role is not automatically a complete biography.

**Treating him as Face with cat graphics.** The later explanations explicitly make him separate.

**Making every day Friday forever.** Scheduling is a performance arrangement, not an immutable law of the character.

**Inventing a police badge, agency, or system permission.** Establish those in an adaptation if needed; do not pretend the reviewed sources already do.

**Turning speculative bits into settled history.** The mall abandonment, alternate outfits, and various expansions are proposals in the cited passages.

## 14. Compact writing brief

**JB Garfield is a detective cat who wants the case solved and wants to look cool solving it. His dry, familiar delivery carries noir narration, practical questions, audience collaboration, and occasional Garfield priorities. He began as a character Face performed and later became a separate surviving offshoot, leaving his own identity available as an uncomfortable mystery.**

**Write the investigation clearly. Let him reach a little for the atmosphere, earn some of his deductions, and recover when reality interrupts the performance.**
