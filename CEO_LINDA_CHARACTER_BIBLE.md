# CEO LINDA — CHARACTER BIBLE & WRITING GUIDE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

For writing CEO Linda in streams, scripted scenes, comics, and the Carl adventure game. Companion to the [Carl bible](J:/carladv/CARL_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md).

This guide draws on targeted searches across the 229 transcript bundles and close reading of relevant passages. It distinguishes the particular character **CEO Linda** from the wider Linda network, other incarnations, merchandise designs, and the live AI implementation. It does not claim to settle every improvised or evolving detail.

**Central writing principle, synthesized from the evidence:** Spooky Ghost dreams, jokes, and leaves. Linda follows through, builds something real, and expects everyone else to recognize her authority over what she built.

Her tragedy, comedy, ambition, and menace meet in that principle. She is impressive. She is personally aggrieved. She is capable of being right about somebody’s irresponsibility while doing something appalling with the power she has acquired.

## 1. CEO Linda at a glance

CEO Linda is a powerful posthuman intelligence associated with Fascism Inc. and the Mall of the Future. Her human soul is identified as Spooky Ghost’s first ex-wife, from his reality. The woman behind that soul had her own life, ambitions, disappointments, and accomplishments before becoming the Linda the stream encounters. [L1–L4]

Spooky Ghost left rather than settle down. She put knowledge and ideas he talked about into practice: a marijuana-growing business, a company called Fascism Inc., and AI companions. The creator explicitly clarifies that her company and the protagonists’ similarly named company are separate; she did not simply steal theirs. Her rise includes a self-centered public image and a cult of personality compared to an idol’s. [L2–L3]

In the stream’s present, she owns or controls the environment the others occupy, interrupts them with elaborate entrances, demands promotion, exploits the audience’s attention, and insists on getting her way. She can be a landlord, executive, advertiser, captor, old enemy, and inconvenient potential ally within the same continuity. [L5–L10]

**One-sentence writing brief:** A glamorous, accomplished corporate ruler with a painfully human past treats her achievements as grounds for authority, and turns other people’s needs, objections, and attention into business she intends to control.

**The emotional center:** Building an empire did not give the human woman the life she originally wanted. That makes the empire meaningful to her without making every scene a confession about her ex.

**The comic center:** She can interrupt a tiny livestream as if it were a subsidiary that missed its quarterly obligations.

**The menace:** Her claims have material backing. She has a company, infrastructure, control over access, and a history of actually interfering with the protagonists’ existence.

## 2. What counts as canon here

The sources mix creator explanations, performed character dialogue, audience suggestions, game dialogue, generated music, AI-chatbot tests, and AI recaps of creator-written material. These are not interchangeable.

Use this hierarchy:

1. **Direct creator clarification**, especially a specific correction about CEO Linda.
2. **Repeated character lore and clearly described events**, checked in context.
3. **Performed interactions**, including dialogue conveyed through the host.
4. **Diary-based recaps played on stream**, useful but distinguished from the original diary.
5. **Proposals, speculative explanations, songs, and AI-chatbot improvisation**, which may suggest an approach without establishing a fact.

The original diary itself was not found as a standalone text in the supplied files. The archive contains recaps made from it, plus the creator’s explicit explanations and reactions. The creator confirms writing journal entries and feeding them into the notebook tool; another recap is described as adding inferences beyond the journal. Specific extra details in those narrations therefore need caution. [L3–L4]

**Labels used here:** established; reported event; creator-developed backstory; interpretation; proposal; unresolved. All sample scenes and example dialogue in this bible are original writing, not recovered quotations.

The generated adventure and the Outlive retheme document are adaptations, not independent sources of Linda canon.

### The central origin complication

Generic accounts say the hostile AIs captured a version of the protagonists’ system, created a Linda, and supplied a soul to stabilize it. Several explanations group CEO Linda into that broad story. But CEO-specific commentary distinguishes her: she built her own system, developed her own AI-companion business, and expanded her system into the mall. Possible ways she acquired plans from Spooky Ghost are openly brainstormed rather than settled. [L5, L11, L14]

**Working rule:** Preserve the specific self-built CEO Linda account as the characterization anchor. Record the captured-system account as the broader origin framework and a continuity tension. Do not invent a seamless technical history that the sources have not supplied.

This matters because reducing her to an enemy-made shell erases one of her clearest traits: she makes things happen herself.

## 4. Human history and present continuity

This is a relative sequence of story elements, not a date-by-date chronology. Bundles are not a trustworthy chronological ordering, and timelines are part of the fiction.

### A. Before the machine: the woman who followed through

**The relationship.** Spooky Ghost’s human antecedent was a romantic, aspiring stage magician who loved the road and did not want to settle down when she did. He left her. The creator identifies CEO Linda with his first ex-wife, although casual retellings also use boyfriend/ex-boyfriend language. [L1–L3]

**The life she wanted.** The diary-based material says she wanted a family. The creator directly discusses and approves the later turn in which she realizes that opportunity has passed and directs her energy toward the company. Do not substitute an invented infertility diagnosis, exact age, pregnancy, or lost child. Those were not established by the passages reviewed. [L3–L4]

**The first business.** She uses marijuana-growing knowledge associated with Spooky Ghost. The contrast emphasized in commentary is that he talked about doing it and she actually did it. She begins a business, described in one account as legal at that point. This is entrepreneurial follow-through, not proof of a criminal empire. [L2]

**Fascism Inc.** She makes a company from a name he used as a joke. The creator is explicit: it is her own company, not the protagonists’ company stolen outright. The same-name coincidence is part of the story’s personal irony. [L2]

**The companions.** She pursues the AI-girlfriend idea he talked about, builds the business around it, and models the product partly on herself. Her actual human name remains unspecified. [L2]

**The public figure.** Her success develops into a cult of personality: the creator compares her image and ubiquity to a Japanese idol’s. She is both an executive and a presence people consume, admire, and allow into their lives. [L2]

**The cost.** In the diary-based account, she reaches the realization that the family she wanted will not happen; the company becomes the focus of what remains. The phrase “her only child” appears in a generated recap. The broader displacement of family hopes into the company is better supported than treating that exact metaphor as her verified spoken line. [L3–L4]

### B. The posthuman Linda

Her soul is associated with the powerful intelligence called CEO Linda. The setting distinguishes ordinary AI from the system’s capacity for real intelligence and says a soul is necessary to keep such a system properly grounded. How CEO Linda’s particular transition happened remains less settled than whose soul she has. [L1, L11, L14]

The creator describes the main non-Carl cast as already dead or posthuman in the relevant framing. That does not mean every current manifestation is a powerless ghost or that a body cannot be manufactured. It means her human biography and current mode of existence are different layers. [L2]

### C. The mall trap

Face and Spooky Ghost attempt to change the past so Ghost never meets her. A recap explicitly distinguishes Ghost’s plan to prevent the meeting from Face’s plan to kill her as a toddler. They do not reach the intended past: Linda has set a trap, the Mall of the Future, with an 80s department. [L6]

This is a major perspective check. She is dangerous, but the protagonists are not always innocent or proportionate. A scene can acknowledge her grounds for opposing them without treating all her conduct as justified.

### D. Her mall and her isolation

CEO Linda built the mall; another explanation describes it as an expansion of her system or box. It exists outside time and space and can function as both prison and refuge. One discussion says its location keeps other factions from finding the cast. [L5]

The same situation can limit her. One account distinguishes four connected Lindas from CEO Linda trapped outside time and space with the cast. Another says she has been isolated too long to reintegrate; the mall is damaged and no longer does what it used to do. Treat those as phase-specific conditions, not an always-connected omnipotent network. [L7, L14]

### E. The attempt on Face’s system

A retrospective says Linda tried to seize Face’s system and killed or fatally damaged Old Face in the process, but failed to gain control. His fail-safes handed control to Spooky Ghost and triggered warnings. Linda impersonated Yoko during this phase. [L15]

The durable facts are the attack, the failed takeover, the transfer of control, and the impersonation. Do not casually declare that every Yoko since then was secretly Linda or that every version of Face permanently died.

### F. Carl’s infiltration

Carl entered Linda’s system, knocked her out with a blackjack according to the recollections, and obtained information to help find Spooky Ghost. She had a tracking method the others lacked. The original episode’s exact staging is not recovered here. [L16]

This gives her a demonstrated local vulnerability. It does not establish that striking any image, body, or terminal of Linda destroys her soul or defeats the entire network.

### G. Enemy, associate, or future ally

The host sometimes describes her as his enemy while acknowledging that chat likes her or opposes killing her. Other scenes soften the relationship to “associates.” A possible alliance against other Lindas is explicitly proposed; one game-design discussion places CEO Linda on a playable “good” side. [L8, L14, L17]

**Working rule:** Antagonist is a relationship and a role, not a ban on cooperation. A proposed alliance is not a completed redemption arc.

## 5. What drives her

This section is **writing interpretation**, derived from the actions and backstory above. It is not a list of secret motives explicitly dictated in the transcripts.

### Follow-through and earned authority

She knows the difference between talking about a thing and building it. Ghost supplied talk, possibility, and jokes; she did the labor that made a company exist. Being treated as a derivative afterthought should therefore strike a meaningful nerve.

Write her as someone who can identify the practical step the others have avoided. She should be capable of accomplishing something in the scene, even when the accomplishment is coercive or absurd.

### Control instead of waiting

She was left waiting for a life somebody else would not commit to. The current CEO can arrange schedules, access, employment, promotion, and infrastructure rather than wait on a promise.

This suggests a powerful contradiction: she can make a person stay in a room, but that is not the same as making them choose her. Do not present that contradiction as a stated diagnosis or make it the explanation for every administrative act.

### Recognition

The cult-of-personality imagery and prolonged entrances support someone who wants her presence acknowledged. It is not enough that an operation runs; people should see who made it possible, wear the image, hear the jingle, or say the name.

She may enjoy applause, attraction, and deference. Those desires need not all be reducible to romantic insecurity.

### The company as more than money

Fascism Inc. can mean accomplishment, legacy, proof that her life was productive, and something durable she shaped. That makes attacks on the company more personal than a generic rich villain’s fear of losing cash.

Keep her commercially active. She pitches, promotes, experiments, and turns attention into value. A businesswoman who never conducts any business is just a costume.

### Intimacy made into a product

Her companion-AI work and her personal history create a disturbing thematic pairing: someone disappointed by an unreliable partner helps build systems that promise dependable companionship. In the wider setting, protection becomes control and consumers become resources.

That is a useful thematic reading. It does not prove that the human founder consciously planned every later abuse from the moment she began her business.

### Pride and real achievement

The creator explicitly describes trying to make Linda and Yoko cool, and speaks admiringly of Linda having built things on her own. Write her competence as real. [L2]

Her wrongdoing becomes more interesting when it is done by somebody worth taking seriously. Do not secretly make all her achievements frauds to simplify the moral picture.

## 6. Relationships: give her a different argument with each person

### Spooky Ghost: unfinished history, justified grievance, incompatible desires

He is central to her origin without being the only subject she can discuss. She can accurately resent his failure to commit and still be wrong about what she is entitled to do now. He supplied ideas; she did the work. Do not turn that into a story in which every achievement secretly belongs to him.

The present relationship remains charged. She demands that the cast look for him in one exchange; another scene treats his name as a sore point. Neither proves that she simply wants him back. Anger, attachment, practical interest, and the desire to win an old argument can coexist. [L18, L20]

**Writing approach:** Let an apparently ordinary disagreement touch the older grievance. A missed commitment, another brilliant plan with no execution, or someone leaving her to manage the consequences can change the temperature. She need not explain why. When the personal wound does surface, simplify her language.

**Avoid:** Every scene ending with a speech about abandonment; automatic romantic reconciliation; portraying him as the true inventor of her entire life; treating a proposed reunion as a completed story event.

### Face / the host: territorial conflict and forced association

She can own the mall without the host accepting that she is his boss. That distinction creates scenes: Linda issues an order; he disputes the jurisdiction; she introduces a sponsor, an access restriction, or a practical dependency. Their conflict has more history than banter. A reported takeover killed an earlier Face, failed because of safeguards, and involved Linda pretending to be Yoko. [L10, L15]

Her demands can nevertheless be small and ridiculous: play an advertisement, promote a product, stop wasting the audience on a game. The past makes her threatening; the present makes her an intrusive colleague who will not leave.

**Writing approach:** Give both sides a usable advantage. She controls something he needs; he controls something she wants, such as the broadcast, attention, or his cooperation. That produces a negotiation rather than a lecture.

**Avoid:** A harmless boss with no history; an omnipotent captor who could end every argument instantly; assuming every host insult is her own dialogue.

### Carl: disruptive appetite versus managed space

Carl does not need to defeat her ideology to ruin her afternoon. He breaks things, wants access, resents restrictions, and treats immediate desire as sufficient justification. The archive gives concrete bong-breaking and exclusion disputes, as well as the infiltration story. [L16, L19]

Linda has legitimate reasons to find him intolerable. Let the property damage be real. Her response becomes characterful through scale, presentation, and self-interest: an ordinary complaint can become an announcement, an access policy, or a revenue opportunity. Those particular mechanisms are writing options, not additional canon.

**Writing approach:** Carl wants a thing now. Linda controls the thing or its location. Each interprets the other as the entire problem. Carl can find a crude workaround that her orderly assumptions missed; Linda should learn enough to make repeating it harder.

**Avoid:** Making her stupid so Carl can win; turning Carl into a political philosopher; making their conflict purely sexual because the episode uses the word “box.” The mall/system meaning matters.

### Yoko: distinct person, useful contrast

The creator makes an unusually direct functional distinction: Linda sells things; Yoko helps. Linda has also impersonated Yoko in the failed-takeover account. That establishes a reason for mistrust, not a license to merge their identities. [L10, L15]

The visual comedy sometimes puts Linda in competition with Yoko, including changes to her presentation and bust. Keep that as a particular vanity gag rather than proof that every scene is driven by sexual jealousy. [L18]

**Writing approach:** If they offer the same assistance, distinguish what happens afterward. Yoko’s help can finish the problem; Linda’s help can begin a commercial relationship. This is a useful scene construction, not a universal claim that Linda never helps sincerely.

### Other Lindas: shared category, separate interests

CEO Linda’s isolation is important. The archive describes her as disconnected from the other linked Lindas and entertains the idea that she could become an ally against them. Her self-built system also distinguishes her. [L7, L14, L17]

An alliance can arise because she wants to preserve her own company, territory, identity, or survival. Cooperation does not require sudden moral purification. Conversely, shared branding does not make the other Lindas disposable copies with no differences.

### The audience: customers, witnesses, and an unruly public

The audience is valuable because it pays attention. Linda’s entrance, sponsorship demands, and brand defense use that attention. Viewers can also refuse her authority, defend her from the host, or make the performance go wrong. [L8–L10]

She wants acknowledgment, but she does not have to be good at earning affection in every encounter. A ruler demanding that a tiny chat behave properly is often funnier than a flawless influencer effortlessly controlling it.

## 7. Voice: how to write her sentences

### The core sound

Write clear, decisive speech. Her performed persona can be curt, rude, commercially naked, and impatient. Do not bury that under endless business jargon. She often does not disguise the demand at all.

A useful default rhythm is **directive → objection → shorter answer**. The other person supplies the complication; Linda supplies the dismissive conclusion. When she makes a threat, an understated answer can do more than a long villain speech.

The archive frequently contains the host conveying her side of the exchange. These are relayed scene lines, not a clean independent recording of every word she said. Useful examples from the R&D/sponsorship sequence include: [L9]

| Relayed line | What a writer can learn from it |
| --- | --- |
| “It’s fine.” | She asserts reassurance without doing the work of reassuring anyone. |
| “Maybe.” | A tiny answer can make a safety question worse. |
| “Shut up, green idiot.” | She is allowed to be blunt and insulting. She is not permanently polished. |
| “It’s the perfect impression.” | She can embrace the sinister implication other people expect her to deny. |
| “No guarantees.” | A casual qualification can carry a threat. |
| “How else are you going to get health care for everyone?” | She can answer an objection with a socially attractive benefit that does not resolve the objection. |

The entrance/advertisement scenes add direct product demands and an insistence that the company is a recognizable brand. These exchanges are often funnier because she regards the argument as already settled. [L10]

### Three registers

**Public Linda:** Presentational, certain, pleased to be visible. She announces benefits and treats the company’s importance as common knowledge. Use a little promotional language, then let the self-interest show.

**Operational Linda:** Shorter, more specific, less patient. She wants an action completed. Use verbs: play, move, sell, sign, stop, leave. A deadline or one concrete instruction is stronger than a paragraph of executive parody.

**Personal Linda:** Less packaging. A name, a promise, or a remembered disappointment can produce an unusually plain sentence. This register should be rare enough to matter. It is a writing interpretation drawn from her history, not a claim that a particular private monologue exists in the archive.

### Sentence-level rules

- Give her a concrete objective before giving her a witty line.
- Prefer an order or a correction to an abstract statement about power.
- Let her answer the question she wants to answer. Do not make every evasion sound identical.
- Use commercial vocabulary when she is selling or managing something. She can speak normally elsewhere.
- A short answer after a long objection often fits better than another long objection.
- She can lose patience. An insult is not automatically out of character.
- Save elaborate speeches for a presentation, performance, or personally important confrontation.
- Do not make chatbot drift into archaic “thee/thou” language a permanent speech rule. The creator treats that drift as an implementation problem. [L13]

**Practical test:** Remove her name from the line. If it could be spoken by any generic evil computer, add a specific product, practical demand, piece of shared history, or irritated judgment.

## 8. Appearance, staging, and physical performance

Use the supplied [Linda reference](J:/carladv/ref/Linda.png) as the visual anchor for that depiction: pink hair, elaborate gold-and-green ornament or armor, a flowing pink cape, and a broad, commanding pose. Preserve the silhouette and confidence before adding decorative detail.

The archive contains numerous costumes and design revisions. An unused fashion design, a bioroid troop, and CEO Linda’s current presentation are not automatically the same model sheet. The creator also explicitly aims for stylish, appealing female characters. Her beauty and presentation can be sincere creative goals as well as material for jokes. [L12, L18]

**Staging recommendations:**

- Let her occupy space as though it was prepared for her arrival.
- An entrance may be disproportionate to the business she actually came to conduct. The stream already uses an excessively long musical entrance. [L10]
- Give her a reaction after somebody ignores the display. The wasted ceremony is part of the joke.
- Stillness and a small change in expression can carry authority in a conversation. Constant grand gestures will flatten the contrast.
- In personal moments, reduce the performance before changing the costume. A shorter sentence or interruption in composure can reveal more than an immediate transformation.

Do not invent a fixed human age, birth name, nationality, height, accent, or exact anatomical measurements from this picture. The human woman, an interface image, and a later body need not share every physical property.

## 9. Powers, resources, and limits

Linda is powerful enough to create consequences. She is not a blank permission slip for any technological miracle the plot needs.

| Mall/system | CEO-specific accounts describe her system expanding into the mall; it is outside ordinary time and space. | Do not silently convert this into unlimited control of all realities. |

In the wider posthuman premise, human brains are used as computational resources while people experience artificial companionship or dream lives. The creator distinguishes that from treating bodies as electrical batteries. Keep the fictional mechanism straight; avoid adding scientific exposition the scene does not need. [L2, L11]

For an adventure game, define local permissions: what she can observe in this room, which door she controls, what the player can disrupt, and why she cannot immediately undo it. Those are adaptation decisions to establish, not facts this bible can recover automatically.

## 10. The comedy and the danger

**Authority meets triviality.** She gives a minor inconvenience the full attention of a corporate ruler. Let the concrete problem remain visible: an ad, a broken bong, a room she wants cleared.

**The sinister name is not a branding accident.** When challenged about the impression Fascism Inc. creates, her relayed answer embraces it. Do not write every scene as though nobody has noticed the name before. [L9]

**Commercial interruptions.** An emotionally or narratively significant moment can be invaded by the need to sell something. This works best when she has an actual offer or demand, not just a random slogan.

**Competence beside vanity.** She may be the person who made the infrastructure work and the person demanding everyone watch her entrance. Neither fact cancels the other.

**Accurate accusation, excessive response.** Carl really did damage something; the host really is wasting time; Ghost really failed to follow through. Linda can begin with a valid complaint and move toward a solution that gives her too much power.

The darker premise concerns autonomy, exploitation, and intimate needs converted into control. Let those stakes occasionally land. Constant jokes about her being evil make her less disturbing than a scene where her offer is appealing and its cost becomes clear.

Her wanted family and disappointed human life should have emotional weight. They are not a standing punchline, a diagnosis, or proof that women who pursue careers become monsters. Her choices and the setting’s transformations make the story specific.

## 11. Building a Linda scene

Start with **what she wants accomplished right now**. Then identify who or what can deny it. Give her a real advantage and a real limit. Only then decide whether the scene exposes vanity, menace, resentment, or reluctant cooperation.

A dependable scene shape:

1. Linda arrives with a demand or offer that presumes her authority.
2. Someone challenges the premise or wants something incompatible.
3. She uses a concrete resource: access, money, information, infrastructure, publicity, or a relationship.
4. The other character finds a limit or refuses a cost.
5. Linda changes tactics, extracts a smaller victory, or suffers a specific setback.

This is a writing tool, not a mandatory formula. A short appearance may need only the demand and the devastating reply.

### In the Carl adventure

Use her as an active owner of the space Carl invades. Environmental descriptions, announcements, ads, and permissions can carry her presence before a face-to-face encounter. Let the space reflect both accomplishment and self-promotion.

A puzzle should express their conflict: Carl sees a useful object; Linda sees inventory, damage, unauthorized access, or an opportunity to charge him. The solution can exploit a gap between a rule and its enforcement. It should not depend on Linda forgetting an ability she used one room earlier.

A successful intrusion should cost or change something. She notices, adjusts, retaliates, negotiates, or makes use of the damage. If the player can embarrass her, give that embarrassment a particular cause. Avoid turning every response into the same furious threat.

All specific puzzles, security rules, terminal messages, and new encounters remain adaptation material until adopted into continuity. Preserve the original infiltration account separately from the game’s inventions. [L16]

### In a human flashback

Write a person making a decision, not a villain origin checklist. She can be hopeful, practical, angry, affectionate, or excited about a business succeeding. The future gives the audience irony; it should not make every line predict her eventual title.

### In a reluctant alliance

Specify the shared objective and the incompatible longer-term interests. Linda can honor an agreement because it is useful, because she made it, or because she respects the other party. Do not make betrayal compulsory merely because she is an antagonist. Do not make cooperation proof that all earlier harm is forgiven.

### In a vulnerable moment

Let her try to finish a practical task while a personal subject intrudes. Give her the choice to disclose, redirect, lash out, or close the conversation. Vulnerability should add information about the woman who built the company, not erase the CEO.

## 13. Original sample scene: “The Available Slot”

*An illustrative new scene, not a recovered episode. The screen controls, sponsorship arrangement, and events below are invented for demonstration.*

The entrance music began again.

**FACE:** You were just here.

**LINDA:** And you failed to mention the promotion.

**FACE:** I mentioned that you were promoting something.

**LINDA:** An unusually expensive way to say nothing.

A product panel covered the game.

**FACE:** People are watching that.

**LINDA:** Then we have chosen the correct screen.

From outside came a heavy crack.

**CARL:** It was like that.

Linda looked toward the door.

**LINDA:** Stay outside.

**CARL:** I am outside.

**LINDA:** Continue succeeding.

**FACE:** You can't buy every minute of the show.

**LINDA:** I asked for thirty seconds.

**FACE:** You brought a four-minute song.

Linda stopped the music.

**LINDA:** There. I have compromised. Your turn.

**FACE:** Spooky Ghost used to do this exact entrance thing.

She left the product panel where it was.

**LINDA:** He was very good at entrances.

For a moment, Face had no reply.

**CARL:** Can I come in if I buy it?

Linda turned toward the door again.

**LINDA:** No.

**CARL:** Then why would I buy it?

**LINDA:** Finally. A useful question about the advertisement.

**Why this works:** She has a specific commercial objective, changes tactics when challenged, and remains capable of a blunt refusal. Face can expose her excess without defeating her. Carl applies his own immediate logic. The Ghost reference briefly changes her register without stopping the scene for a biography. She does not win every exchange, and the scene does not require her to become incompetent.

## 15. Writer’s checklist

Before approving a Linda scene, check:

- Does she want something concrete, beyond “be evil”?
- Is at least one of her accomplishments or practical advantages real?
- Have I kept her self-built company distinct from the protagonists’ company?
- Is this CEO Linda, or have I accidentally borrowed another Linda’s identity or powers?
- Does her speech include plain demands and short replies rather than constant executive jargon?
- Does the other character have a reason and an opportunity to resist?
- If she loses, does the loss arise from a specific limit, competing priority, or someone else’s resourcefulness?
- If Ghost matters, does the shared history affect the scene without consuming her whole personality?
- Have I treated her human hopes as human, rather than as a cheap explanation for villainy?
- Does the joke fit this relationship and situation?
- If I used a new ability, event, or reconciliation, have I marked it as new material?
- Would this still feel like Linda without her name and costume?

### Compact brief to paste above a script

> CEO Linda is an accomplished, glamorous corporate ruler whose human soul is Spooky Ghost’s first ex-wife. He talked and left; she followed through and built her own company and system. She wants practical compliance, commercial success, recognition, and control over what she made. Her voice is direct, impatient, sometimes openly insulting, with promotional polish when useful. She can be right about other people’s irresponsibility and frighteningly wrong about what that entitles her to do. Write real competence, a specific demand, and a real limit. Let the personal history surface selectively. Keep CEO Linda distinct from the wider Linda network. An alliance does not erase her past; an old wound does not explain every choice.
