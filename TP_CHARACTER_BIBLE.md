# TP / THROWING IN THE TOWEL — CHARACTER & PERFORMANCE BIBLE

**Transcript-grounded working edition · 29 September 2026**

For writing and performing TP in streams, scripted interludes, and possible game adaptations. Companion to the [Carl bible](J:/carladv/CARL_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md), [CEO Linda bible](J:/carladv/CEO_LINDA_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md), and [Spooky Ghost bible](J:/carladv/SPOOKY_GHOST_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md).

**The central principle:** TP is the performance that happens when Cracky announces that he is giving up on performing the elaborate story—and remains a talking toilet-paper roll doing increasingly elaborate bits with the audience.

That is an interpretation of the recurring form, not an invented origin story. The archive directly supports both sides of the apparent contradiction: TP is outside the regular story and described as out of character; he is also explicitly acknowledged as a character-like performance that exaggerates the performer’s outrageous tendencies. [T1–T3]

**Write the contradiction. Do not solve it.**

## 1. What TP is

TP is a toilet-paper-roll presentation used for “Throwing in the Towel”: occasions when the normal character rotation and overarching plot are set aside. The performer hangs out, plays games, talks directly to viewers, experiments with bits, and lets the presentation become ridiculous.

He originated in the creator’s frustration with streaming on Kick: inconsistent favor or front-page attention, verification ambitions, and the feeling that the extra work was not producing the recognition he wanted. Those are the creator’s descriptions of his experience, not independent findings about the platform. The phrase “throwing in the towel” led to the toilet-paper presentation. [T1]

TP does not need a human childhood, a soul-transfer incident, a secret laboratory, or an explanation for how he became paper. In an explicit clarification, he does not exist to the other characters in the regular lore. His premise is withdrawing from that machinery. [T2]

But “out of character” does not mean a transparent recording of everyday behavior. The creator says he was being as ridiculous as possible, leaning into outrageous tendencies, and saying things he would normally hold back. He specifically says he is not like TP in real life. [T3]

**One-sentence writing brief:** A fed-up host declares a night off from the grand production, appears as a toilet-paper roll, and turns hanging out with chat into an intimate, unstable comedy of friendliness, insult, mock violence, repair, and renewed friendliness.

**The comedy:** He has stopped doing the big fiction and immediately creates another performance. He is visibly toilet paper and indignant that anyone would propose using him as toilet paper.

**The audience relationship:** The viewers are company, collaborators, hecklers, an apparent physical presence, and sometimes an audience being asked to perform for another imaginary audience.

**The emotional pressure:** Disappointment at effort going unrecognized becomes permission to play differently. He gives up on a particular burden without actually losing the impulse to entertain.

**The defining mistake to avoid:** Writing him as an ordinary lore character whose gimmick happens to be that he is made of toilet paper.

## 2. The evidence problem is part of the character

This bible draws on targeted searches and close reading of the supplied transcripts. It distinguishes direct creator explanation, performed speech, recollection, proposals, and interpretation. Transcripts alone cannot establish every visual detail or precisely time every effect.

TP is unusually easy to misread because some of his explanations contradict each other at the level of literal wording:

- “There is no character today.”
- The creator acknowledges that TP is nevertheless clearly a character in some sense.
- He describes TP as himself, off the cuff.
- He also says he deliberately exaggerates and does not behave exactly this way in everyday life.

The most useful reconciliation is limited: **out of the regular fiction; still a performance.** The creator’s explanations support that distinction. It does not require declaring every serious remark fake or every outrageous remark a private confession. [T2–T3]

### Reading rules

1. Prefer explicit explanations of TP’s function over attempts to infer secret lore.
2. Use recurring performed behavior to describe how he works.
3. Treat an individual insult, political remark, game answer, or chat quotation in context. It is not automatically a permanent personality rule.
4. Do not convert remarks about frustration into a diagnosis or a claim that the performer actually quit.
5. Mark new scripted material as new material.
6. Keep the audience’s contributions visible. Some of the best material begins with a viewer setting him up.

The transcripts frequently render TP as “TV,” “tifi,” or similar sounds, and toilet paper as “toilet favor.” Use context to identify those references. Do not turn every occurrence of TV into TP: actual television, watched clips, game dialogue, and other speakers also appear.

## 3. The layers of the performance

| Layer | What is happening | What to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The working streamer** | He has real games to start, people to greet, equipment to fix, and a stream to run. | The ordinary task remains present beneath the bit. |
| **The declared withdrawal** | He is “done” with the elaborate plot and its demands for the occasion. | The refusal is specific enough to mean something. It is not necessarily quitting streaming. |
| **The visible avatar** | He is a toilet-paper roll while saying this is just him. | The image contradicts any claim to completely unmediated normality. |
| **The amplified host** | He talks with fewer restraints and pursues ridiculous impulses. | It is a deliberate performance mode, not an exact copy of everyday behavior. |
| **The temporary scene with chat** | A comment becomes an offense; a camera effect becomes the audience’s condition; a microphone creates an interview. | The scene can become vivid without joining the larger canon. |
| **The performer exposing the mechanism** | He fixes an effect, misses a cue, asks for another take, or admits a line came out wrong. | Showing the machinery can extend the joke instead of ending it. |

These are simultaneous layers, not separate people sharing a body. There is no need for a metaphysical explanation of how TP switches between them.

The interesting movement is often very small: he says “we need to have a conversation,” brings the viewer closer, acts out a confrontation, then returns to the same unfinished game setup. The regular activity gives the detour its shape.

## 4. Origin, recurrence, and retirement

### The founding frustration

The creator directly connects TP to feeling jerked around by Kick’s apparent attention and approval. One retrospective describes being favored one week and not the next; another links the toilet-paper roll and baseball bat to being fed up with verification and metrics. [T1]

The writing lesson is not that TP must discuss Kick forever. It is that the character begins as a response to the feeling: **I keep doing all this, and what is the point?**

The response is still a show. That distinction makes the premise more interesting than straightforward resignation.

### A declared special, not necessarily an unplanned breakdown

One announcement builds a week of story material toward a Throwing in the Towel special, calling it a crescendo and reassuring viewers that the stream is not really going away. Another explanation explicitly says that even throwing in the towel was a bit. [T8]

Do not stage every TP appearance as an accidental emotional collapse. The creator can plan the occasion while improvising much of its content.

### The “low-effort” show still has choices

In a retrospective, the creator describes a largely improvised, less preplanned special, but also explains why the selected dungeon game fit the meta joke and notes that another game was lined up. The withdrawal from the normal format did not erase selection, timing, or craft. [T6]

“Not doing all that work today” is therefore a productive tension. A writer should not take it as an instruction to produce dull or careless material.

### From one-off release to recurring format

Viewers requested more. A proposed return becomes a birthday/party occasion in one passage. Other episodes combine TP with Balatro, Blood Bowl, Jackbox, dungeon games, or simply a game the performer was already enjoying. The variety matters more than any single mandatory title. [T4–T5, T7, T12–T13]

The format can carry community pleasure as well as frustration. He does not need a fresh platform grievance every time he appears.

### The vault, not a canonical death

Later commentary says TP may no longer have much reason to appear after the move away from Kick. Another response puts him “in the vault,” available if there is a good bit, rather than permanently retired. [T1, T9]

Mickey Mouse is later discussed as a different out-of-character presentation. The creator distinguishes that relatively ordinary hanging-out mode from TP’s deliberate extremity. This does not establish a transformation, death, or succession story inside the fiction. [T3, T10]

### The quieter TP is still TP

A bonus-game stream explicitly defines the roll as out of character plus a baseball-bat bit, yet later declines to take up viewers’ attempts to trigger the wipe routine because the game is absorbing his attention. [T7]

That is valuable evidence: **the signature bit is available, not compulsory.** A sustained stretch of actual gaming does not break the character.

## 5. Personality without an invented biography

These are descriptions of the performed mode, not claims about the performer’s private psychology.

### Fed up, but not indifferent

He says he is giving up while continuing to greet people, make choices, build jokes, and try to give viewers a good time. The performance depends on enough remaining investment for his claimed indifference to be interesting.

Do not write him as secretly hating everyone who watches. His warmth, gratitude, and interest in the room are visible in the same episodes as the insults and staged aggression. [T4–T5]

### Disinhibited and confrontational

The creator explicitly describes TP as a mode of saying more of what comes to mind. He can be profane, argumentative, provocative, and willing to take a joke into uncomfortable territory. [T3]

Do not sanitize him into a gently sarcastic office mascot. Equally, a catalogue of offensive subjects is not a character voice. The important thing is how he reacts to a specific comment and pursues it, not a requirement to introduce a taboo into every exchange.

### Ridiculously insistent on personal dignity

A toilet-paper roll refusing to be used for its apparent purpose is the signature boundary. He treats the predictable bathroom joke as a matter that requires serious discussion with everyone present. [T4–T5]

A useful interpretation is that the self-deprecating image does not grant the audience unlimited permission to reduce him to an object. That reading fits the behavior, but it is not an established philosophical manifesto.

### Abruptly solicitous

After the simulated confrontation, he worries about the audience’s vision, asks whether they are all right, and offers to hear complaints. The concern can be partly theatrical and partly ordinary host management at once.

Do not resolve whether each “sorry” is sincere, manipulative, or a cover for fixing the effect. The shifting implication is often the joke.

### Responsive rather than random

The format gives him freedom, but the strongest bits are caused by something: a bathroom comment, a confusing chat message, a missed input, a complaint, an unfinished game, or someone entering the stream.

He can go far from the original subject. The audience should still be able to remember how he got there.

### Capable of genuine ordinary conversation

He discusses games, thanks supporters, talks about streaming, and tells anecdotes. Those passages give the audience a place to stand between the extreme moments. TP should not be yelling continuously or visibly waiting for the next bat cue.

## 6. His most important relationship is with the viewer

The creator identifies the focus of an early TP performance as the VTuber talking directly to the viewer and creating an atmosphere of that contact. He names both the bits and the bong interaction as part of it. [T6]

That explanation is the center of this bible.

### Chat as company

He welcomes people, counts whether enough are present for party games, asks questions, responds to names, and hangs out. This is not filler to cut away before the “real” character begins. It creates the relationship the sharper bits play with.

### Chat as the instigator

Someone proposes using the toilet paper, asks for squares, or pushes the running joke. TP can pick that up, warn, escalate, or decline to engage. The audience helps choose the tempo.

### Chat as an apparent body

He asks viewers to look at him, keep their eyes open, count fingers, or explain why they are wobbling. The picture’s distortion is treated as something happening to **their** perception. [T4–T5]

This is more specific than general fourth-wall breaking. He makes the screen feel like a shared physical situation rather than merely reminding viewers that a screen exists.

### Chat as a guest with a microphone

He offers the audience a microphone and asks what they want to say “to the people at home.” The viewers become both the people watching and someone being interviewed in front of other people watching. [T4–T5]

Do not explain away this impossible seating arrangement. It is a useful temporary fiction that needs no architectural plan.

### Chat as a source of resistance

Viewers can keep making the same joke, give an irrelevant answer, notice a failed effect, or ask why the game still has not started. Let that resistance change the performance. If every response merely feeds a predetermined monologue, TP loses much of what makes him feel live.

### Chat as people he appreciates

Gratitude does not automatically end the bit. He can thank someone earnestly, resume an argument, and then start the game. The transitions are part of the texture.

## 7. The signature routine: offense, impact, concern, complaint desk

The creator calls this the “torture bit.” In the reviewed passages, its defining action is a staged hit toward the camera, supported by audiovisual effects and direct address. This is a performance routine; the sources do not require an offscreen torture chamber, named prisoner, or secret history. [T1, T3–T5]

A typical sequence, reconstructed from repeated exchanges:

1. **The setup:** TP is hanging out or doing something ordinary.
2. **The invitation or trigger:** A viewer makes the bathroom-use joke. Sometimes TP explicitly warns people not to make it, effectively putting the premise in reach.
3. **The earnest boundary:** “We need to establish something.” He insists that looking like toilet paper does not make him theirs to use.
4. **The staged escalation:** The bat/camera bit turns an absurd disagreement into apparent physical interference with the viewer.
5. **The reversal:** “Are you okay?” The person who caused the problem becomes the concerned host.
6. **The perception game:** He treats blur, wobble, color, or disorientation as the viewer’s condition, sometimes denies doing anything, and tries to make them see normally.
7. **The complaint microphone:** He offers a voice to the people he has just theatrically overpowered.
8. **The return:** The game, music, or mundane conversation resumes—often still waiting exactly where it was before.

Not every performance contains every beat, and the order can vary. The point is the change in relationship, not the number of effects.

### What makes it work

**The tiny offense receives a huge response.** He chose to be toilet paper; the audience makes the obvious joke; he acts as though an extraordinary boundary violation has occurred.

**The concern is inseparable from the disturbance.** He asks whether the viewers can see, while he is also controlling the thing that makes seeing difficult.

**The mechanism can fail in public.** He misses, says the effect did not work, resets, or tells everyone to pretend they did not see something. A passage even has him lose his own “don’t blink” challenge. These failures give chat leverage. [T4]

**The original activity remains embarrassingly visible.** In one exchange, the performer notices the game is still waiting for him to start it during the elaborate detour. That is the whole premise in miniature: the host who gave up on elaborate performance is delaying the game with another elaborate performance. [T4]

### How to vary it

Use a warning without a hit. Offer the microphone first. Let the audience’s complaint be painfully ordinary. Have the effect fail while TP tries to maintain authority. Let him choose the game over the bait, as he actually does in a bonus stream. [T7]

Do not repeat the entire sequence every time someone types a bathroom word. Repetition should let viewers anticipate a possibility; it should not turn the stream into an automatic response machine.

## 8. Voice and dialogue

### The core sound

TP sounds like someone speaking immediately to people in the room. His language is casual, profane, interrupted, repetitive, and full of practical adjustments: “guys,” “bro,” “hold on,” “listen,” “one second,” “go ahead,” “sorry.”

Those words are useful because of what they do. “Hold on” stops the current activity and claims attention. “Listen” makes a trivial subject official. “Are you okay?” changes aggression into caretaking. “Go ahead” appears to give the floor away while the host still controls the scene.

The voice should not become eloquent just because the concept is meta. The analysis can be sophisticated; TP’s line may simply be, “No. You don't get a square.”

### Four common transitions

**Relaxed host → serious boundary:** He has been chatting normally; now everyone must understand one important thing.

**Aggressor → worried helper:** The camera is disturbed; he asks what is wrong with the viewers.

**Confident bit → visible repair:** He notices the effect, timing, or words came out wrong and fixes it in front of them.

**Complaint → hospitality:** He wants to hear their concerns, thanks them, welcomes a newcomer, or asks what they want to play.

These transitions distinguish him more reliably than a list of opinions.

### Writing rules

- Begin with a specific comment or practical need.
- Prefer direct “you” address to abstract commentary about “the audience.”
- Let him repeat a phrase when trying to regain control, not merely to fill space.
- Keep apologies, repairs, and mistakes available. He is not a perfectly controlled menace.
- Allow a sincere sentence to remain sincere. Not every thank-you needs a sinister reveal.
- Use vulgarity as part of the delivery, not as the sole source of character.
- Preserve the difference between an improvised absurd claim and a lasting statement about the world.
- A scripted version should retain enough interruption to feel responsive while trimming endless transcript filler.
- Do not give him Ghost’s sustained tragic monologues, Carl’s specific alien history, or Linda’s corporate agenda by default.

## 9. Visual and physical performance

**Established anchors:** a toilet-paper roll; a baseball bat used in the camera-hit bit; a microphone offered to chat; smoking/bong business in some performances; movement and viewpoint changes that support proximity; effects discussed as blur, wobble, color changes, and damaged or restored vision. [T1, T4–T7]

No dedicated TP model sheet was identified among the supplied `ref` filenames. The transcripts are sufficient to describe his performance functions, but not to certify a complete facial design, exact dimensions, hand rig, bat model, or permanent costume. References to magazine-cutout faces during a party game should not automatically become his anatomy.

### What the image contributes

The object is mundane, disposable, and absurdly unsuited to commanding a room. It lets the performer announce an apparent retreat from production while still appearing through an unmistakable invented presentation.

The bat is a prop for the sudden reversal. The microphone is equally important: it turns force into an invitation to speak. If an adaptation keeps only the bat, it loses half of the relationship.

### Blocking recommendations

- Begin at a comfortable conversational distance so approaching the camera means something.
- Let the roll’s angle, proximity, and timing do work before adding effects.
- Direct a staged hit toward the viewer’s point of view, not vaguely into an unrelated room.
- Treat the aftermath as a conversation: look for a response, ask a question, make an adjustment.
- Keep a usable return to the normal view. The reset allows the next ordinary moment to register.
- Let a visible repair become a joke when it earns one; do not manufacture a malfunction every time.

### For an interactive adaptation

If the signature gag is implemented in a game or website, offer reduced motion and a skip/reset path, and avoid rapid flashing. These are implementation recommendations, not additional character lore. The joke can survive through sound, framing, dialogue, and a mild effect; prolonged visual discomfort is not what defines TP.

## 10. Cannabis, spontaneity, and the difference between a method and a character

Heavy cannabis use is part of the creator’s account of early TP performances. He discusses being unusually intoxicated, difficulty starting a game, and not wanting to repeat that degree of consumption. He also identifies concrete creative decisions he remembers making and the direct-address atmosphere he was trying to produce. [T3, T6]

Preserve that context without turning the bible into a consumption recipe. Being high is neither a full personality nor a requirement for reproducing the writing.

For a script, translate the observed effects into performable choices: an overextended aside, strange specificity, difficulty returning to a task, a burst of conviction, or an unexpected connection. These are techniques, not a claim that every moment was caused by intoxication.

The creator later contrasts the levels of the different character performances. Avoid flattening Carl and TP into the same “stoner voice.” TP’s distinctive situation is being outside the usual story and addressing the viewer; Carl has his own anxieties, wants, and fictional circumstances.

## 11. Relationship to the other characters

**Primary boundary:** TP is outside the regular character universe and does not ordinarily exist to its inhabitants. The creator says this directly when asked whether TP belongs alongside Face, Carl, Ghost, and the others. [T2]

| Character or layer | Useful contrast | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Carl** | Carl brings a specific anxious, impulsive fictional self to events; TP sets that continuity aside for host/viewer play. | A TP day without Carl does not mean TP killed Carl. A joking “yes and no” is followed by the explicit explanation that there is no normal plot that day. [T4] |
| **Spooky Ghost** | Ghost wants his significance recognized inside a personal story; TP can refuse the burden of doing that story at all. | A joke that Ghost would be depressed by TP’s success does not establish a meeting or shared cosmology. [T6] |
| **CEO Linda** | Linda conducts business and asserts authority in the fiction; TP negotiates the immediate host/viewer relationship. | A later joke that TP broke a Linda effect is not proof he attacked her system. [T6] |
| **Face** | Face has system history and a place in the continuity; TP does not need that explanation for the screen or audience. | “Out of the universe” does not mean TP secretly created or controls Face’s world. |
| **Mickey Mouse presentation** | Also discussed as out of character, but distinguished from TP’s amplified extremes. | Treat it as another presentation choice, not TP evolving into a different species. [T3, T10] |
| **The actual performer** | TP uses real conversational habits, frustrations, interests, and direct audience contact. | Performance exaggeration prevents a simple one-to-one equation with private behavior. [T3] |

The creator has also considered TP for an awards-host role because he is outside the fictional universe, and resisted adding him to a lore-oriented card concept for the same reason. These are useful examples of how the boundary affects format choices, not immutable laws against any future adaptation. [T11]

## 12. How to build a TP scene

Start with **an ordinary activity**, **a viewer contribution**, and **a possible detour**. Keep the ending open enough that the contribution can matter.

A small scene may need only:

1. TP announces that today is simple.
2. Chat introduces the obvious complication.
3. He invents a disproportionate procedure for dealing with it.
4. The procedure itself needs fixing.
5. They return to the activity, which has barely advanced.

The procedure can be an interview, a warning, a complaint desk, a demonstration, or something invented on the spot. It does not always need a bat.

### Write opportunities, not a transcript of imaginary spontaneity

For a live performance, prepare the premise, a few possible replies, the available props, and a way out. Do not require chat to supply one exact sentence. The reviewed material explicitly allows both baiting a premise and ignoring an offered trigger. [T4, T7]

For a fixed script, make the audience response specific enough to change the next beat. A faceless chorus that always says the convenient thing will feel less like the stream.

### Keep a low-intensity version

TP can simply play the game, respond honestly, and make a few odd choices. The extreme possibility provides tension even when it is unused. This makes returning to him sustainable without requiring every appearance to top the previous one.

### Do not write a compulsory redemption arc

He is a recurring mode more than a protagonist advancing through a single biography. An appearance can end because the game starts, the conversation changes, the host signs off, or everyone has had enough of the bit. It does not need a revelation explaining why he was paper all along.

A meaningful larger change might be that the original platform frustration no longer applies and the format is put away. That is supported by the creator’s discussion of TP becoming less necessary. [T1, T9]

### Bringing TP into the Carl adventure

The most faithful default would be an optional interlude that explicitly steps outside the adventure’s fiction: a bonus room presented as a break, an out-of-character commentary segment, or a separate menu extra. Those are proposals, not existing canon.

Let him address the player and the game presentation. Avoid quietly making him a mall resident, Linda’s experiment, Carl’s secret boss, or an omnipotent god who can rewrite the plot. Any such decision would be a deliberate new crossover premise and should be identified as one.

## 14. Original sample scene: “No Production Tonight”

*An illustrative new scene. Dialogue, exact effects, and events below are invented. It demonstrates the performance logic without adding TP to the adventure’s canon.*

The game menu sat behind the toilet-paper roll.

**TP:** We're keeping it simple tonight. No plot. No big production. I'm going to click this and we're going to play.

**CHAT:** Can I have two squares?

TP stopped beside the start button.

**TP:** We need to have a conversation.

**CHAT:** You said no plot.

**TP:** This isn't a plot. This is something you need to understand about visiting somebody.

He moved closer.

**TP:** You don't come into someone's stream and ask for pieces of them.

**CHAT:** One square?

A prop bat crossed the frame. The picture tilted slightly.

**TP:** Are you okay?

**CHAT:** You did that.

**TP:** I'm asking how you are. You can answer a question without starting another thing.

He adjusted the view. It tilted the other way.

**TP:** Hold on. No. That's worse.

The normal picture returned.

**TP:** There. And I do want you to feel heard.

He brought out a microphone.

**TP:** Go ahead. Tell the people at home what you need.

**CHAT:** I need you to start the game.

TP looked back at the menu.

**TP:** Yeah. Fair enough.

He put the microphone away.

**TP:** This is why we're not doing a production tonight. Takes forever.

He clicked Start.

**Why this works:** The “no production” promise is contradicted by the detour. The viewer’s comment gives the action a cause. TP becomes the source of disturbance, the concerned helper, and the apparent defender of audience participation. Chat supplies a practical correction that ends the bit. The ordinary game remains there throughout.

## 15. What not to accidentally make canon

| Temptation | Better treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Explain how a man physically became toilet paper. | The avatar and withdrawal premise are enough unless a new story explicitly chooses otherwise. |
| Treat “no character” as proof that everything is unperformed truth. | Keep the creator’s explicit account of deliberate amplification. |
| Treat the performance as proof of the creator’s everyday personality. | The creator directly distinguishes TP from how he behaves in real life. |
| Treat the bat scene as evidence of actual violence or a permanent prisoner. | It is a camera-directed performance routine in the reviewed material. |
| Give him universal power because he is outside the lore. | Outside the lore describes his relationship to the format, not a power level. |
| Make every other character remember him. | Default to the explicit boundary; crossovers need a new decision. |
| Require intoxication or escalating offensiveness to make him recognizable. | Preserve the relationship, rhythm, and improvisational permission instead. |
| Make “throwing in the towel” mean the stream really ended. | The creator explicitly identifies the special as a bit and reassures viewers. |
| Fix his retirement as final. | The archive leaves room for a good reason to bring him back. |
| Make the bathroom trigger automatic. | He demonstrably sometimes lets it pass. |
| Convert jokes about broken software into lore feats. | Distinguish commentary on the tools from events inside the fictional universe. |
| Assume an uncertain transcript word names a recurring character or prop. | Leave it unresolved until the source is clear; phonetic guesses are not canon. |

## 16. Writer’s checklist

- Is TP outside the normal continuity unless this is an explicitly chosen crossover?
- What ordinary activity gives the scene its baseline?
- What viewer contribution actually changes the scene?
- Does the denial of performance coexist with visible performance?
- Is he addressing the viewer rather than merely talking about viewers?
- Does any camera effect have a conversational purpose?
- Have I retained warmth, gratitude, or ordinary interaction alongside confrontation?
- Is a failure or interruption allowed to change his approach?
- Is the signature bit optional rather than automatic?
- Am I borrowing specific opinions or personal history without enough context?
- Can the scene return to the game or conversation without a lore explanation?
- Does this feel like TP, rather than Carl with a different model or Ghost without a sheet?

### Compact brief to paste above a script

> TP is the toilet-paper-roll performance used for Throwing in the Towel: Cracky sets aside the regular character plot, hangs out with chat, and deliberately lets the presentation become outrageous. He is outside the story universe but is still performing; do not equate him mechanically with everyday private behavior. His main relationship is with the viewer. He can turn a bathroom joke into a grave boundary dispute, stage a hit toward the camera, treat the resulting distortion as the viewer feeling woozy, “help” restore their vision, and offer a microphone for complaints. Keep the casual voice, visible repairs, genuine warmth, profanity, and responsive improvisation. He says there is no production while making another one. Give him no unnecessary biography, no automatic god powers, and no obligation to perform the bat bit every time.
